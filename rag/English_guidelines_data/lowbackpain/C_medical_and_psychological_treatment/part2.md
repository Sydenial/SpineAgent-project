# Low Back Pain: Treatment and Management

## Med/Psych Question 3

In patients with low back pain, is topical treatment (e.g., lidocaine patch) effective in decreasing duration of pain, decreasing intensity of pain, increasing functional outcomes of treatment, and improving the return-to-work rate?

There is insufficient evidence to make a recommendation for or against the use of lidocaine patch for the treatment of low back pain.

Grade of Recommendation: I

Gimbel et al. assessed the effectiveness and safety of a 5% lidocaine patch for the treatment of low back pain in an open-label pilot study. Patients with low back pain from five different centers in the United States were categorized into groups based on the duration of low back pain: acute/subacute (3 months), $n=21$; short-term chronic (3-12 months), $n=33$; or long-term chronic (12 months), $n=77$. Participants applied the lidocaine patch up to four times daily for six weeks. The results indicated improvements in pain intensity and quality of life (QoL) at 2 and 6 weeks. Adverse events included skin reactions, lightheadedness, and headache. The study concluded that the 5% lidocaine patch significantly improved pain intensity and QoL, and further randomized controlled trials are warranted. This study provides Level III evidence.

Topical capsicum is recommended as an effective treatment for low back pain on a short-term basis (3 months or less).

Grade of Recommendation: A

Frerick et al. conducted a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled multicenter study to assess the efficacy and tolerance of capsicum plaster for the treatment of low back pain. A total of 320 patients were randomized to receive either a 12x18 cm capsicum plaster ($n=160$) or a placebo plaster ($n=160$). The treatment group experienced a significantly greater reduction in pain (42%) compared to the placebo group (31%). The responder rate (≥30% pain reduction) was greater in the treatment group (67%) compared to the placebo group (49%). Adverse events likely related to capsicum included excessive heat or erythema. The study provides Level I evidence that capsicum plaster may offer an alternative treatment for low back pain.

Keitel et al. studied the efficacy of capsicum plaster in a double-blind randomized controlled study with 154 participants. The treatment group experienced a significantly greater reduction in pain and improved pain scores compared to the placebo group. Adverse reactions included local warmth and itching. The study provides Level I evidence that capsicum plaster is effective for chronic nonspecific low back pain.

## Future Directions for Research

There are no specific recommendations for future research on topical treatments for low back pain.

## Med/Psych Question 4

Following treatment for low back pain, do patients with healthy sleep habits experience decreased duration of pain, decreased intensity of pain, increased functional outcomes, and improved return-to-work rates compared to patients with poor sleeping habits?

A systematic review of the literature yielded no studies to adequately address this question.

## Future Directions for Research

Methods or tracking tools to evaluate low back treatment outcomes in patients with and without healthy sleep habits.

## Med/Psych Question 5

In patients with low back pain, is cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and/or psychosocial intervention and/or neuroscience education effective in decreasing duration of pain, decreasing intensity of pain, increasing functional outcomes, decreasing anxiety and/or depression, and improving return-to-work rates?

Bendix et al. compared rehabilitation outcomes from three different therapy approaches in a randomized study. A total of 132 patients were randomized into one of three groups: Group 1 ($n=46$) received intensive multidisciplinary rehabilitation; Group 2 ($n=43$) received active physical training; and Group 3 ($n=43$) received a combined psychological and physical therapy program. Group 1 showed significantly improved work-readiness, health care contacts, pain, disability, and physical activity. The study provides Level I evidence that multidisciplinary rehabilitation is economically worthwhile despite being more expensive than less-intensive programs.

Dufour et al. studied the efficacy of a biopsychosocial rehabilitation program versus an individual intensive exercise program. Both programs showed significant improvements in pain and disability over the long term, with no major differences between the two treatments. This study provides Level I evidence that both programs are effective for chronic low back pain.

Lamb et al. conducted a multicenter trial comparing cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) with active management for low back pain. The CBT group showed significantly greater improvements in pain, disability, and function at 3, 6, and 12 months compared to the active management group. This study provides Level I evidence that CBT is more effective than active management alone.

Linden et al. assessed the efficacy of CBT compared to an inpatient orthopedic rehabilitation program in patients with chronic low back pain. The intervention group, which received CBT, showed greater improvements in pain and fear of movement compared to the control group. This study provides Level I evidence that CBT is effective in treating chronic low back pain.

Sullivan et al. investigated the addition of psychosocial treatment to physiotherapy. The treatment group showed improved outcomes, including reduced pain catastrophizing and depression. This study provides Level II evidence that psychosocial treatment can enhance the outcomes of physiotherapy.

Turner et al. compared cognitive therapy with behavioral treatment for chronic low back pain. All treatment groups showed improvements in pain intensity, but there were no significant differences between cognitive therapy and relaxation training. This study provides Level I evidence that CBT, relaxation training, or their combination are all effective for reducing low back pain.

## Future Directions for Research

Future research should explore the optimal combination and frequency of psychosocial interventions (e.g., CBT, neuroscience education) for the treatment of low back pain.
