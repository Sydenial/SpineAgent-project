# What is the role of pharmacological treatment in the management of spinal stenosis?

There is insufficient evidence to make a recommendation for or against the use of pharmacological treatment in the management of spinal stenosis.

Grade of Recommendation: I (Insufficient Evidence)

## Intramuscular Calcitonin

Eskola et al. conducted a double-masked, randomized, controlled, crossover trial of 39 patients with neurogenic claudication from lumbar spinal stenosis. Each patient was treated with intramuscular calcitonin for a portion of the study period, allowing each patient to serve as their own control. Clinical inclusion criteria included bilateral leg pain and maximum walking tolerance of 1500 m. Radiographic inclusion criterion was less than 10 mm spinal canal diameter on myelography. Outcome measures were walking distance, pain (Visual Analog Scale), and a performance test of lower extremity power and swiftness.

At the three- to six-month follow-up, walking distance and pain were improved during calcitonin treatment. After crossover, pain relief was more significant than walking distance improvement. Patients with mild pain or severe neurogenic claudication showed no improvement. The authors critiqued the study's radiographic inclusion criteria as somewhat contradictory, as only 19 out of 39 patients had central stenosis. The two groups were not matched for severity of initial symptoms, and the baseline characteristics were not statistically compared. The results were not stratified between patients with central or lateral recess stenosis. These data provide Level II therapeutic evidence for the effects of calcitonin in the treatment of lumbar spinal stenosis.

Sahin et al. described a prospective comparative study assessing the short-term effects of physical therapy (PT) alone and in combination with calcitonin on pain, physical examination results, and the functional status of patients with neurogenic claudication and lumbar spinal stenosis. Of the 45 consecutive patients, 22 received physical therapy and 23 received both physical therapy and calcitonin. At eight-week follow-up, both groups showed significant improvement, with no statistically significant differences noted between the two groups in pain scores, range of motion, functional status (assessed by the Roland Morris Disability Questionnaire), and walking distance. The authors concluded that PT improved short-term clinical outcomes in patients with lumbar spinal stenosis, but adding calcitonin was not necessary. Due to limitations like small sample size, lack of validated outcome measures, and improper randomization, the study provides Level III therapeutic evidence that PT alone is effective in the short-term treatment of lumbar spinal stenosis.

Tafazal et al. reported a randomized controlled trial testing the efficacy of nasal salmon calcitonin in the treatment of lumbar spinal stenosis. After a six-week washout period, both groups received calcitonin. No significant differences were noted between the groups at sixteen weeks, except for low back pain scores, which showed more improvement in the calcitonin group. The authors concluded that nasal salmon calcitonin does not seem to have a role in the conservative treatment of lumbar spinal stenosis.

Eskola et al. conducted an "open follow-up study" to test the efficacy of intramuscular calcitonin for lumbar spinal stenosis. The study followed 15 patients for six months, showing statistically significant improvements in symptom intensity and walking distance, but with no validated outcomes and unclear methodology. This study provides Level IV therapeutic evidence for the effectiveness of intramuscular calcitonin in treating neurogenic claudication associated with lumbar spinal stenosis.

## Intranasal Salmon Calcitonin

Podichetty et al. reported a randomized, double-masked, controlled trial studying the effectiveness of intranasal salmon calcitonin for lumbar spinal stenosis. Fifty-five patients were randomized, with 36 in the treatment group and 19 in the control group. After six weeks, the placebo group received calcitonin. Outcome measures included the Modified Oswestry Low Back Pain questionnaire, walking time and distance, the Lumbar Canal Stenosis (LCS) specific questionnaire, SF-36, and Visual Analog Scale for pain. At final follow-up, no significant differences were found between the treatment and control groups in VAS pain, SF-36, or walking time and distance. This study provides Level II evidence that intranasal salmon calcitonin is not effective for the treatment of lumbar spinal stenosis.

## Methylcobalamin

Waikakul and Waikakul performed a randomized controlled trial to evaluate methylcobalamin as an adjunct to medical/interventional treatment in 152 patients with lumbar spinal stenosis. The study showed no significant differences between groups, and the data provide Level II evidence that methylcobalamin is not effective for the treatment of lumbar spinal stenosis.

## Intravenous Lipoprostaglandin E(1)

Iwamoto et al. evaluated the effects of intravenous lipoprostaglandin E(1) on 20 elderly men with neurogenic claudication from lumbar spinal stenosis. The study showed a small improvement in the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score, but the follow-up period was short and the population was small, leading to Level IV therapeutic evidence for intravenous lipoprostaglandin E(1).

Murakami et al. reported a series of 37 patients treated with intravenous lipoprostaglandin E(1). At short-term follow-up, statistically significant improvements were observed in JOA scores, but only 10 out of 31 patients with sustained improvement at long-term follow-up. This provides Level IV therapeutic evidence for the short-term benefit of intravenous lipoprostaglandin E(1) in patients with lumbar spinal stenosis.

## Prostaglandin E(2)

Matsudaira et al. reported a randomized controlled trial comparing Limaprost and Etodolac for lumbar spinal stenosis treatment. Limaprost was significantly more effective across multiple outcome measures, providing Level II therapeutic evidence that Limaprost results in better short-term outcomes than Etodolac in patients with lumbar spinal stenosis.

## Gabapentin

Yaksi et al. described a randomized controlled trial examining whether gabapentin provides effective analgesia in patients with lumbar spinal stenosis. The study showed improved walking distance and significantly lower VAS scores for the gabapentin group, providing Level II therapeutic evidence for the use of gabapentin in the treatment of symptomatic lumbar spinal stenosis as part of a comprehensive treatment plan including physical therapy.

## Future Directions for Research

Future research should focus on large, double-masked, randomized controlled trials with long-term observation periods to further examine the efficacy of intramuscular calcitonin for lumbar stenosis.

## Recommendation

A large, double-masked, randomized controlled trial with a long-term observation period is recommended to examine the potential benefits of intramuscular calcitonin for the treatment of lumbar stenosis.

# What is the role of physical therapy/exercise in the treatment of spinal stenosis?

There is insufficient evidence to make a recommendation for or against the use of physical therapy or exercise as stand-alone treatments for degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis.

Grade of Recommendation: I (Insufficient Evidence)

## Future Directions for Research

Future research should focus on randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up and validated outcome measures to assess the efficacy of physical therapy in the treatment of lumbar spinal stenosis. The physical therapy program should be standardized and include exercise and education at a minimum.
