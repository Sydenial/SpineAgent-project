# Intraoperative Redosing Recommendations for Antibiotic Prophylaxis in Open Spine Surgery

## Consensus Statement

Intraoperative redosing within 3–4 hours may be considered to maintain therapeutic antibiotic levels throughout the procedure. The superiority of one drug has not been demonstrated in the literature. When determining the appropriate drug choice, the patient’s risk factors, allergies, length and complexity of the procedure, and issues of antibiotic resistance should be considered.

## Evidence Summary

There is no study directly comparing redosing to not redosing. However, several studies used redosing in their cohorts, and their findings are consistent with the consensus statement.

### Hellbusch et al.

Hellbusch et al. conducted a prospective, randomized controlled trial examining the effects of multiple dosing regimens on the postoperative infection rate in instrumented lumbar spinal fusion. Two hundred sixty-nine patients were randomized into either a preoperative-only protocol or a preoperative with extended postoperative antibiotic protocol. Patients in the preoperative-only protocol group received a single dose of intravenous cefazolin 1 g or 2 g based on weight, administered 30 minutes before incision. The extended postoperative antibiotic protocol group received the same preoperative dose plus postoperative intravenous cefazolin every eight hours for three days, followed by oral cephalexin every six hours for seven days.

Because of untoward drug reactions or deviation from the antibiotic protocol, 36 of the 269 patients were eliminated from the study. Therefore, 233 patients completed the entire study; 117 received preoperative antibiotics only, and 116 received both pre- and postoperative antibiotics. At 21 days of follow-up, there was no significant difference in infection rates between the two antibiotic protocols. The postoperative infection rates were 4.3% for the preoperative-only protocol and 1.7% for the preoperative with extended antibiotic protocol. The overall postoperative infection rate was 3%.

The study identified five variables that appeared to demonstrate a trend toward increased infection rate: blood transfusion, electrophysiological monitoring, increased height, increased weight, and increased body mass index. Increased tobacco use trended toward a lower infection rate. Although a comparative study by design, this study provides Level IV therapeutic evidence that patients with prolonged procedures that are redosed have a similar infection rate to simpler procedures without a redosing regimen.

### Kakimaru et al.

Kakimaru et al. reported results from a retrospective comparative study comparing infection rates following spinal surgery with and without postoperative antimicrobial prophylaxis. Of the 284 patients included in the study, 141 received preoperative and postoperative dosing, and 143 received preoperative and intraoperative dosing.

The antibiotics used in the postoperative group included cefazolin 1 g in 108 patients, flomoxef 1 g in 26 patients, and piperacillin 1 g in seven patients. For the preoperative plus intraoperative dosing group, cefazolin 1 g was given to 142 patients, and minocycline 100 mg was given to one patient. Patients in the postoperative dosing group received an intravenous dose within 30 minutes of skin incision, a postoperative intravenous dose, and oral antibiotics for an average of 2.7 days, or a preoperative dose with intraoperative redosing at three-hour intervals and a single postoperative dose. Preoperative plus intraoperative dosing patients received a preoperative dose within 30 minutes of skin incision with intraoperative dosing at three-hour intervals until skin closure.

Infection was confirmed via bacterial cultures and inspection of the wound for redness, heat, swelling, and pain. In the postoperative dosing group, 2.8% (4/141) developed infections (three superficial and one deep); in the preoperative plus intraoperative dosing group, 1.4% (2/143) developed infections (p = 0.335). The authors concluded that the duration of antimicrobial prophylaxis does not influence the rate of surgical site infections and that postoperative administration of antimicrobials appears unnecessary. Although a comparative study by design, this study provides Level IV therapeutic evidence that intraoperative redosing resulted in similar infection rates as single dosing in shorter cases.

### Kanayama et al.

Kanayama et al. performed a retrospective case-control study to compare the rate of surgical site infections in lumbar spine surgeries using two different antibiotic prophylaxis protocols. A first-generation cephalosporin was administered unless the patient had a history of a significant allergy, such as anaphylactic shock, systemic skin eruption, or toxic liver dysfunction.

Postoperative-dose group patients received antibiotics for five to seven days after surgery. No postoperative-dose group patients received antibiotics only on the day of surgery; antibiotics were given 30 minutes before skin incision, with an additional dose administered every three hours to maintain therapeutic levels throughout surgery.

Infections were defined at a maximum of six months by positive wound culture and/or typical infectious signs, including purulent exudate, surrounding erythema, and wound fluctuance. Laboratory studies, such as prolonged elevation in the C-reactive protein value, were also referenced. There were 1,133 patients in the postoperative-dose group and 464 patients in the no postoperative-dose group. The rate of instrumentation surgery was not statistically different between the postoperative-dose group (43%) and the no postoperative-dose group (39%).

The overall rate of surgical site infection was 0.7%. The infection rate was 0.8% (9/1,133) in the postoperative-dose group and 0.4% (2/464) in the no postoperative-dose group; the difference was not significant. Regarding the organisms responsible for surgical site infection, resistant strains of bacteria were cultured in five (83.3%) of six patients in the postoperative-dose group, whereas none were cultured in the no postoperative-dose group. The authors concluded that no statistical difference was observed between protocols. This study provides Level IV therapeutic evidence that intraoperative redosing resulted in similar infection rates as single dosing in shorter cases.

### Mastronardi et al.

Mastronardi et al. presented a retrospective case series evaluating the safety and efficacy of a specific intraoperative antibiotic protocol for a variety of spinal surgeries. Over a three-year period, 973 patients received ampicillin/sulbactam 1.5 g intravenously on induction, or teicoplanin 400 mg intravenously on induction if surgery lasted longer than two hours, with redosing of teicoplanin at four hours or after 1,500 mL of blood loss.

Data were gathered at six weeks to one year regarding wound drainage, wound abscess, or positive culture. Wound infection occurred in nine cases (1%), and discitis occurred in four of 657 patients (0.06%). This study provides Level IV therapeutic evidence that intraoperative redosing resulted in similar infection rates as single dosing in shorter cases.

### Riley et al.

Riley et al. described a retrospective study of one year of patients (40) who underwent either simple discectomy or instrumented procedures. Patients received 1.5 g cefuroxime preoperatively and every four hours for a 48-hour duration. Intravenous gentamicin (80 mg) was administered preoperatively, with redosing every six hours intraoperatively and every eight hours postoperatively for a 48-hour duration. No infections occurred in the 40 patients.

The study provided a discussion of the basic science behind the use of cefuroxime and gentamicin as readily disc-eluting antibiotics compared with cefazolin as a less disc-eluting antibiotic. However, this retrospective chart review evaluated an extremely small cohort of patients, and no conclusions regarding the efficacy of a specific regimen can be drawn. This work represents an extension of basic science research examining the penetration of cefazolin, gentamicin, and cefuroxime into disc tissue. It provides Level IV therapeutic evidence that redosing resulted in similar infection rates as single dosing in shorter cases.

### Takahashi et al.

Takahashi et al. performed a retrospective comparative study evaluating the effectiveness of preoperative cephalosporin with various postoperative dosing schedules in reducing infection rates following a variety of spinal surgeries, including decompression with or without fusion and with or without fixation.

Group 1 received first- or second-generation cephalosporin or penicillin administered by intravenous drip infusion for seven days (4 g/day) after the operation, followed by one week of oral cephalosporin. Group 2 received first- or second-generation cephalosporin administered intravenously, with the initial dose given at anesthesia induction and an additional intraoperative dose if operative time exceeded five hours; administration continued for five days (2 g/day), followed by one week of oral therapy. Group 3 received first- or second-generation cephalosporin with an initial dose at anesthesia induction and additional doses every three hours intraoperatively, followed by three days of postoperative intravenous therapy (2 g/day) and one week of oral therapy. Group 4 received first-generation cephalosporin with an initial dose at anesthesia induction and additional intraoperative doses every three hours, followed by two days of postoperative intravenous therapy (2 g/day).

Of the 1,415 patients included, 539 were in Group 1, 536 in Group 2, 257 in Group 3, and 83 in Group 4. Using CDC guideline criteria, superficial infections involved only skin and/or subcutaneous tissues, while deep infections involved deeper soft tissues such as fascia and muscle. The overall frequency of surgical site infections was 2.6% (14/539) in Group 1, 0.9% (5/536) in Group 2, and 0% in Groups 3 and 4. Comparison using Tukey’s multiple comparison test showed p < 0.05 when comparing infection rates among Groups 1, 2, and 3.

The authors concluded that with thorough prophylactic countermeasures, the frequency of surgical site infections can be decreased, and the duration of antimicrobial prophylaxis can be reduced from seven days to two days. This study provides Level IV therapeutic evidence that intraoperative redosing resulted in similar infection rates as single dosing in shorter cases.

## Future Directions for Research

## Recommendation #1

A case-controlled study is suggested utilizing available national databases to determine the relative efficacy of redosing antibiotic prophylaxis in specific patient populations undergoing spine surgery.

## Recommendation #2

A series of randomized controlled studies evaluating dosing regimens is recommended. Each study could address a specific subpopulation defined by diagnosis, procedure, and comorbidity.
