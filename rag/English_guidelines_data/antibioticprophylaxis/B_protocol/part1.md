## B. Protocol

# For patients receiving antibiotic prophylaxis prior to open spine surgery, what are the recommended drugs, their dosages, administration routes and timing resulting in decreased postoperative infection rates?

Preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis is suggested to decrease infection rates in patients undergoing spine surgery. In typical, uncomplicated spinal procedures, the superiority of one agent, dose, or route of administration over any other has not been clearly demonstrated. When determining the appropriate drug choice, the patient's risk factors, allergies, length and complexity of the procedure, and issues of antibiotic resistance should be considered.

**Grade of Recommendation: B**

Petignat et al. conducted a prospective, randomized controlled trial assessing the efficacy of one preoperative 1.5 g dose of cefuroxime in preventing surgical site infection after lumbar laminotomy and discectomy for herniated disc. Of the 1237 patients included in the study, 613 received 1.5 g intravenous cefuroxime on induction, and 624 received placebo. Infection presence, as defined by CDC guidelines, was assessed at six weeks, three months, and six months. The baseline characteristics were similar in both groups. Eight (1.3%) patients in the cefuroxime group and 18 patients (2.8%) in the placebo group developed a surgical site infection. There was a significant reduction in spondylodiscitis in the cefuroxime group. This study provides Level I therapeutic evidence that for uncomplicated lumbar microdiscectomy, a single preoperative dose of cefuroxime significantly reduces the incidence of spondylodiscitis.

Barker et al. performed a retrospective meta-analysis of 451 prophylaxed patients compared with 392 controls to examine the efficacy of antibiotic prophylaxis in spinal surgery. This meta-analysis showed a significant reduction in infection rates with prophylactic antibiotics, though the superiority of one regimen was not demonstrated. This study provides Level II therapeutic evidence that prophylactic antibiotic therapy with gram-positive coverage is effective in reducing the risk of infection after spinal surgery.

Pons et al. described a prospective, randomized trial comparing perioperative antibiotic protocols using either 2g ceftizoxime or 1g vancomycin plus 80 mg gentamicin. Infections were confirmed using bacterial cultures for deep infections. The infection rates were similar in both groups (ceftizoxime: 2.8%, vancomycin/gentamicin: 2.7%), and there was no statistical relationship between postoperative infection and any clinical variable. This study provides Level II evidence that both ceftizoxime and vancomycin/gentamicin are equally effective in preventing infections.

Rubinstein et al. performed a prospective, randomized controlled trial to investigate the efficacy of a single dose of 1 g of cephazolin in reducing postoperative infections. The results showed a significant reduction in infections with cephazolin compared to placebo, though it was not statistically significant for wound infections. This study provides Level II therapeutic evidence that a single preoperative dose of cephazolin is effective in reducing infection rates in lumbar spinal surgery.

Dobzyniak et al. described a retrospective comparative study examining the efficacy of single versus multiple dosing for lumbar disc surgery. The infection rate was found to be similar between single and multiple dosing regimens. This study provides Level III evidence that a single preoperative dose of antibiotics is as effective as postoperative antibiotics in preventing wound infections in lumbar disc surgery.

Hellbusch et al. conducted a randomized controlled trial to examine the effects of multiple dosing regimens on the postoperative infection rate in lumbar spinal fusion. No significant difference in infection rates was found between the preoperative-only protocol and the extended postoperative antibiotic protocol. This study provides Level III therapeutic evidence that preoperative prophylactic antibiotics are effective in reducing postoperative infection rates.

Kakimaru et al. performed a retrospective comparative study comparing infection rates following spinal surgery with and without postoperative antimicrobial prophylaxis. The results showed no significant difference in infection rates between the two groups. This study provides Level III evidence that postoperative antimicrobial prophylaxis does not influence the rate of surgical site infections.

Kanayama et al. compared infection rates in lumbar spine surgeries for two different antibiotic prophylaxis protocols. The study showed that the overall infection rate was low in both groups, and no significant difference was observed. This study provides Level III evidence that preoperative dosing with intraoperative redosing is effective in preventing surgical site infection.

Luer et al. performed a retrospective case control study comparing postoperative infections after laminectomy/discectomy. The study showed that the timing of prophylactic antibiotics was crucial, with dosing within two hours prior to incision yielding a lower infection rate. This study provides Level II therapeutic evidence that antibiotic prophylaxis within two hours of surgery improves infection outcomes.

Mastronardi et al. conducted a retrospective comparative study evaluating the efficacy of two intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis protocols in lumbar microdiscectomies. The results showed no significant difference in postoperative spondylodiscitis between cefazolin and ampicillin/sulbactam. This study provides Level III evidence that both cefazolin and ampicillin/sulbactam are effective in preventing discitis following lumbar discectomy.

Rohde et al. performed a retrospective comparative study on postoperative spondylodiscitis incidence in cases with and without antibiotic prophylaxis. The study showed a significant reduction in postoperative spondylodiscitis with the use of prophylactic antibiotics. This study provides Level III evidence that prophylactic antibiotics, specifically gentamicin-containing collagen sponges, are effective in preventing spondylodiscitis.

Sweet et al. performed a comparative study to evaluate the safety and efficacy of adjunctive local application of vancomycin for infection prophylaxis in posterior instrumented thoracic and lumbar spine surgeries. The study showed a significant reduction in wound infections with local vancomycin application. This study provides Level III evidence that adjunctive local application of vancomycin decreases the postoperative infection rate compared to intravenous cephalexin.

Takahashi et al. compared the effectiveness of various postoperative dosing schedules in reducing infection rates following spinal surgeries. The study concluded that a shorter duration of antimicrobial prophylaxis (two days) was more effective in reducing infections compared to longer durations. This study provides Level III evidence that shorter durations of antimicrobial prophylaxis are more effective in preventing infections.

## Future Directions for Research

Large multicenter randomized controlled trials assessing the efficacy of various protocols tailored to specific patient populations (e.g., obesity, diabetes, trauma, neuromuscular injury or disease, prolonged multilevel instrumented surgery) at increased risk for surgical site infections should be conducted.

## Consensus Statement

In patients with comorbidities or undergoing complicated spine surgery, alternative prophylactic regimens, including redosing, gram-negative coverage, or the addition of intrawound application of vancomycin or gentamicin, are suggested to decrease the incidence of surgical site infections when compared to standard prophylaxis regimens. Comorbidities and risk factors reviewed in the literature include obesity, diabetes, neurologic deficits, incontinence, preoperative serum glucose levels >125 mg/dL, or postoperative serum glucose levels >200 mg/dL, trauma, and prolonged multilevel instrumented surgery.

Olsen et al. performed a retrospective case-control study to identify the independent risk factors for surgical site infections in laminectomy or spinal fusion patients. The study identified risk factors including obesity, diabetes, and postoperative incontinence. The infection rate was 2.76%, with a higher prevalence of gram-negative rods and anaerobes in lumbar or lumbosacral procedures. This study provides Level III evidence that these factors increase the risk of infection in spinal surgery patients.

Olsen et al. conducted another retrospective study focusing on the risk factors for spinal surgical site infections. The study identified diabetes, suboptimal timing of prophylactic antibiotics, and elevated preoperative or postoperative serum glucose levels as independent risk factors. This study provides Level III evidence that these factors, along with obesity, significantly increase the risk of infection following orthopedic spinal operations.
