# For Patients Undergoing Open Spine Surgery with Spinal Implants, Does Antibiotic Prophylaxis Result in Decreased Infection Rates as Compared to Patients Who Do Not Receive Prophylaxis?

Prophylactic antibiotics may be considered to decrease the rate of infections following instrumented spine fusion.

Grade of Recommendation: C

## Study 1: Rechtine et al. (2001)

Rechtine et al. described a retrospective case series study of 235 consecutive fracture patients. Of the 235 patients, 117 underwent surgical stabilization. Of these 117 patients, 12 suffered a perioperative infection: two had a staphylococcal infection and 10 had a polymicrobial infection with both gram-negative and gram-positive organisms. There was a statistically higher infection rate in completely neurologically injured patients compared to those with no deficit or incomplete injuries. The authors concluded that aggressive and earlier intervention is required in this patient population. The study provides Level IV therapeutic evidence, suggesting that the infection rate in instrumented spinal surgery for trauma patients receiving antibiotic prophylaxis is 10%. In cases with neurologic injury, the infection rate is higher, and infections tend to be polymicrobial. The study does not provide direct answers but gives epidemiological evidence that instrumented spinal procedures have a higher-than-expected infection rate.

## Study 2: Hellbusch et al. (2008)

Hellbusch et al. conducted a prospective, randomized controlled trial to examine the effects of different antibiotic dosing regimens on the postoperative infection rate in instrumented lumbar spinal fusion. Two hundred sixty-nine patients were randomized into either a preoperative-only protocol or a preoperative with extended postoperative antibiotic protocol. Patients in the preoperative-only group received a single dose of intravenous cefazolin, while the extended postoperative group received the same dose before surgery and continued with postoperative cefazolin every 8 hours for 3 days, followed by oral cephalexin for 7 days. At 21 days, there was no significant difference in infection rates between the two protocols. The study provides Level IV evidence and suggests that a single dose of cefazolin is as effective as a multiple-dosing regimen for lumbar fusion procedures.

## Study 3: Wimmer et al. (1998)

Wimmer et al. performed a prospective series examining antibiotic prophylaxis in an instrumented spinal fusion population. Of 110 patients, 56 were treated for painful spondylolisthesis and 54 for scoliosis. Two grams of cefamandole were given preoperatively, followed by three postoperative doses. One early infection was reported in the spondylolisthesis group, and one late infection was reported in the scoliosis group. The study provides Level IV therapeutic evidence, indicating that perioperative prophylactic antibiotics are effective in reducing the infection rate in instrumented spinal surgery.

## Future Directions for Research

The North American Spine Society recommends basing antibiotic prophylaxis on existing data and does not call for further definitive studies. However, randomized controlled trials could strengthen these recommendations by stratifying results across specific patient populations, comorbidities, clinical conditions, and varying dosing and routes of administration.

# What Rate of Surgical Site Infections Can Be Expected with the Use of Antibiotic Prophylaxis in Patients With and Without Medical Comorbidities?

## Consensus Statement

Despite appropriate prophylaxis, the rate of surgical site infections in spine surgery is 0.7% - 10%. The expected rate for patients without comorbidities ranges from 0.7% to 4.3%, and for patients with comorbidities, it ranges from 2.0% to 10%. Current best practices with antibiotic protocols have not been able to eliminate surgical site infections entirely.

## Study 1: Chen et al. (2001)

Chen et al. performed a retrospective case-control study to determine the role diabetes plays in spinal infection risk. Of 195 spinal infection patients, 30 had diabetes, and 165 did not. Prophylactic protocols varied, and surgeries were heterogeneous with both instrumented and uninstrumented procedures. The study found that diabetes significantly increased the risk of infection in spinal arthrodesis surgery. This provides Level II prognostic evidence, confirming that diabetes increases the risk of infection after spinal surgery.

## Study 2: Hellbusch et al. (2008)

Hellbusch et al. conducted a prospective, randomized controlled trial on the effects of multiple antibiotic dosing regimens on the postoperative infection rate in lumbar spinal fusion. The study found no significant difference in infection rates between a preoperative-only and preoperative plus extended postoperative regimen. This study provides Level II evidence, indicating that a single dose of cephazolin is as effective as a multi-dose regimen.

## Study 3: Sweet et al. (2007)

Sweet et al. performed a retrospective study evaluating the safety and efficacy of adjunctive local application of vancomycin in posterior instrumented thoracic and lumbar spine wounds. The study found that local vancomycin powder application significantly reduced the postoperative wound infection rate (0.2%) compared to intravenous cephalexin (2.6%). This study provides Level II prognostic evidence supporting the use of local vancomycin in spinal surgeries.

## Study 4: Rubinstein et al. (1994)

Rubinstein et al. conducted a prospective randomized controlled trial to investigate the efficacy of a single dose of 1g of cephazolin in reducing postoperative infections in lumbar spine surgeries. The study found that 12.7% of patients in the placebo group developed infections, compared to 4.3% in the cephazolin group. This study provides Level II prognostic evidence supporting the use of a single dose of cephazolin in lumbar spinal surgery.

# Future Directions for Research

Well-designed studies are needed to further define the rate of surgical site infections in patients with comorbidities and to optimize antibiotic regimens and dosing strategies for these populations.
