## Discontinuation of Antibiotic Prophylaxis in Spinal Surgery

### Introduction

For patients receiving antibiotic prophylaxis prior to open spine surgery, does discontinuation of prophylaxis at 24 hours result in decreased postoperative infection rates as compared to longer periods of administration?

For typical, uncomplicated cases, a single dose of preoperative prophylactic antibiotics with intraoperative redosing as needed is suggested to decrease the risk of surgical site infection.

**Grade of Recommendation**: B

### Evidence Supporting Antibiotic Discontinuation

#### Dobzyniak et al. (2003)

Dobzyniak et al. conducted a retrospective comparative study examining the efficacy of single versus multiple dosing for lumbar disc surgery. The antibiotics used for prophylaxis included cephazolin (1 g), clindamycin (600 mg), and vancomycin (1 g plus clindamycin 600 mg). In total, 635 patients were included in the study, with 418 receiving the multidose regimen and 192 receiving a single dose. Infection was confirmed at six weeks via cultures and physician assessment. The infection rate was 1.56% (3/192) with single dosing versus 1.20% (5/418) with multiple dosing, with no statistically significant difference ($\mathtt{p}=0.711$). The authors concluded that a single preoperative dose of prophylactic antibiotics is as effective as multiple dosing in preventing wound infections in lumbar disc surgery.

#### Hellbusch et al. (2008)

Hellbusch et al. conducted a prospective, randomized controlled trial to examine the effects of multiple dosing regimens on postoperative infection rates in instrumented lumbar spinal fusion. Two hundred sixty-nine patients were randomized into either a preoperative-only protocol or preoperative with extended postoperative antibiotics. At 21 days follow-up, there was no significant difference in infection rates between the two groups. The postoperative infection rates were 4.3% for the preoperative-only group and 1.7% for the preoperative with extended antibiotic group, but statistical significance was not achieved. The study suggested that preoperative prophylactic antibiotics are effective in reducing infection rates but prolonged postoperative antibiotic dosing is not necessary.

#### Kakimaru et al. (2010)

Kakimaru et al. conducted a retrospective comparative study comparing infection rates in spinal surgery with and without postoperative antimicrobial prophylaxis. Of the 284 patients included in the study, 141 received preoperative and postoperative dosing, and 143 received preoperative and intraoperative dosing. Infection rates were 2.8% in the postoperative dosing group and 1.4% in the no postoperative dosing group ($\mathtt{p}=0.335$). The authors concluded that postoperative antimicrobial prophylaxis does not influence the rate of surgical site infections and may be unnecessary.

#### Kanayama et al. (2007)

Kanayama et al. performed a retrospective comparative study to compare the rate of surgical site infections in lumbar spine surgeries for two different antibiotic prophylaxis protocols. In the postoperative-dose group, 1133 patients received antibiotics for 5-7 days after surgery, while 464 patients in the no-postoperative-dose group only received antibiotics on the day of surgery. The infection rate was 0.8% in the postoperative-dose group and 0.4% in the no-postoperative-dose group, but this difference was not significant. The authors concluded that no significant difference was observed between protocols.

#### Takahashi et al. (2009)

Takahashi et al. compared the effectiveness of preoperative cephalosporin with various postoperative dosing schedules in reducing infection rates following a variety of spinal surgeries. The study included 1415 patients with different groups receiving various dosing regimens. The overall frequency of surgical site infections in the different groups was 2.6% (Group 1), 0.9% (Group 2), 0% (Group 3 and Group 4). The authors concluded that a shorter duration of antimicrobial prophylaxis is more effective than longer durations.

### Prolonged Antibiotic Use in Complex Cases

While there is no significant benefit to prolonged antibiotic use in standard spine surgery, some complex cases (e.g., trauma, cord injury, neuromuscular disease, diabetes) may require extended prophylaxis. Comorbidities and complex situations reviewed in the literature include obesity, diabetes, neurologic deficits, incontinence, and elevated preoperative or postoperative serum glucose levels.

**Grade of Recommendation**: C

While there appears to be clinical scenarios where prolonged antibiotics may be helpful, the evidence is weak, and the specific indications are not completely clear. Until better data is available, the surgeon should make this decision based on their experience, the regional historical profile of infections in their centers, the complexity of the surgery, and the specific comorbidities of the patient.

### Risk Factors for Surgical Site Infection

Olsen et al. (2003) performed a retrospective case-control study to identify the independent risk factors for surgical site infections after laminectomy or spinal fusion. The study found that postoperative incontinence, obesity, tumor resection, and a posterior approach were significant risk factors. This study provides Level III prognostic evidence that these factors increase the risk of infection.

Olsen et al. (2008) also identified independent risk factors for surgical site infection following orthopedic spinal operations, including diabetes, suboptimal timing of prophylactic antibiotic therapy, and elevated serum glucose levels. This study provides Level III prognostic evidence that these factors increase the risk of infection and that prolonged prophylactic regimens may be necessary for these high-risk patients.

## Future Directions for Research

Controlled studies comparing infection rates in spinal surgical patients with trauma, neuromuscular disease, diabetes, or other comorbidities who received antibiotics for 24 hours versus longer durations are suggested.
