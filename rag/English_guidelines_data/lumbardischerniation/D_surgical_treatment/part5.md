## What are the medium-term (one to four years) and long-term (greater than four years) results of surgical management of lumbar disc herniation with radiculopathy?

The performance of surgical decompression is suggested to provide better medium-term (one to four years) symptom relief compared to medical/interventional management of patients with radiculopathy from lumbar disc herniation whose symptoms are severe enough to warrant surgery.

**Grade of Recommendation: B**

Butterman et al. conducted a prospective randomized controlled trial comparing microdiscectomy to epidural steroid injection in a select population of patients with large lumbar disc herniations. Of the 100 patients included, 50 were assigned to each treatment group. Outcomes were assessed at three years using the VAS, Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), and patient satisfaction as determined by a patient questionnaire. At one and three months, the surgically treated patients had a significant increase in motor function compared to the patients treated with epidural steroid injection. At two years, the motor function was not significantly different. The ODI and pain levels were similar at all time points. The surgical group reported a statistically significant decrease in pain medication usage at one and three months. The surgically treated group expressed 92%-98% satisfaction, versus 42%-56% for the epidural steroid injection group. Twenty-seven patients who failed epidural steroid injections crossed over to the surgical treatment group. The authors concluded that epidural steroid injection was not as effective as discectomy in reducing symptoms associated with a large herniation of the lumbar disc. This study provides Level II therapeutic evidence that patients with large disc herniations, occupying more than 25% of the spinal canal, with symptoms that do not resolve in six weeks, benefit more from surgery than epidural injections. However, about 50% of patients who have injections will improve.

Weinstein et al. reported results from a prospective comparative study including 743 patients comparing surgical and medical/interventional treatment of lumbar intervertebral disc herniation. The surgically treated group consisted of 528 patients, while the medical/interventional group had 191 patients. Outcomes were assessed for up to two years using the SF-36, ODI, patient self-reported improvement, work status, and satisfaction. At three months, patients in the surgical group showed statistically significant improvement in measures of bodily pain, physical function, and the Oswestry Disability Index, with these improvements remaining statistically significant at two years. The authors concluded that patients with persistent sciatica from lumbar disc herniation improved in both surgical and medical/interventional treatment groups, with those opting for surgery reporting greater improvements. This study provides Level II therapeutic evidence that surgical treatment of lumbar disc herniation results in earlier and greater improvement compared to medical/interventional treatment.

Weinstein et al. conducted a randomized controlled trial with 472 patients to assess the efficacy of surgery and medical/interventional treatment for lumbar intervertebral disc herniation. The intent-to-treat analysis was inconclusive due to significant crossover, but the "as-treated" analysis, which was based on the treatment choices of the patients, showed that surgery provided clinically and statistically significant benefits in every measure at each time point up to two years. This study provides Level II evidence supporting the efficacy of microdiscectomy for symptomatic lumbar disc herniations.

Peul et al. conducted a prospective study including 283 patients comparing early surgical intervention with prolonged conservative care followed by surgery if necessary. Outcomes were assessed at 52 weeks using the Roland Morris Disability Questionnaire and VAS. While there was no significant difference in disability scores between the groups, leg pain relief was faster for those assigned to early surgery. The study concluded that early surgery offers faster recovery and better pain relief compared to prolonged conservative measures, but long-term outcomes were similar for both groups.

### Surgical decompression provides long-term (greater than four years) symptom relief for patients with radiculopathy from lumbar disc herniation whose symptoms warrant surgery. It should be noted that a substantial portion (23-28%) of patients will have chronic back or leg pain.

## Level of Evidence: IV

Bakhsh et al. described a retrospective case series assessing the long-term outcome of lumbar disc surgery on relief of sciatic leg pain. Of the 68 patients treated with surgical decompression, 54 were pain-free in the immediate postoperative period. However, at 10 years, only 27% had long-lasting pain relief. This study provides Level IV therapeutic evidence indicating that significant pain relief can be obtained in most patients, but symptoms may recur over time.

Findlay et al. conducted a retrospective case series evaluating the long-term outcome of lumbar microdiscectomy for herniated nucleus pulposus with sciatica. Of 88 patients, 90% were followed for 10 years. The study showed that while 75% of patients were "unchanged" from their six-month results, 91% experienced success at six months, and 83% at 10 years. The study concluded that there was no significant deterioration of the high success rate of lumbar microdiscectomy at long-term follow-up.

Loupasis et al. assessed the long-term effects of conventional discectomy for lumbar disc herniation over an average of 12.2 years. Late results were satisfactory in 64% of patients, with a reoperation rate of 7.3%. This study provides Level IV therapeutic evidence that most patients treated with discectomy have satisfactory long-term results, although 28% still experience significant pain.

Padua et al. reported long-term results from standard discectomy surgery for disc herniation in a cohort of 150 patients. Long-term follow-up data were available for 120 patients, showing that the procedure provided good outcomes in the majority of cases, with 23.4% experiencing leg pain. This study provides Level IV therapeutic evidence that discectomy yields good long-term results.

Porchet et al. reported the results of a retrospective comparative study evaluating long-term outcomes for surgery for foraminal and extraforaminal lumbar disc herniation. The study included 22 patients and showed good to excellent results in 73% of cases. This study provides Level IV therapeutic evidence supporting the use of far lateral discectomy for lumbar disc herniation.

Wenger et al. described the long-term outcomes of 104 patients after Williams'sequestrectomy. Success rates were 92.5%, 94.7%, and 93.3% for lumbalgia, radicular pain, and neurological dysfunction, respectively, at five years. The study concluded that sequestrectomy is a safe operative modality and yields favorable outcomes in the long term.

## Future Directions for Research

The work group identified the following suggestions for future studies, which would generate meaningful evidence to further define the medium- and long-term results for surgical management of lumbar disc herniation with radiculopathy.

### Recommendation #1:
Follow-up of patients in studies describing medium-term outcomes would provide information on long-term treatment outcomes.

### Recommendation #2:
Future long-term studies should include an untreated control group when ethically feasible.

### Recommendation #3:
Future long-term outcome studies should include results specific to each of the surgical treatment methods.
