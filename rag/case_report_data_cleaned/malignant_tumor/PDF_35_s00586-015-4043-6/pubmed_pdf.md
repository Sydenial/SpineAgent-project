# Metastatic thymoma presenting as spontaneous epidural lumbar haematoma

## Abstract

We report the case of a 44-year-old man who was found to have metastatic thymoma to his lumbar spine presenting as a spontaneous epidural haematoma. The man presented with back pain and cauda equina–like symptoms in the absence of trauma, antiplatelet, or anticoagulant agents. Following a laminectomy and excision of the epidural collection, he made a full neurological recovery. Histopathology of the haematoma demonstrated metastatic thymoma. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first such case of metastatic thymoma to the lumbar spine presenting as a spontaneous epidural collection. We believe that in all patients with spontaneous spinal epidural haematoma and a background of malignancy, histopathological analysis should be sought.

## Introduction

Spontaneous spinal epidural haematoma (SSEH) is an exceedingly rare condition with an incidence of 0.1 per 100,000 [1]. A number of potential causes for SSEH have been identified, including anticoagulant and antiplatelet use, bleeding diatheses, vascular malformations, and hypertension, but there have only been four previously described cases where an underlying malignancy has been responsible [2–5]. Three of those cases were haematological malignancies, and the fourth was metastatic bronchial adenocarcinoma. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case of an SSEH arising from metastatic thymoma.

## Case Report

### History and Examination

This 44-year-old man presented with a 2-week history of midline lower back pain, gait disturbance, right leg sensory disturbance, bladder discomfort, and bowel incontinence. He described claudicant lower limb symptoms for 6 weeks.

The patient had a past history of pericardial effusion and locally advanced type B2 thymoma, which were diagnosed concurrently 5 years earlier. He had received three cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy with cisplatin, adriamycin, and cyclophosphamide, after which only partial debulking surgery was possible as the tumour had encased the great vessels. Adjuvant radiotherapy was also administered. He had been disease free during 5 years of follow-up. He denied any previous spinal trauma or procedures and also denied anticoagulant or antiplatelet use.

On examination, there was a mixed distribution weakness of both lower limbs. His ankle power was most affected, with absent ankle jerks.

### Investigations

Full blood count, electrolytes, coagulation, and renal function tests were normal. The patient was afebrile.

Pre- and post-contrast lumbar spine MRI demonstrated a posterior epidural lesion at L4–5 with irregular rim enhancement. The lesion was 3.5 cm in diameter and caused significant compression of the cauda equina. The differential diagnosis included epidural abscess, epidural haematoma, and neoplasia.

### Operation and Postoperative Course

The patient underwent emergency L3/4 laminectomy and excision of the epidural collection. Intraoperatively, an epidural haematoma was visualised at L3/4 with some extension to L5. This was removed in its entirety and sent for histopathological analysis. The dura was opened, and no intradural mass was found.

Postoperatively, the patient’s symptoms improved, and he was able to mobilise with assistance. Magnetic resonance angiography and autoantibody testing postoperatively failed to identify a cause. The patient was discharged and underwent outpatient rehabilitation, but he was lost to initial follow-up.

Histopathology from the haematoma demonstrated metastatic thymoma. After difficulty contacting the patient, he was eventually reviewed via his oncologist, and an MRI performed 11 months postoperatively demonstrated metastatic deposits within L2 and L3. The patient remained asymptomatic and received radiotherapy to his lumbar spine. Subsequently, whole-body SPECT/CT detected further metastasis in his left ileum, which was also irradiated. Surveillance imaging later demonstrated liver metastases, which were treated with selective internal radiation therapy. Despite his metastatic disease, the patient remained remarkably well. At 2-year follow-up, he was asymptomatic with a normal neurological examination.

## Discussion

### Spontaneous Spinal Epidural Haematoma

First described by Blauby [6], SSEH is a very rare cause of spinal cord compression, with an estimated incidence of 0.1 per 100,000 people [1]. SSEH can affect all age groups but is most common after 50 years of age [7, 8]. Anatomically, SSEH most commonly affects the cervical and thoracic spine, with C5 to T2 accounting for approximately one-third of reported segments [1, 8].

Patients typically present with localised pain and symptoms of spinal cord or nerve root compression. Time to surgery and the degree of preoperative neurological deficit are key prognostic factors [9, 10]. Prompt diagnosis with MRI [11] and early decompressive surgery are therefore the most effective management strategies [1, 9, 10].

A number of potential causes of SSEH have been described, including anticoagulant and antiplatelet therapy, vascular malformations, pregnancy, and increases in vascular pressure [8]. Malignancy as a cause has been described only four times previously [2–5]. Three cases involved haematological malignancies, and the fourth involved bronchial adenocarcinoma with vertebral metastases and an epidural haematoma [4]. The mechanism remains unclear but may relate to epidural inflammation or venous plexus fragility [2, 4].

### Thymoma

Thymomas are relatively uncommon, typically slow-growing neoplasms of thymic epithelial cells [12]. They are associated with autoimmune conditions such as myasthenia gravis and systemic lupus erythematosus, as well as blood dyscrasias and other malignancies [12, 13]. The Masaoka staging system is the most widely used classification system and includes metastatic disease [14].

Metastatic thymoma is uncommon, and extrathoracic metastasis is even rarer, with an estimated incidence of 1–6% [15, 16]. Reported metastatic sites include liver, bone, and lymph nodes, with less frequent involvement of brain, kidney, and soft tissue [16–18]. Metastasis of thymoma to the spine causing cord compression has been reported in a small number of cases [17, 19–21], but none have been associated with haemorrhage. In general, metastatic thymoma is not considered a haemorrhagic tumour, with only one reported case of haemorrhagic cerebral metastasis [22].

Histopathologically, type B2 thymoma cells tend to palisade around vessels and cause dilation of perivascular spaces [23]. It is possible that vascular invasion in our patient resulted in haemorrhage. The relationship between histological subtype and metastatic potential remains unclear [16]. Despite these uncertainties, a 5-year progression-free survival rate of 41.7% has been reported for advanced disease in Masaoka’s series [14].

### Management of SSEH and Metastatic Thymoma

Patients with SSEH usually present with acute pain and neurological deficits. Management options include conservative and surgical approaches. When there is complete preoperative sensorimotor deficit, surgery within 36 hours yields the best outcomes, while in patients with incomplete deficits, surgery within 48 hours is associated with better recovery [10]. Once identified on MRI, management typically involves decompressive laminectomy [9], which also allows tissue sampling and identification of underlying pathology, as in this case.

Due to the small number of reported cases, an optimal management strategy for metastatic thymoma has not been clearly defined [15, 24]. Generally, treatment involves surgical resection where feasible, combined with adjuvant radiotherapy [24]. In our patient, the tumour was radiosensitive, and radiotherapy alone achieved good neurological outcomes, with no recurrence or neurological deficit at 24 months follow-up.

## Conclusion

Spontaneous spinal epidural haematoma is a very rare and rapidly progressive cause of spinal cord compression. In patients with a known malignancy, metastatic disease should be considered as an underlying cause. Emergency decompressive laminectomy and postoperative rehabilitation remain key aspects of management.
