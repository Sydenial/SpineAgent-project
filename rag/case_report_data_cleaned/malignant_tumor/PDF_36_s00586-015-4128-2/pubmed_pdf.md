# Giant cystic sacral schwannoma mimicking Tarlov cyst: a case report

## Abstract

**Purpose**  
To present a rare case of a giant schwannoma of the sacrum mimicking a Tarlov cyst.

**Methods**  
A 58-year-old woman had a 1-year history of low back pain. MRI revealed a large cystic mass in the sacral canal with bony erosion. A radiological diagnosis of Tarlov cyst was made.

**Results**  
The patient underwent surgical treatment for the lesion, which revealed a solid mass. Histopathological examination of the tumor confirmed the diagnosis of schwannoma. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient had significant improvement in her pain 1 month postoperatively.

**Conclusion**  
Giant cystic schwannoma of the sacrum is a very rare diagnosis that may be overlooked in favor of more common cystic etiologies, but its treatment is significantly different. Care should be taken to include this diagnosis in the differential for a cystic sacral mass.

## Introduction

Schwannomas are benign, slow-growing neoplasms that arise from myelinated nerve sheaths. Spinal schwannomas are the most common intradural extramedullary tumors, accounting for 25% of all primary spinal tumors [1, 2]. However, sacral schwannomas are very rare, accounting for only 1–5% [3]. Because of their indolent growth, the mobility of the sacral nerve roots, and the generous width of the sacral canal, these tumors can reach large sizes before becoming symptomatic.

Most schwannomas are solid or heterogeneous tumors, occasionally exhibiting hemorrhagic, calcific, or fibrotic degenerative changes. Cystic changes, however, are exceedingly rare, with only a few cases reported in the literature [4–6]. We present a case of a 58-year-old woman with a cystic giant sacral schwannoma mimicking an atypical Tarlov cyst.

## Case Report

A 58-year-old woman presented with a 1-year history of left buttock and lower back pain. Occasionally, she experienced left leg pain and rare tingling sensations extending into her toes in a general sciatic distribution. Her symptoms were aggravated by sitting and progressively worsened throughout the day. Buttock pain improved when lying supine.

General physical examination revealed some loss of lumbar lordosis. Neurologic examination demonstrated bilateral patellar and Achilles hyperreflexia. MRI of the lumbar spine without contrast revealed an expansile $2.2 \times 3.5 \times 3.3$ cm cystic lesion expanding the sacrum beginning at S2–3. The mass principally involved the left S3 segment but crossed the midline and also involved the right S3 nerve root. The signal characteristics differed slightly from cerebrospinal fluid around the nerve roots.

Computed tomography with contrast revealed a well-circumscribed, hypodense, non-enhancing cystic structure occupying the paramedian sacral central canal, extending from the superior aspect of S2 to the inferior aspect of S3, causing mild expansion and scalloping of S2–S3 and the posterior column.

The preoperative diagnosis was a Tarlov cyst. The patient underwent left sacral laminectomies of S1, S2, and S3 with mass resection and reconstruction using a paraspinal muscle flap. After removal of the thinned sacral lamina, a solid mass was identified beneath a thinned dural layer. The mass was rubbery in consistency and associated with the left S2 nerve root both within and external to the sacral dura.

The dura was slightly torn during bony removal, but no obvious cerebrospinal fluid leak was identified. Normal sacral roots traversing the mass superiorly and inferiorly were identified and stimulated using neuromonitoring; these included the S1 and S3 roots and were not involved in the mass. Although the mass had minimal attachment to the S2 nerve root, this nerve was atrophic and nonfunctional as demonstrated by intraoperative stimulation. Portions of the mass and associated nerve tissue were removed, along with a smaller lesion of similar appearance. Closure was performed using a paraspinous muscle flap.

Histopathological examination demonstrated Antoni B area tissue with nuclear palisading in a neurofibromatous background, confirming the diagnosis of cystic schwannoma.

The patient tolerated the procedure well. The postoperative course was uneventful, and she was discharged home 3 days after surgery without complications. At 1-month follow-up, the patient reported a reduction in left buttock pain from constant to intermittent and from focal to more diffuse. Mild residual left leg pain persisted, but she was able to walk several miles without pain limitation.

## Discussion

Nerve sheath tumors such as schwannomas and neurofibromas account for approximately 30% of all intraspinal tumors [7], most commonly arising in the lumbar region with a preference for sensory nerves and dorsal roots [8]. Sacral schwannomas are rare, with approximately 50 cases reported worldwide [3]. Due to their slow growth and the mobility of sacral nerve roots, symptoms may not manifest until the tumor becomes quite large [9].

Common presenting symptoms include progressive low back pain, numbness or paresthesias, neurological deficits, and failure of conservative therapy [10]. Acute low back pain may result from nerve root torsion or intratumoral hemorrhage [11, 12], and nonspecific abdominal pain has also been reported [13].

Sacral schwannomas with a predominantly cystic component are rarer still. Proposed mechanisms for cyst formation include ischemic necrosis, intratumoral hemorrhage, mucinous degeneration, and microcyst formation. The differential diagnosis for a large cystic lesion in this region is broad and includes abscess, arachnoid cyst, ependymoma, epidermoid cyst, cystic lymphangioma, cystic teratoma, and cystic meningioma.

MRI is the most useful imaging modality. Schwannomas typically demonstrate low-to-intermediate signal intensity on T1-weighted images and heterogeneous signal intensity on T2-weighted images corresponding to cystic areas [14, 15]. Rim enhancement following contrast administration is characteristic [15]. CT may reveal a nonspecific, well-circumscribed lesion with low or heterogeneous density, occasionally with central necrosis [16]. However, no radiographic features are pathognomonic for cystic schwannomas.

Tarlov cysts are commonly located in the S1–S4 region, arise from the nerve root sheath at the dorsal root ganglion, and contain fluid with signal characteristics similar to cerebrospinal fluid [17]. These features, along with nonspecific symptoms, frequently lead to misdiagnosis. Given that treatment strategies differ substantially, cystic schwannomas should be considered in the differential diagnosis of a cystic osteolytic sacral mass.

Histopathological examination is essential for definitive diagnosis. Schwannomas classically demonstrate Antoni A and Antoni B patterns, with spindle-shaped cells and occasional Verocay bodies [18]. In contrast, previously treated Tarlov cysts may resemble solid tumors histologically.

Unlike Tarlov cysts, the optimal treatment for giant spinal schwannomas is complete surgical excision, which minimizes the risk of recurrence associated with subtotal resection [6, 19, 20]. Although sacrifice of an associated nerve root may be necessary, complete excision without neurological deficit is possible, as nerve root entrapment occurs in only approximately 50% of cases [21–23].

The surgical approach depends on tumor extent. A posterior approach is suitable when the tumor does not extend anterior to the vertebral body. Tumors with significant anterior extension may require a combined approach. Large sacral tumors pose challenges due to fragile capsules, indistinct arachnoid planes, increased vascularity, and bony erosion that may compromise spinal stability. Various stabilization techniques have been described [6, 24]. In this case, complete excision was achieved without stabilization because the anterior sacral bone remained intact.

Cystic sacral schwannomas are exceedingly rare and may closely mimic other cystic lesions radiographically and clinically. Inclusion of this entity in the differential diagnosis of cystic sacral masses is critical, as management differs significantly from that of other lesions such as Tarlov cysts.
