# Tenosynovial Giant Cell Tumor, Diffuse Type / Pigmented Villonodular Synovitis in a Pars Defect: A Case Report

## Abstract

### Study Design
Case report.

### Summary of Background Data

## Introduction

Tenosynovial giant cell tumor, diffuse type / pigmented villonodular synovitis (PVNS) is a slowly progressing mass lesion of the synovial membrane with uncertain etiology that occurs predominantly in the knee and hip joints. Spondylolysis is a defect of the pars interarticularis and is a known sequela of stress fracture. Pars defects are considered to communicate with both the cranial and caudal facet joints. Furthermore, they are sometimes accompanied by inflammation and cause low back pain in the form of communicating synovitis, which spreads from the adjacent facet joints [1].

Although PVNS rarely occurs in the spine, several case studies have reported PVNS in the facet joints [2]. However, no studies in the English literature have reported PVNS in a pars defect in lumbar spondylolysis. Here, we present a case of PVNS in a pars defect in a patient with lumbar spondylolysis.

---

## Case Report

A healthy 14-year-old female was referred to our hospital with a 5-month history of low back pain. She belonged to a basketball team and had not experienced any obvious traumatic episodes. Physical examination revealed tenderness around the L5 spinous process and in the bilateral L4–L5 paravertebral muscles. She had no neurological symptoms such as sensory disturbance or motor weakness in either lower limb.

Plain radiographs revealed bilateral spondylolysis at L5, and computed tomographic scans revealed a tumorous mass measuring approximately $1 \times 2$ cm located on the left pars defect at L5, slightly eroding the L5 lamina.

Magnetic resonance imaging comprising T1-weighted images, short-tau inversion recovery, T2-weighted images, T2 STAR-weighted images, and gadolinium-enhanced fat-saturated T1-weighted images was performed with a field strength of 1.5 T. The mass lesion showed intermediate intensity and gadolinium enhancement on T1-weighted images and high intensity on T2-weighted and T2 STAR-weighted images. Sagittal imaging confirmed that the mass arose from the pars defect.

After undergoing computed tomography–guided needle biopsy, a preoperative pathological diagnosis of PVNS was made. Photomicrographs showed proliferative mononuclear histiocyte-like cells and a few multinucleated giant cells. Foamy cells and slight hemosiderin deposition were evident.

Gross total resection and marginal resection of the lamina and ligamentum flavum were planned. The patient was positioned prone, and a midline lumbar incision was made to clear the biopsy tract. Only the left side of the L4–L5 lamina was exposed, preserving the posterior structures on the right side. The mass located on the pars defect was considered to communicate with the adjacent facet joints at L4–L5 and L5–S1. It adhered to the ligamentum flavum at L4–L5 and was adjacent to the infralamina at L4 and the supralamina at L5.

Total gross resection with partial laminectomy at left L4 and L5 and partial facetectomy at left L4–L5 and L5–S1 was required, with careful protection of the L5 nerve root passing beneath the pars defect. The resected mass was a softly elastic, light yellow-reddish oval lesion measuring approximately $2.5 \times 1.5 \times 1.5$ cm. Both the gross appearance and postoperative pathological diagnosis were consistent with PVNS.

The postoperative clinical course was uneventful. Postoperative computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging revealed no residual lesion and no apparent instability.

## Discussion

PVNS occurs in approximately 80% of cases in the knee joint and 15% in the hip joint, but it rarely occurs in the spine [3,4]. Kleinman et al. reported the first case of PVNS in the cervical spine in 1980 [5]. Because of the rarity of spinal PVNS, careful evaluation is required to obtain an accurate diagnosis. Reported symptoms include low back pain, radiculopathy, and myelopathy, although one-third of cases are asymptomatic and detected incidentally by imaging. Our patient presented only with low back pain, a nonspecific symptom that cannot alone distinguish PVNS.

Imaging studies are vital for diagnosis. PVNS often exhibits bone erosion on plain radiographs and computed tomography. Typical magnetic resonance imaging features include low intensity on both T1- and T2-weighted images, with low intensity on T2 STAR-weighted images reflecting hemosiderin deposition [2]. Gadolinium-enhanced fat-saturated T1-weighted imaging may show contrast enhancement, suggesting a soft tissue tumor [6]. In the present case, the lesion demonstrated intermediate signal intensity and gadolinium enhancement on T1-weighted images and high intensity on T2-weighted and T2 STAR-weighted images, findings that suggested a soft tissue tumor but were not specific for PVNS. Therefore, computed tomography–guided needle biopsy was necessary to establish a definitive diagnosis. Although hemosiderin deposition is characteristic of PVNS, spinal cases are often less pigmented [7,8], which was also observed in this case.

PVNS is a benign but locally aggressive tumor, and complete excision is required to reduce the risk of recurrence [10–12]. In this patient, gross total resection and marginal resection of the lamina and ligamentum flavum were performed because a histological diagnosis had been obtained preoperatively.

The origin of PVNS remains controversial. Jaffe et al. first used the term PVNS to describe a proliferative villonodular lesion of the synovium [13]. Whether PVNS represents an inflammatory lesion or a neoplasm is still debated, and the cells of origin remain unclear [14,15]. In the present case, it was not possible to determine whether the tumor originated from the L4–L5 or L5–S1 facet joint because it adhered to the pseudoarthrotic pars defect. Previous studies have demonstrated that pseudoarthrotic pars defects in lumbar spondylolysis communicate with both cranial and caudal facet joints [1,16]. Therefore, we speculate that the PVNS arose from an adjacent facet joint and extended into the pars defect.

Overexpression of inflammatory mediators such as colony-stimulating factor 1 in neoplastic cells has been proposed to cause accumulation of non-neoplastic cells, forming a tumorous mass in PVNS [15,17]. It is possible that PVNS in this case resulted from an inflammatory reaction associated with spondylolysis, although this remains speculative given the rarity of such cases. Consequently, PVNS in pars defects of lumbar spondylolysis may represent a combination of reactive and neoplastic processes.

---

## Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of PVNS occurring in a pars defect in a patient with lumbar spondylolysis. Although complete resection was achieved, careful long-term follow-up is warranted due to the locally aggressive nature of the disease.

---

## Key Points

- This is the first reported case of PVNS occurring in a pars defect in lumbar spondylolysis.  
- PVNS is a benign but locally aggressive tumor, and complete excision is required to minimize recurrence risk.  
- Preoperative computed tomography–guided needle biopsy was essential for establishing a definitive diagnosis.  
- Communication between pseudoarthrotic pars defects and adjacent facet joints may play a role in the origin of PVNS in lumbar spondylolysis.
