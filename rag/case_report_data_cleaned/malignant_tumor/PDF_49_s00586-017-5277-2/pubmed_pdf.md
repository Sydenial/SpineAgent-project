# Malignant triton tumor: Grand Round presentation of a rare aggressive thoracolumbar spine tumor

## Abstract

### Introduction
We report a rare and aggressive case of malignant triton tumor (MTT) at the thoracolumbar junction with foraminal extension, initially misdiagnosed as a schwannoma.

### Materials and Methods
A 70-year-old man presented with a 2-year history of lower back pain and left L4 radiculopathy, with no history of neurofibromatosis.

### Results
Preoperative MRI suggested a typical schwannoma. After complete marginal resection, histological findings revealed an MTT. The patient developed local and regional recurrence and died 10 months after surgery. MTTs are a subgroup of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors that arise from Schwann cells of peripheral nerves or within existing neurofibromas and show rhabdomyoblastic differentiation.

### Conclusion
Based on this Grand Round case and a review of the relevant literature, we describe a highly aggressive and fast-growing tumor with very high local and distant recurrence rates. There is no consensus treatment strategy, and patients usually die shortly after diagnosis.

## Case Presentation

A 70-year-old man with a 2-year history of lower back pain and left L4 radiculopathy was admitted to our department. His radicular pain had gradually increased. He had a history of prostatic adenocarcinoma 7 years earlier, treated with surgery and hormone therapy. There was no personal or family history of neurofibromatosis. Physical examination revealed no systemic signs of neurofibromatosis. The neurological examination was strictly normal, with no evidence of myelopathy or cauda equina syndrome.

## Diagnostic Imaging

Computed tomography showed degenerative changes of the lumbar spine and a left T12–L1 foraminal mass. MRI of the thoracolumbar spine demonstrated an intradural mass at the T12–L1 level extending through the left T12 foramen into the paraspinal muscles. The tumor measured 32 mm × 11 mm × 2 mm. It appeared homogeneous with intermediate signal intensity on both T1- and T2-weighted images, with heterogeneous enhancement after intravenous gadolinium administration. Based on these findings, the radiologist suggested the diagnosis of a schwannoma arising from the left T12 nerve root.

## Surgical Procedure

Under general anesthesia, a posterior surgical approach was performed. A T12 laminectomy and left-sided medial T12–L1 facetectomy were carried out. After opening the dura mater, a well-encapsulated mass originating from the left T12 nerve root was identified. The macroscopic appearance was consistent with a benign schwannoma. A complete marginal resection was performed, followed by T12–L1 posterolateral fusion with autograft.

## Histological Analysis

Histological examination revealed a highly cellular tumor composed predominantly of fusiform atypical cells, with occasional nuclear pleomorphism. Approximately ten mitoses per ten high-power fields and areas of necrosis were observed. Scattered within the tumor were large cells with rhabdoid features, characterized by abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and peripheral atypical nuclei. Immunohistochemical analysis showed focal expression of S-100 protein in fusiform cells and desmin expression in rhabdoid cells. The tumor cells were negative for CK AE1/AE3, CD3, and EMA. These features supported the diagnosis of a malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor with rhabdomyosarcomatous differentiation, consistent with malignant triton tumor.

## Outcome and Follow-Up

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient experienced significant improvement in back and leg pain. Given his favorable postoperative performance status (World Health Organization score 0), adjuvant chemotherapy was initiated. A lumbar spine MRI performed 3 months postoperatively revealed local and regional recurrence with multimetastatic dissemination throughout the cauda equina. PET-CT demonstrated hypermetabolic lesions at the T12 and L4 levels, as well as rectal hypermetabolism, which was not further investigated endoscopically.

During chemotherapy, the patient developed febrile neutropenia complicated by septic shock. Chemotherapy was discontinued and replaced with palliative radiotherapy. The patient died 10 months after diagnosis.

## Description of the Condition

Malignant triton tumor is an extremely rare and aggressive sarcoma arising from peripheral nerve sheaths, characterized by a high rate of local recurrence. It is a subtype of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs) and is defined by rhabdomyoblastic differentiation [1–3].

Rhabdomyosarcomatous elements in MPNSTs were first described by Masson in 1932 in patients with neurofibromatosis [4]. Fewer than 170 cases of MTT have been reported in the literature. MTTs account for approximately 5–10% of MPNSTs and are associated with neurofibromatosis type 1 in about 50% of cases, while the remaining cases appear sporadic [5].

The histopathogenesis of MTT remains unclear. Common anatomical locations include the head, neck, extremities, and trunk [5,6], while mediastinal and retroperitoneal sites are less frequent [5,7]. Spinal MTTs are particularly challenging to manage and are associated with a poor prognosis, similar to tumors arising in the trunk and buttocks [8].

Diagnosis is based on histological examination, as clinical and radiological suspicion is difficult due to the rarity of the disease [5]. In the present case, a rare spinal location with foraminal extension was observed in a 70-year-old man, whereas most patients with MTT are younger than 40 years (mean age 39 years) [6]. The clinical presentation of back and leg pain without neurological deficit mimicked degenerative radiculopathy. Imaging suggested a benign schwannoma, leading to an incorrect preoperative diagnosis.

Schwannomas typically arise from dorsal nerve roots and are usually benign, although malignant variants exist. They commonly occur in the fourth to fifth decades of life and may be associated with neurofibromatosis type 2, which carries a risk of malignant transformation. Spinal schwannomas most frequently involve the cervical spine (31%), cauda equina (24%), thoracic spine (22%), upper cervical spine (16%), and conus medullaris (7%) [9]. On MRI, schwannomas are well-circumscribed, often show iso-intensity on T1-weighted images, hyperintensity on T2-weighted images, and variable contrast enhancement.

Similar cases of spinal MTT misdiagnosed as benign tumors have been reported [8]. There is currently no consensus on optimal treatment guidelines. Radical excision with wide margins followed by radiotherapy is generally recommended, while chemotherapy has shown limited efficacy. In retrospect, a preoperative percutaneous biopsy might have altered surgical planning and management in this case. Despite palliative chemotherapy and radiotherapy, the patient died 10 months after diagnosis, consistent with reported median survival times of approximately 12 months [10].

Given the aggressive nature of MTT and the diagnostic challenges it presents, clinicians and radiologists should consider this diagnosis when evaluating presumed schwannomas whenever possible prior to surgical resection.
