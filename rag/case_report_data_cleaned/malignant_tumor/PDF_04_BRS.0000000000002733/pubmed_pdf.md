# Whorling-Sclerosing Variant Meningioma of the Spine: Surgical Management and Outcome of an Extremely Rare Case

## Abstract

### Study Design
Case report.

### Summary of Background Data
Whorling-sclerosing variant meningioma (WSM) is a rare histopathological variant of meningioma, mostly reported in the brain. Only one case of cervical spine WSM has been previously reported. Due to its rarity, there is limited information regarding its clinical, radiological, and histopathological characteristics.

### Methods
A 57-year-old woman presented with progressive quadriparesis and myelopathy for 10 days. Spine magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed multiple ventral cervical and thoracic lesions that were hypointense on T2-weighted images with heterogeneous and scant gadolinium enhancement. The patient underwent anterior cervical corpectomy with gross total resection and circumferential stabilization of the cervicothoracic spine. Thoracic lesions were subsequently resected via laminectomy and right lateral extracavitary approach, followed by pedicular screw fixation.

### Results
Postoperatively, the patient showed improvement in muscle strength and spasticity and became functionally independent (Frankel D). Histopathological examination revealed a hypocellular lobulated sclerotic mass with a whorling growth pattern in the sclerotic areas, consistent with WSM.

### Conclusions
Multiple spinal WSM is an extremely rare condition with malignant behavior and possible cerebrospinal fluid seeding. Surgical management is challenging and requires experience and specialized equipment.

**Level of Evidence:** 4

## Background

The whorling-sclerosing variant meningioma was first described in 1989 by Davidson and Hope as a histological subtype of meningioma. Because of its rarity, there is a paucity of information regarding its clinical course and behavior. Most reported cases involve intracranial locations, and only one case of cervical intramedullary WSM has been described previously. This report presents a rare case of multiple cervical and thoracic WSM successfully managed surgically.

## Clinical Presentation

A 57-year-old woman presented with progressive weakness and paresthesia in both upper extremities, followed by gait disturbance for 10 days. Her medical history included ischemic heart disease and coronary artery bypass grafting five years prior. Neurological examination revealed muscle strength of 4/5 in both upper and lower extremities, hyperactive deep tendon reflexes, positive Hoffmann sign, upward Babinski reflex, spastic gait, and urinary retention.

MRI of the entire spine demonstrated four ventral lesions in the cervical and thoracic regions. The lesions were isointense on T1-weighted images and hypointense on T2-weighted images, with heterogeneous and scant gadolinium enhancement. In the first stage, the cervical lesions were addressed surgically. The patient underwent anterior corpectomy of C4, C5, and C6 with microscopic gross total resection of the lesion, followed by anterior fixation using a mesh cage and cervical plate. Posterior cervical fixation was also performed to ensure spinal stability. The lesion was firm, reddish, and adherent to the dura.

Postoperatively, the patient’s muscle strength improved, and myelopathy and spasticity resolved within two months. After nine months, she developed paraparesis with muscle strength of 1/5, myelopathy, and urofecal retention. A second surgery was performed to remove the thoracic lesions. Bilateral thoracic laminectomy and right lateral extracavitary approach allowed complete resection of the ventrolaterally located lesions. Pedicular screw fixation of the thoracic spine was performed and connected to the previous cervical construct.

Following the second surgery, the patient demonstrated gradual improvement in muscle strength, spasticity, and sphincter function over six months. She became ambulatory with assistance and independent in daily activities (Frankel D).

Histopathological examination revealed a hypocellular lobulated sclerotic mass with a whorling growth pattern. The peripheral region showed small monomorphic cuboidal cells with clear cytoplasm and no atypia or mitotic activity. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated negative staining for EMA, cytokeratin, Olig-2, and S-100, with positive progesterone receptor expression in peripheral tumor cells. Ki-67 index was low at 4%. These findings confirmed the diagnosis of WSM. Cervical and thoracic lesions showed identical pathology.

At one-year follow-up, muscle strength had nearly normalized, sphincter function was intact, and only mild upper extremity paresthesia persisted. There were no signs of myelopathy (Frankel E), and the patient was fully independent in daily living activities. Written informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient and her family.

## Discussion

Whorling-sclerosing variant meningioma is a rare meningioma subtype not included in the latest World Health Organization classification of central nervous system tumors. Most reported cases are intracranial, with spinal involvement being exceptionally rare. This case represents a unique presentation of multiple ventrally located cervical and thoracic WSMs managed surgically over one year.

Several noteworthy aspects merit discussion. First, the multiplicity and ventral spinal location of the lesions suggest possible cerebrospinal fluid dissemination, supported by the appearance of a small budding lesion in the lumbosacral region on follow-up imaging. Second, the tumor demonstrated a significant growth rate, particularly in the thoracic region during the nine-month interval between surgeries. Third, the tumor consistency was extremely firm and adherent to the dura, making resection technically challenging and necessitating advanced surgical techniques and equipment. Finally, the unusual location and behavior of the tumor raise questions regarding its classification and grading. It may warrant inclusion in future WHO classifications with a grade higher than I but lower than II.

In conclusion, multiple spinal whorling-sclerosing variant meningiomas are exceedingly rare, exhibit aggressive behavior, and pose significant surgical challenges. Successful management requires staged surgical approaches, expertise, and appropriate instrumentation.
