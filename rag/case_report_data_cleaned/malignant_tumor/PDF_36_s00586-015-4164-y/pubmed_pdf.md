# Chest wall reconstruction after en bloc Pancoast tumour resection with the use of MatrixRib and SILC fixation systems: technical note

## Abstract

Study design: Technical note.  
Objective: In cases in which partial resection of the rib cage is accomplished with vertebrectomy, reconstruction of the chest wall may be challenging. This is due to the lack of an anchor point, which would typically be the proximal end of a rib or transverse process. We report a straightforward technique for chest wall reconstruction using two fixation systems commonly applied in spinal practice.  
Methods: The operation was performed on a squamous cell carcinoma (Pancoast tumour) of the right lung, infiltrating T2, T3, and T4 vertebrae, through a T4 lateral thoracotomy. Posterior instrumentation with transpedicular screws T1–3–5 on the left and T1–5 on the right was followed by right upper lobectomy and hemivertebrectomy. The laminae and facet joints of T2-T4 vertebrae were removed on the tumour side. An osteotomy was performed medial to the pedicle at the lateral aspect of the dural sac. Proximal parts of four adjacent ribs were removed to allow radical en bloc resection with tumour-free margins. The distal ends of four rib plates (MatrixRib Precontoured Plate system) were attached to the proximal ends of the ribs, and the proximal ends of the plates were then secured to the posterior fixation construct using a flexible polyethylene terephthalate (PeT) band of the SILC™ fixation system. The other end of the PeT band was passed through the top-loading clamp and attached to the rod of the posterior fixation.  
Results: The patient did not require additional procedures for chest wall reconstruction. Seven months postoperatively, chest CT showed satisfactory expansion of the remaining lung tissue with proper spinal alignment and anatomical shape of the rib cage.  
Conclusions: The reported technique is straightforward, safe, and effective, providing good cosmetic and functional outcomes for chest wall reconstruction in cases involving total or subtotal vertebrectomy with rib cage resection.

Keywords: En bloc resection, Pancoast tumour, Chest wall reconstruction, Rib plates

## Introduction

According to many authors, prognosis in patients diagnosed with Pancoast tumour depends mainly on the T stage of the tumour, response to preoperative chemo-radiotherapy, and completeness of resection [1–6]. The surgical treatment itself is associated with 5% mortality and 7–38% morbidity rates [4]. The overall 5-year survival for R0 resections appears to be as good as 60% (54–77%) [2, 4]. The main pattern of recurrence is distant metastases, especially in the brain. Invasion of the vertebral column is not a contraindication for surgery, which should be performed en bloc with pulmonary parenchyma resection [5, 7–12].

The technical aspects of spinal reconstruction following en bloc resection are well described and established [3, 5, 10, 13–15]. Surprisingly, there are only a few reports describing in detail the methods of rib cage repair after radical excision [12, 16]. None of these papers describe the method of chest wall reconstruction after an extensive resection of a Pancoast tumour, including vertebrectomy and excision of the proximal part of the ribs. We present our own technique, which is novel, relatively easy to apply, and effective.

## Materials and methods

### Case presentation

A 66-year-old fit ex-smoker with a history of asbestos exposure and long-standing symptoms, including coughing with occasional blood, breathlessness, and 2–3 months of dull back pain, presented with a mass in the right upper lobe infiltrating the vertebral bodies of T2, T3, and T4 (Fig. 1a). Biopsy confirmed squamous cell carcinoma, meeting the topographical criteria for Pancoast tumour [17].

### Surgical technique

The first stage of the operation involved posterior instrumentation of T1-T3-T5 on the left side. On the right side, transpedicular screws were inserted into T1 and T5 vertebrae without the rod.  

A right posterior lateral thoracotomy was performed with the patient in the prone position. The table was tilted about 45° to enter the pleural cavity through the 4th intercostal space. The tumour was attached to the anterior surfaces of T2, T3, and T4. The upper lobe was dissected, and the spinal team then took over. Hemivertebrectomy T2-T4 was performed similarly to Jain et al.'s method [15]. The laminae and facet joints were removed on the tumour side. An osteotomy was performed medial to the pedicle at the lateral aspect of the dural sac. Proximal parts of three adjacent ribs were removed, allowing radical en bloc resection with free margins (Fig. 2). Anterior column support was provided with an autologous rib graft (5th rib) fixed with screws to the remaining vertebral bodies (Fig. 3a). Posterior fixation was accomplished with the insertion of a titanium rod on the right side.  

Reconstruction was performed using the author's method. Rib plates (MatrixRib Precontoured Plate system; Synthes®, USA) and the SILC™ fixation system (Globus Medical, Inc., USA) were used. A plate of appropriate length was chosen to ensure at least two screws on the proximal end of the rib. The plate was shaped to the contour of the rib. Two holes were drilled at the end, and locking screws were used to secure the plate to the resected rib end. The proximal end of the plate was attached to the rod of posterior fixation using a flexible polyethylene terephthalate (PeT) band from the SILC™ fixation system, passing through the most proximal hole of the Matrix plate. The other end of the PeT band was passed through the top-loading clamp and attached to the rod of posterior fixation.

### Results

Histopathological examination confirmed the initial diagnosis of squamous cell carcinoma. All margins of the resected specimen were negative for neoplastic cells. The patient was treated in the intensive therapy unit for 3 weeks and discharged after 8 weeks. At the 8-month follow-up, he was fully mobile and independent, with no clinical or radiological signs of recurrence. He experienced pain in the right arm and scapular region, which was well controlled with 200 mg of Gabapentin daily.  

On the 7-month follow-up chest X-ray, satisfactory postoperative results were observed, with the remaining lung tissue well-expanded, proper anatomical shape of the rib cage, and good spinal alignment (Fig. 1b).

### Discussion

Since the late 19th century, when Parham first described chest wall resection and reconstruction, several advances in techniques have been made [20]. Despite these advances, full-thickness chest wall defects remain complex and controversial to repair [19]. The widely accepted principle is that when a flail segment of the chest wall is greater than 5 cm, ventilation becomes inefficient. In patients with two or more rib segments lost, surgical stabilization has been shown to reduce mechanical ventilator days, improve long-term outcomes, and lower hospitalization costs [21, 22].

The introduction of alloplastic materials has been a major advancement in chest wall reconstruction [20]. The MatrixRib Precontoured Plate system is one such solution, using plates and screws to bridge defects and provide support following resection [18, 23, 24]. However, in our case, it was insufficient after hemivertebrectomy, as we lacked a proximal fixation point. Initially, we considered using a simple wire, but this was deemed suboptimal due to the risk of fatigue failure. Therefore, we decided to use the SILC™ fixation system, designed for multidimensional spinal deformities. Its band is typically applied on the lamina or both the lamina and transverse process, with the top-loading clamp attached to the rod. It allows standard reduction maneuvers without the need for pedicle purchase. This system allowed us to securely and smoothly connect the rib plate to the fixation system, providing a cost-effective and reproducible solution.

The technique we describe is straightforward, reproducible, and cost-effective. It can be an effective addition to the surgical armamentarium for the management of locally advanced lung and chest wall tumours.
