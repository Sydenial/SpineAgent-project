# Multiple melanocytoma of the thoracic spine: a case report and literature review

## Abstract

### Background Context
Primary melanocytic neoplasms of the central nervous system (CNS) are rare tumors that present in diffuse or localized form; multiple localized melanocytomas are even rarer.

### Purpose
To report an unusual case of multiple melanocytoma of the thoracic spine and discuss the diagnosis and treatment of primary CNS melanocytoma.

### Study Design
A clinical case report and review of the literature.

### Methods
Chart review and analysis of the relevant literature.

### Results
A 60-year-old man presented to our neurosurgery department with progressive truncal numbness spanning three months. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thoracic spine revealed two intradural extramedullary masses at the T1 and T3–4 levels, respectively. Gross total resection of the lesions was performed. Postoperative positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET-CT) and MRI revealed no residual tumor or recurrence.

### Conclusions
We report a case of multiple primary spinal cord melanocytomas located at the T1 and T3–4 levels.

## Introduction

Primary melanocytic neoplasms of the central nervous system (CNS) are rare tumors that present in diffuse or localized form; multiple localized lesions are even rarer [1]. Here, we report a case of multiple primary thoracic spine melanocytoma and discuss the preoperative diagnosis and treatment of primary CNS melanocytoma.

## Case Report

A 60-year-old man was admitted with progressive truncal numbness spanning three months. Physical examination revealed no evidence of cutaneous melanoma. MRI of the thoracic spine revealed two intradural extramedullary lesions at the T1 and T3–4 levels, respectively. Both lesions were hyperintense on T1-weighted images and hypointense on T2-weighted images, and showed homogeneous enhancement following the administration of contrast agent.

The patient underwent T1–4 thoracic laminectomy for symptomatic improvement. Intraoperatively, the dural, leptomeningeal, and arachnoid layers were partly dark-stained. The neoplasms were dark and closely adhered to the dura. Following gross total resection of both masses, the dura closely adhered to the masses was coagulated. Some of the dark-stained dura and the leptomeningeal layer that could be adequately exposed were coagulated using bipolar coagulation to prevent recurrence.

Histopathological examination suggested the diagnosis of intermediate-grade melanocytoma. Light microscopic examination revealed tumor cells with intracytoplasmic brownish pigment and well-delineated nuclei. The mitotic count was 2 per 10 high-powered fields. Immunohistochemical staining revealed that the lesion was positive for S-100 and Ki-67 but negative for epithelial membrane antigen (EMA) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP).

Postoperatively, the truncal numbness was markedly relieved, but the patient refused radiotherapy. He was discharged a few days later and resumed normal activities. PET-CT conducted four months later confirmed the diagnosis of primary CNS neoplasm and showed that the lesions had been completely resected. Follow-up MRI revealed no residual tumor or recurrence 19 months after surgery.

## Discussion

Primary melanocytic neoplasms of the CNS arise from leptomeningeal melanocytes and can be diffuse or localized. This group consists of a spectrum ranging from well-differentiated melanocytoma to its overtly malignant counterpart, melanoma [2]. Liubinas et al. reviewed the English literature on primary melanocytic neoplasms of the CNS and found that multiple lesions are very rare [1]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the fourth report in English of multiple localized melanocytoma.

Meningeal melanocytoma presents with focal neurological signs associated with lesion location, intracranial hypertension or hemorrhage, neuropsychiatric symptoms, spinal cord compression, or seizures. Lesions most commonly occur in the cervical and thoracic spine as intradural extramedullary masses [1].

Primary melanocytic lesions have characteristic neuroimaging findings: hyperintensity on T1-weighted images and hypointensity on T2-weighted images, which may be attributable to the paramagnetic effects of free radicals within melanin. In the present case, both lesions demonstrated these imaging features and enhanced homogeneously after contrast administration. Therefore, when physicians encounter a neoplasm that is hyperintense on T1 and hypointense on T2, whether solitary or multiple, primary CNS melanocytic lesions should be considered.

Melanocytomas are low-grade neoplasms that are potentially curable by surgical resection, whereas melanomas are aggressive tumors with variable prognoses depending on tumor site and extent of resection. Even benign melanocytomas may follow an aggressive course, with recurrence possible after apparently complete excision. Therefore, adjuvant radiotherapy has been advocated after both complete and incomplete resection [1].

In the present case, gross total resection was achieved for both neoplasms. The dark-stained dura and leptomeningeal layers were extensively coagulated; however, the patient refused postoperative radiotherapy. No recurrence was detected 19 months after surgery. We believe that, whether lesions are solitary or multiple, complete resection should be the first choice of treatment. The optimal management of dark-stained dural and leptomeningeal layers remains uncertain.

## Conclusions

Multiple primary CNS melanocytomas are extremely rare and poorly documented. We report a case of multiple primary spinal cord melanocytomas located at the T1 and T3–4 levels, with favorable outcome following gross total resection.
