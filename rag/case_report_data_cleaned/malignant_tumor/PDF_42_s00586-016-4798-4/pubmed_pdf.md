# A Unique Procedure of Joined Transoral and Retropharyngeal High Cervical Approach (JTRC) Without Mandibulectomy for Treating Upper Cervical Neoplasm Involving Both C2 and C3

## Abstract

**Purpose** To investigate a unique procedure of joined transoral and retropharyngeal high cervical approach (JTRC) without mandibulectomy for treating upper cervical neoplasm involving both C2 and C3.

**Methods** A 23-year-old male patient had a neoplasm involving C2 and C3 that caused a pathologic fracture of C2 and bony destruction of C3. Neoplasm excision and cervical spine reconstruction were performed through the JTRC approach without splitting the mandible. In this approach, two surgical windows could be applied alternately by closing or opening the mouth to gain ideal exposure. By rotating the mandible, maximized exposure could be achieved for either window. In addition, an overlap zone between the transoral approach and the high cervical retropharyngeal approach could eliminate the blind area behind the mandible.

**Results** All procedures were successfully performed; the surgical incisions healed without infection. By operating alternately between the two surgical windows, neoplasm excision and cervical spine reconstruction involving both C2 and C3 were completed. Pathologic results showed metastatic renal cell cancer to the resected cervical tumor and confirmed the patient’s diagnosis of tuberous sclerosis (Bourneville disease). Three-month postoperative cervical spine radiography and CT scan demonstrated favorable placement of the bone implant and favorable instrumentation.

**Conclusion** The JTRC approach could provide good surgical exposure for treating disorders involving both upper and lower cervical spine without splitting the mandible.

**Keywords** Standard transoral approach (STOA); Transmandible approach by mandible splitting (TMA); Retropharyngeal high cervical approach (RHC); Joined transoral and retropharyngeal high cervical approach (JTRC)

## Introduction

In patients with disorders involving the craniocervical junction or upper cervical spine, the standard transoral approach (STOA) can expose lesions from the lower part of the clivus to the C2/3 disk [1, 2], providing direct access for tumor resection or screw insertion. However, in cases involving C3 or more distal cervical vertebrae, the common transoral approach may fail to provide sufficient exposure because of anatomic limitations from the tongue and mandible.

An expanded transoral approach—transmandible combined with anterior cervical approach by mandible splitting (TMA)—can provide more extensive exposure from the clivus to the lower cervical spine compared with the standard transoral approach and has been used to surgically treat patients with osseous metastases involving both upper and lower cervical spine [3–6]. However, this technically demanding approach is associated with postoperative complications including mandibular osteotomy nonunion, occlusal disturbance, sensory disturbance, and carotid artery thrombosis. Additionally, morbidity related to the large surgical wound has limited its wider clinical use.

Here, we introduce a method for addressing pathologies involving both upper and lower cervical spine without splitting the mandible. We named it the “joined transoral and retropharyngeal high cervical (JTRC) approach,” in which the mandible is kept intact during osseous metastasis resection and reconstruction.

## Case Report

A 23-year-old male patient presented with severe neck pain. Neurological examination revealed no paresis, normal deep tendon reflexes, and full power in both upper and lower extremities. Neck CT scan (Fig. 1) revealed destructive osseous lytic lesions in C2 and C3: a pathologic fracture crossed the base of the odontoid and extended into the C2 vertebra; another small round lytic lesion was located in the posterior C3 vertebral body. The posterior cortex of C2 had been destroyed. Additionally, there were sclerotic metastases in the C4 vertebral body and spinal processes of C2, C3, and C4.

T2-weighted MRI of the cervical spine in the sagittal plane (Fig. 2) demonstrated a neoplasm involving both C2 and C3 vertebral bodies. C2 had collapsed due to pathologic fracture and expanded circumferentially, with a long T2 signal anterior to C2–4 indicating hemorrhage. Axial T2 imaging showed a mass lesion destroying the body of the axis and invading the spinal canal, contacting the thecal sac without compression. Bilateral vertebral arteries were not invaded by the metastatic lesion and passed through the groove normally.

Additional abdominal MRI revealed a retroperitoneal tumor that appeared to originate from the left kidney. Fine-needle biopsy of the cervical spine lesion and the retroperitoneal mass demonstrated vascular leiomyoma with low-grade malignancy. Thus, the patient was diagnosed with tuberous sclerosis (Bourneville disease) associated with a pathologic fracture of C2.

In view of the low-grade malignancy possibility of the cervical lesions and the anatomic complexity of the upper cervical region, en bloc resection was not selected. We decided to perform piecemeal excision and anterior autograft bone implantation with plate fixation combined with posterior instrumentation and fusion.

### Surgical Procedures

Our surgical procedures included: (1) removal of the ventral lesion and reconstruction of cervical spine stability via an anterior approach; and (2) occipitocervical fusion via a posterior approach.

The patient was placed supine. Nasal intubation was performed after induction of general anesthesia. The surgical field was prepared with 10% povidone iodine and sterile drapes. A Codman retractor was used to expose the oral cavity, with a fiberoptic light to ensure visualization of the posterior pharyngeal wall. A straight incision was made along the midline of the pharyngeal wall. Monopolar electrocautery was used to strip the bilateral longus colli to expose the anterior arch of C1 and the anterior part of C2.

The axis was invaded by tumor and bulged into surrounding soft tissue. A pathologic fracture was visible at the base of the odontoid. A rongeur was used to remove the tumor piecemeal, with special attention to avoid damaging the vertebral artery. The anterior arch of the atlas was kept intact, while the axis body, most of the dens, and the C2/3 disk were resected thoroughly under the transoral surgical window. However, C3 was hindered by the tongue and mandible and was out of reach via this approach.

Subsequently, the Codman retractor was removed and the patient’s mouth was closed. A high cervical retropharyngeal incision was made on the right side of the neck to create a second operative window for exposure of C2–5.

A long straight incision along the sternocleidomastoid muscle was made, and the platysma was dissected. A gap between the carotid artery sheath and the esophagus-trachea sheath was identified and retracted outward, allowing access to C3 and C4. After ligation of the superior thyroid artery and lingual artery, the circumglossal soft tissue was retracted upward as far as possible with a retractor or periosteal elevator to access the C2/3 gap. The hypoglossal nerve, lingual nerve, esophagus, and carotid artery were carefully protected. The remaining tumor in C3 was then removed thoroughly (Fig. 3).

After tumor removal, an appropriately shaped iliac crest bone graft was harvested and implanted into the excision cavity, spanning from the anterior arch of C1 to the C4 body (Fig. 4). A suitably sized titanium plate was selected for fixation. After the anterior plate was applied and secured through the high cervical window, two locking screws were inserted into the anterior arch of C1 and the body of C4 via different surgical windows, respectively (Fig. 5). These procedures allowed instrumentation from C1 to C4 while avoiding the extensive wound associated with mandible splitting (TMA).

The pharyngeal wall incision was closed in two layers, and the high cervical incision was sutured after placement of a drainage tube. After vertebrectomy of both C2 and C3, concern remained regarding mechanical insufficiency with anterior instrumentation alone. The patient was then turned prone, and posterior instrumentation and fusion were added.

## Results

All procedures were successfully performed, and the patient was sent to intensive care for 24 h. After extubation, the patient was transferred to the general ward. The patient received 7 days of nasogastric tube feeding without complications. Surgical incisions healed without infection. On postoperative day 10, the patient began a rehabilitation program.

Pathologic examination of the resected cervical tumor showed metastatic renal cancer and confirmed the diagnosis of tuberous sclerosis (Bourneville disease). The patient then received rapamycin for further treatment. He was discharged on postoperative day 14 with a rigid cervical collar. Postoperative cervical spine radiography and CT scan demonstrated favorable placement of the bone implant and instrumentation. At 3-month follow-up, there was no displacement or loosening on CT or radiography (Fig. 6), and the patient returned to work.

## Discussion

For lesions in the upper cervical region, three anterior surgical approaches are commonly considered:

1. **Standard transoral approach (STOA)** is commonly chosen for ventral lesions between the clivus and upper cervical vertebrae. It provides direct access, allowing decompression of the ventral medulla and upper cervical spinal cord. It can also allow neoplasm resection, release and/or reduction for atlantoaxial facet joints, and even atlantoaxial fixation with a plate [7–9]. However, for lesions extending into the lower cervical spine, it may not provide adequate exposure because of mandibular constraint.

2. **Retropharyngeal high cervical approach (RHC)** is recognized as an extension of the common anterior cervical approach and can be used for removal of lesions involving the atlas and axis [10, 11]. Compared to the transoral approach, it is less likely to be complicated by infection. However, it has limitations including awkward trajectory, restricted depth of exposure, and indirect midline access. When the mandible is small or positioned low, it may hinder complex upper cervical procedures.

3. **Transmandible combined with anterior cervical approach by mandible splitting (TMA)** enlarges the transoral exposure to reach the lower cervical spine and is used for ventral lesions involving both upper and lower cervical spine [12–15]. Although exposure can be excellent, it is not widely accepted due to a high incidence of complications including dysphagia, dysphonia, dysgeusia, nonunion of the split mandible, and cosmetic concerns. Patients often require prolonged nasogastric feeding until mandibular fusion occurs, with reduced quality of life.

Accordingly, we explored a method to achieve extensive exposure for both upper and lower cervical spine without splitting the mandible, thereby avoiding complications associated with TMA. The JTRC approach maintains an intact mandible while enabling neoplasm excision and reconstruction similar in intent to TMA.

In our procedure, two operative windows were established: a transoral window and a high cervical retropharyngeal window. The surgical work could be shifted between windows as needed to obtain ideal exposure. Although a relative blind area may appear to exist under the mandible, it can be avoided by alternating between the two windows. When operating within the mouth, the mandible opens downward; when switching to the high cervical window, the mouth is closed and the mandible can be pushed upward, functioning like a door rotating around the temporomandibular joints. This mechanism maximizes exposure for either window. An overlap zone accessible from both windows (Fig. 7) reduces the likelihood of a blind area between them; although the overlap may resemble a tunnel, it can be adequate for procedures.

During work through the high cervical window, the superior thyroid artery and lingual artery require ligation. The hypoglossal nerve, lingual nerve, esophagus, and carotid artery require careful protection. Deliberate exposure of nerves is not necessarily required; the carotid artery and esophagus can be gently retracted laterally to provide sufficient working space.

In the overlap zone, the trajectory remains somewhat awkward compared with mandible-splitting approaches. When neoplasm invades surrounding soft tissue extensively, the JTRC approach may be suboptimal for en bloc resection, or when free-flap closure of the pharyngeal wall is required, representing a limitation.

## Conclusion

The JTRC approach offers potential advantages including smaller incisions and less invasiveness than TMA, improved cosmetic outcomes due to the absence of facial scars, and better postoperative quality of life with faster return to normal eating. Contraindications may include mouth-opening disability, restricted neck extension, poor mandibular condition (small size or hypoplasia), and oral infection.

Our preliminary experience suggests that the JTRC approach may provide good surgical exposure for treating disorders involving both upper and lower cervical vertebrae without splitting the mandible. With ongoing development of endoscopic techniques, if combined with endoscopic assistance, JTRC may become a more minimally invasive and convenient option for treating various pathological conditions affecting the craniovertebral junction.
