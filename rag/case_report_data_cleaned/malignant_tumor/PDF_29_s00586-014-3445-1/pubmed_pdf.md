# Atypical teratoid rhabdoid tumour of the spine: report of a case and literature review  

## Abstract

Atypical teratoid rhabdoid tumour (ATRT) is a rare and highly aggressive malignant neoplasm of the central nervous system (CNS), which occurs predominantly in children less than 2 years of age. There are less than 50 cases described in adults. We report a case of primary spinal ATRT in a 65-year-old male who presented to us with cauda equina syndrome. To the best of our knowledge, our patient is the (1) second oldest patient to be diagnosed with ATRT and only the third case of adult spinal ATRT reported in the literature; (2) first reported case of CNS ATRT occurring in a patient with non-rhabdoid renal cancer; (3) first adult patient of ATRT to present with cauda equina syndrome.

**Keywords**: Atypical teratoid rhabdoid tumour · Intradural extramedullary tumour · INI 1 protein · Leptomeningeal dissemination · Cauda equina syndrome

## Case history

A 65-year-old Caucasian male presented with a 5-week history of gradually progressive pain and weakness in the legs and 1-day history of painless urinary retention. His medical history included a right-sided nephrectomy for renal carcinoma 10 years ago and ankylosing spondylitis. He had seen a rheumatologist 3 weeks ago for his symptoms and was started on steroids as his symptoms were attributed to progression of ankylosing spondylitis. On examination, he had 4/5 power in the right lower limb and 3/5 in the left lower limb, with perianal numbness. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the whole spine showed a well-defined, diffusely enhancing intradural extramedullary mass at the T12 vertebral level. The lesion appeared isointense on T1 and hypointense on T2-weighted sequences. The differential diagnosis included either a benign lesion such as meningioma or metastasis, given the history of renal carcinoma. Computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, pelvis, and MRI/magnetic resonance angiography (MRI/MRA) of the renal visceral system showed no abnormality.

The patient underwent T11-L1 laminectomy and subtotal resection of the intradural extramedullary tumour. Intraoperatively, the tumour was found to be infiltrating the nerve roots of the left side and mixed with the cauda equina. The postoperative period was uneventful. At the time of discharge to the rehabilitation unit, the patient had power 4/5 in both legs, except for a left foot drop (power 2/5), and was self-catheterizing. Postoperatively, he received external beam radiotherapy as adjuvant treatment (1.67 gray/fraction; 5 fractions/week; 30 fractions in total). He re-presented to our department 1.5 years later with increasing back pain and leg weakness. MRI of the spine showed a recurrence at T4 for which he underwent T4/T5 laminotomy and resection of the intradural extramedullary tumour. He again received adjuvant radiotherapy postoperatively. He re-presented again after 5 months with worsening confusion and mobility decline. On examination, his Glasgow Coma Score (GCS) was E3V4M6. CT of the head showed enlarged ventricles with periventricular lucency, but MRI of the brain did not show leptomeningeal dissemination. He had a ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt inserted to manage his hydrocephalus; however, post-shunt, he continued to be confused. Repeat MRI of the spine showed tumour involvement of his sacrum and pelvis. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) obtained from the shunt reservoir showed atypical cells similar in morphology to the original spinal tumour, indicating infiltration by malignant tumour cells. The patient was referred for palliative treatment and succumbed to the disease a month later.

## Neuropathology

### Macroscopic appearance

Fragments of light and dark brown slightly firm tissue were observed.

### Microscopic appearance

The tumour consisted of sheets of pleomorphic large cells with rhabdoid and epithelial features. Cells had well-defined cell membranes, abundant eosinophilic and clear cytoplasm, and large oval to round vesicular nuclei with prominent nucleoli. Mitoses were common. No necrosis, whorl formation, psammoma bodies, or papillary architecture was seen. Under electron microscopy, intermediate filaments were present within the cytoplasm with some vacuoles, similar to myofilaments in thickness and arrangement. There was no basement membrane around the cells and no desmosomes. Differential diagnoses included: (1) metastatic carcinoma, especially given the history of renal cell carcinoma; (2) rhabdoid meningioma; (3) ATRT; (4) rhabdomyosarcoma of the meninges; (5) unusual lymphoid tumour. Other less likely differentials were: (1) melanoma; (2) angiosarcoma or proximal epithelioid sarcoma; (3) plasmacytoma; (4) metastatic adrenocortical carcinoma; (5) epithelioid malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour.

### Immunohistochemistry

Vimentin was strongly positive in the majority of tumour cells. Epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), cell adhesion molecule (CAM), pancytokeratin (PanCK), and melanoma antigen (Melan-A) were positive in scattered cells. Staining for renal cell carcinoma (RCC), cytokeratin 7 and 20 (CK7, CK20), human melanoma black (HMB-45), S-100, smooth muscle actin (SMA), CD10, and desmin were negative.

### Cytogenetics report

Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) and other chromosomal studies did not reveal any deletions of either 1p36 or 22q. INI1 protein expression was present in the original tumour from the kidney, but there was loss of INI1 protein expression in the current sample.

The current resected sample was compared to previous renal cell carcinoma. The renal cell carcinoma was a papillary carcinoma of low to intermediate grade, whereas the current tumour showed no evidence of papillary configuration. The tumour cells in the current sample were very different from those seen in the previous renal carcinoma, in that the nuclei were large with prominent nucleoli and large accumulations of eosinophilic material in the cytoplasm, with no rhabdoid cells in the renal carcinoma. The nephrectomy for renal carcinoma was almost 10 years ago, and while late metastases from renal carcinoma are a well-described phenomenon in low- and intermediate-grade tumours, the metastasis almost invariably resembles the primary tumour morphologically, which was not the case in this patient. Despite some cells in the current sample being positive for pancytokeratin markers (MNF and CAM), the results for individual keratins were negative. The pattern of positivity for MNF and CAM was unusual for de-differentiated epithelial tumours with rhabdoid features. The eosinophilic inclusions in this case were also cytokeratin negative. Furthermore, epithelial tumours that are sufficiently de-differentiated to show rhabdoid change are usually highly proliferative, but immunostaining for Ki-67 showed that this was not the case here. There was non-specific positivity for EMA, with only focal membrane-associated specific positivity. Rhabdoid meningioma was ruled out as morphology and immunostaining results did not support this diagnosis. Desmin was negative, ruling out rhabdomyosarcoma. CD31 and CD34 were negative, making the diagnosis of angiosarcoma or proximal epithelioid sarcoma unlikely. Melanoma was considered, but immunostaining for S-100 and HMB-45 were negative. The absence of any deep extradural soft tissue tumour made the diagnosis of proximal epithelioid sarcoma with INI1 loss unlikely. Plasmacytoma was considered, but CD138, CD45, and CD79a were negative. Immunostaining for inhibin was negative, ruling out adrenal carcinoma. Epithelioid malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour with INI1 loss was ruled out because of morphology and the complete lack of S-100 staining.

Given the histological, immunohistochemistry, and loss of INI1 protein expression in the current sample, the diagnosis was confirmed as primary spinal ATRT despite no evidence of chromosome 22q deletion.

## Discussion

ATRT is a rare and highly aggressive malignant neoplasm of the CNS, which occurs predominantly in children under 2 years of age. It constitutes 2-3% of all paediatric brain tumours and around 10% of all CNS tumours in infants. ATRT was first documented by Beckwith and Palmer in 1978, and in 1981, Haas et al. coined the term malignant rhabdoid tumour to describe a variant of Wilms tumour, which resembled rhabdomyosarcoma. Intracranial disease was first described by Lefkowitz et al. in 1987, and in 1996, Rorke et al. defined ATRT as a distinct CNS neoplasm. The term "teratoid" was used as the tumour usually contains different cell types, and the term "rhabdoid" refers to the similarity in cell morphology to rhabdomyosarcoma. However, there is still debate about the tissue of origin of ATRT.

In the paediatric population, ATRT occurs most commonly in the posterior fossa (61%), followed by the cerebral hemispheres (20%), suprasellar and third ventricular regions (5%), pineal region (5%), and spinal cord (1%). It can be multifocal at presentation (5%). In adults, ATRT is most commonly located in the cerebral hemispheres (53%), followed by the sella (17%), cerebellum (13%), and spinal cord (7%). It may even involve the cranial vault or show extracranial extension. The main causes of death in ATRT patients are tumour recurrence or leptomeningeal dissemination (LMD), with median survival being 21 months in adults compared to 6-16.75 months in the paediatric population. Most studies describe the rate of LMD at diagnosis as being around 20-40%, though some studies report a lower rate of 10-15%. ATRTs are polyphenotypic tumours and may be positive for vimentin, EMA, SMA, synaptophysin, CD99, GFAP, S-100, and cytokeratin. They are typically negative for desmin and germ cell markers such as human chorionic gonadotropin and alpha-fetoprotein.

Our case highlights the fact that because of the rarity of this condition, it is rarely mentioned in neuroradiologists' differential diagnoses. Additionally, the clinical presentation and radiological appearance of ATRT can be non-specific, making it necessary to correlate clinical findings with histopathology, immunohistochemistry, and cytogenetics for accurate diagnosis. Given its rarity, ATRT should be considered in the differential diagnosis of enhancing lesions causing spinal cord compression.

## Conclusion

We present a case of adult spinal ATRT, highlighting the challenges in diagnosing this rare tumour due to its rarity, age of presentation, and prior history of non-rhabdoid renal carcinoma. Our case emphasizes the need for multidisciplinary approaches to diagnosis and treatment, particularly in rare conditions like ATRT.
