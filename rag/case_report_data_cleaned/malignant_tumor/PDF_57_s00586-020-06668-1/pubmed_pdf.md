# Posterior Unilateral Exposure and Stability Reconstruction with Pedicle and Lamina Screw Fixation for the Cervical Dumbbell Tumorectomy: A Case Report and Biomechanical Study

Wei Ji, Yongquan Cheng, Qingan Hu, Hiping Huang, Junyu Yin, Deong Yang, Ruoting Ding, Mingui Bao, Jianting Chen, Hui Jiang

Received: 5 April 2020 / Revised: 6 November 2020 / Accepted: 11 November 2020 © Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2020

## Abstract

**Purpose**: Cervical dumbbell tumor is usually removed via a posterior approach and may require spinal fixation. This study describes a new technique of unilateral exposure and stability reconstruction with pedicle and lamina screws fixation for posterior cervical dumbbell tumorectomy and compares it with conventional techniques.

**Methods**: Posterior unilateral exposure, hemi-laminectomy, and facetectomy were performed in one patient with a cervical dumbbell tumor between C3 and C4. The stability was reconstructed by unilateral pedicle and lamina screw fixation (UPLS), and a strip of shaped allograft bone was implanted between the superior and inferior lateral masses. Biomechanical stability tests were performed using seven fresh-frozen human cervical spine specimens (C4–C7) and compared with unilateral pedicle screw (UPS) and bilateral pedicle screw fixation (BPS) techniques. A continuous pure moment of ±2.0 Nm was applied to the specimen in flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation.

**Results**: The cervical dumbbell tumor was completely removed, and bone fusion with continuous bone trabecula was maintained in the patient at 18 months postoperatively. Biomechanical stability tests showed that the range of motion of the UPLS fixation plus graft bone implant was similar to the BPS fixation in flexion (1.8° vs. 1.5°, p=0.58) and extension (2.3° vs. 2.2°, p=0.73), but significantly larger in lateral bending (3.9° vs. 1.0°, p<0.001) and axial rotation (6.8° vs. 3.8°, p=0.002), and smaller than the UPS fixation in all directions (all p<0.001).

**Conclusions**: For the treatment of cervical dumbbell tumor, posterior unilateral exposure and stability reconstruction with pedicle and lamina screw fixation after hemi-laminectomy and facetectomy provides a more stable and less traumatic technique.

**Level of Evidence**: Diagnostic: individual cross-sectional studies with consistently applied reference standards and blinding.

**Keywords**: Dumbbell tumor, Cervical spine, Mechanical testing, Biomechanical stability

## Introduction

Spinal dumbbell tumors, first described as intra/extraspinal tumors of neural origin, usually grow across the bony neural foramen and the dura of the nerve root sleeve. They account for 13–17% of spinal cord tumors, with the majority being schwannomas, frequently located in the cervical spine. Complete resection of the cervical dumbbell tumor is necessary for the maximal reduction in neurological symptoms and minimal neurological deficits after surgery.

However, spinal deformity occurs in up to 18% of adult patients after laminectomy, a rate that increases when combined with facetectomy. Therefore, spinal fixation and fusion are recommended when more than 50% of the facet joint is resected. In cases with pre-existing bone erosion or destruction, fusion with instrumentation is required to prevent complications like pain, deformity, or loss of neurological function. The two primary methods for stability reconstruction following cervical dumbbell tumor resection are:
1. Ipsilateral pedicle screws/lateral mass screws and rod/plate fixation, which carries a higher risk of instability.
2. Bilateral pedicle screws/lateral mass screws and rod fixation, which involves larger soft tissue exposure and bony defect.

This study introduces a new technique for cervical dumbbell tumorectomy, involving posterior unilateral exposure and stability reconstruction with pedicle and lamina screw fixation combined with graft bone implantation to restore spinal stability. We hypothesize that this method provides good biomechanical stability with less trauma compared to conventional methods.

## Materials and Methods

### Case

A 50-year-old woman presented with neck pain and left-sided numbness for three years. Preoperative contrast-enhanced MRI images revealed a crumbly tumor at the C3, C4 level, extending into the vertebral body and both the intra- and extradural regions. Coronal CT imaging showed the tumor involving the C3 and C4 vertebrae, neuro-foramina, and posterior facet joints on the left side.

The surgery was performed by two experienced surgeons under general anesthesia, with the patient positioned prone with the head in neutral alignment. A longitudinal midline incision was made, and subperiosteal dissection was performed on the affected side. The muscles were dissected from the superior to inferior vertebrae and laterally to expose the tumor. Hemi-laminectomy and unilateral facetectomy were performed at C3 and C4, and a strip of shaped allograft bone was implanted between the lateral masses of C2 and C5. Unilateral pedicle and lamina screws were placed, and the tumor tissue was sent for pathological examination.

### Biomechanical Study

Seven fresh-frozen human cadaveric cervical spine specimens (C4–T1) were tested. The donors, aged 35 to 70 years (mean age 44.3), were free from obvious neoplastic, traumatic, congenital conditions, previous surgeries, or facet arthropathy. The specimens were tested using a custom-designed spine testing machine to apply a continuous pure moment of ±2.0 Nm in flexion-extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation.

Each specimen was tested in six configurations: 
1. Intact
2. Injured
3. Unilateral pedicle screw fixation (UPS)
4. Bilateral pedicle screw fixation (BPS)
5. Unilateral pedicle and lamina screw fixation (UPLS)
6. Unilateral pedicle and lamina screw fixation combined with graft bone implant (UPLS+G)

The range of motion (ROM) and neutral zone (NZ) between C4 and C7 in flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation were measured and compared across conditions.

## Results

### Case

Postoperative pathological examination confirmed the tumor as a schwannoma. MRI and CT scans indicated complete tumor resection and adequate cervical spine reconstruction. At 18 months postoperatively, the patient's symptoms had resolved, and bone fusion was observed without instrumentation failure.

### Biomechanical Study

The ROMs of the UPS fixation were significantly larger than those of UPLS, UPLS+G, or BPS fixation conditions in all directions. The ROMs of UPLS+G fixation were the same as those of BPS fixation in flexion (1.8° vs. 1.5°, p=0.58) and extension (2.3° vs. 2.2°, p=0.73), but significantly larger in lateral bending (3.9° vs. 1.0°, p<0.001) and axial rotation (6.8° vs. 3.8°, p=0.002). The neutral zone (NZ) of C4-C7 was reduced in all fixation conditions compared to the injured state.

## Discussion

Cervical dumbbell tumors, which connect multiple regions such as intradural, epidural, and paravertebral spaces, are challenging to treat. The posterior approach is the most widely used for resection. This study highlights the effectiveness of the posterior unilateral exposure technique combined with stability reconstruction using pedicle and lamina screws, providing both good biomechanical stability and reduced surgical trauma compared to traditional methods. The graft bone further improved stability by reducing ROM in axial rotation.

This technique not only preserves contralateral bone and soft tissue but also reduces surgical time, bleeding, and postoperative complications such as axial pain. The inclusion of allograft bone contributes to long-term stability, as demonstrated by bone fusion observed in our case.

## Conclusion

For the surgical treatment of cervical dumbbell tumors, posterior unilateral exposure and stability reconstruction with pedicle and lamina screws fixation, along with graft bone implantation, appears to be a feasible and effective technique for restoring spinal stability after hemi-laminectomy and facetectomy.
