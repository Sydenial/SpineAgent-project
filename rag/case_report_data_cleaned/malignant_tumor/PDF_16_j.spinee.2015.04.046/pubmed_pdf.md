# Spinal Schwannoma Presenting Due to Torsion and Hemorrhage: Case Report and Review of Literature

## Abstract

### Background Context
The presentation of a tumor due to torsion, with hemorrhage from presumed reperfusion injury as a result of infarction of the lesion, is extremely rare and markedly different from typical tumor presentation.

### Purpose
The aim was to describe a patient with a rare case of twisted intradural nerve sheath myxoid schwannoma.

### Study Design
This was a case report and a review of the literature.

### Methods
A patient presenting with acute onset of severe pain was found to have a minimally enhanced intradural extramedullary cystic lesion. The patient underwent bilateral L2 and L3 laminectomy and microsurgically assisted intradural exploration.

### Results
At laminectomy and intradural exploration, the lesion was found to be a schwannoma that had rotated above and below its point of origin, causing torsion. The color change of the nerve ceased abruptly at the site of torsion, suggesting that the mechanism of hemorrhage in and around the schwannoma identified on pathologic evaluation was due to torsion. The torsion caused vascular insufficiency, likely venous, producing subsequent reperfusion-related hemorrhage involving the involved nerve root.

### Conclusions
This acute infarction of the tumor and the associated nerve caused an acute pain syndrome that is not commonly associated with lumbar schwannomas. Patients with acute onset of severe radiating pain may have torsion of a benign tumor arising from the affected nerve.

## Objective

Schwannomas, a type of nerve sheath tumor, make up about one-third of primary spinal tumors [1–3]. Schwannomas develop from Schwann cells and are usually benign. Local pain and radiculopathy from compression of local structures is the most common presenting syndrome; however, when tumors are large enough, motor weakness or cauda equina syndrome may occur. The presentation of a tumor due to torsion of the lesion and its attached nerve is extremely rare [4], and hemorrhage within spinal tumors is also rare [5].

The following is a rare case of an intradural nerve sheath myxoid schwannoma that twisted, resulting in torsion and hemorrhage within the lesion, causing pain and weakness in the corresponding nerve distribution.

## Clinical Presentation

A 62-year-old otherwise healthy man presented to the emergency room with extensive pain and numbness in the right L4 distribution and was unable to ambulate without assistance because of the pain. On examination, he was found to have mild weakness (4/5) of right dorsiflexion. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed an intradural lesion with cystic changes in the regions of L2 and L3 and minimal enhancement with gadolinium administration. The abrupt presentation led to a preliminary diagnosis of a tumor, such as a schwannoma or metastatic lesion, with hemorrhage, parasitic infection, or intradural focal abscess.

## Operative Findings

The patient underwent bilateral L2 and L3 laminectomy with microsurgically assisted intradural exploration. Upon exposure of the cauda equina, the lesion was identified as a reddish, round, purplish mass arising from one nerve fascicle, approximately 1.5 cm in diameter. Further dissection revealed that the proximal and distal ends of the nerve on either side of the lesion demonstrated more than one full turn of rotation in a focal manner, with associated compression of the vascular supply. There was an abrupt border at which the nerve root appearance changed within the coils of torsion, becoming profoundly darker without extension above or below this point. Provocative stimulation of the nerve failed to elicit any activation of leg muscle electromyography, bladder manometry, or anal sphincter electromyography.

After bipolar cautery was applied proximally and distally to the rotated sections of the nerve root, the root was sectioned with microscissors, and the lesion was sent in its entirety for pathologic evaluation.

## Histology

The specimen consisted of a tan-brown soft-tissue nodule measuring $1.8 \times 1.2 \times 1$ cm. Microscopic examination with hematoxylin and eosin staining demonstrated a neoplasm characterized by predominantly loose stroma with evenly distributed round to oval nuclei in a clear myxoid background, interspersed with thick-walled blood vessels and occasional more cellular foci with architecture reminiscent of palisading or Verocay bodies. There was extensive recent hemorrhage throughout the tissue but no evidence of necrosis, atypia, or mitotic activity.

The tumor was diffusely positive for S100 protein and showed weak, isolated immunoreactivity with glial fibrillary acidic protein. Reticulin staining revealed a variably intense reticulin network surrounding individual tumor cells, while trichrome staining highlighted thick vessel walls and stromal components. Alcian blue was focally positive within the myxoid matrix. Scattered lymphohistiocytic infiltrates were present. Neurofilament staining was focally positive only adjacent to the tumor within adherent peripheral nerve tissue but not within the tumor itself. Epithelial membrane antigen, chromogranin, and pancytokeratin were negative. Elastic Van Gieson staining was noncontributory. These findings were most consistent with a diagnosis of myxoid schwannoma.

## Postoperative Outcomes

Postoperatively, the patient demonstrated intact movement of all four extremities, and his preoperative pain was completely alleviated. Magnetic resonance imaging of the spine and brain with and without contrast on postoperative day 1 showed no additional lesions. At two months postoperatively, he reported no back or leg pain, with only a small amount of preexisting left knee pain. At 11-year follow-up, the patient reported complete recovery with no weakness, pain, or numbness. He was able to jog up to 6 miles without neurologic compromise and expressed high satisfaction with the surgical outcome.

## Discussion

This case represents a very rare presentation of a nerve root schwannoma causing severe pain due to torsion and presumed subsequent hemorrhage involving the affected nerve. Only one prior report has described torsion of a nerve sheath tumor [4], and one report has described hemorrhage within such a tumor [5]. This represents the second documented case of torsion with presumed infarction and hemorrhage within a cauda equina nerve root tumor resulting in acute symptoms.

The blood supply to nerves within the cauda equina runs bidirectionally between the exiting nerve sleeve and the spinal cord [6]. Typically, compression at a single point does not result in ischemia because circulation can occur from either direction. However, compression at two points can result in ischemia of the intervening segment. Venous obstruction, occurring at lower pressures than arterial obstruction, may lead to venous engorgement and hemorrhage, or complete vascular obstruction followed by partial reperfusion, resulting in reperfusion injury and hemorrhage.

Rotation of a nerve sheath tumor within the spinal canal can result in torsion of the nerve on either side of the lesion, compressing the tissue at the point of rotation and kinking the blood vessels. Venous insufficiency is likely to occur first due to lower venous pressures. The lack of enhancement with gadolinium may reflect decreased vascular supply from persistent torsion, a finding previously described in cases of acute infarction of schwannomas [7].

Physiologic pulsations within the spinal canal may contribute to focal tumor movement [8]. In the cauda equina, sufficient space exists for lesions smaller than the canal diameter to move. However, the mechanism by which the tumor becomes rotated and remains fixed is unclear. Possible explanations include a ratcheting mechanism due to surface inhomogeneity or arachnoid adhesions that allow rotation in one direction while preventing reversal. The observation that subpial hemorrhage extended only up to the point of maximal torsion suggests that tension at this site occluded further propagation along the nerve.

The severity of the patient’s pain is also not fully understood, as the sensory nerve ganglia are located distal to the lesion. Potential mechanisms include localized subpial vascular irritation, mechanical tension on the nerve, or activation of intrinsic pain fibers. Immediate pain relief following nerve section does not isolate a single cause, as tumor resection and decompression occurred simultaneously.

## Conclusions

Patients may present with acute symptoms due to torsion-induced hemorrhage within a nerve root tumor. Whether torsion or hemorrhage represents the initiating event remains unclear, but surgical removal of the lesion can result in immediate and sustained symptom relief. This entity should be considered in the differential diagnosis of patients presenting with acute symptoms from intradural tumors, particularly those arising from nerve roots within the cauda equina.
