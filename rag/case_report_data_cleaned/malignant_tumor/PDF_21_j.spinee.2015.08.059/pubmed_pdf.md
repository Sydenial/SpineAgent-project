# Primary pleomorphic liposarcoma of the thoracic epidural space: Case report

## Abstract

**Background Context:** Pleomorphic liposarcoma (PLS) is a rare malignant soft tissue tumor comprising 5%–15% of liposarcomas and is characterized by high malignant potential. To our knowledge, only three cases of this entity have been reported in the spine.  

**Purpose:** We describe the only reported case of a purely epidural PLS with no macroscopic bone involvement at diagnosis.  

**Study Design/Setting:** A case presenting clinical evidence that PLS may arise from epidural fat is reported.  

**Methods:** The clinical presentation, management, and outcome in a case of primary PLS of the thoracic spine, along with a review of the literature, are presented.  

**Results:** A 70-year-old male presented with sudden onset lower extremity weakness, constipation, and back pain. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed an epidural lesion at T5 with mass effect compressing the spinal cord and extension to the T5–T6 foramen. Urgent decompressive laminectomy with gross total resection was performed. Histopathology revealed high-grade PLS. Adjuvant radiotherapy was prescribed. The tumor recurred 3 months later. Despite repeat surgery, additional radiation, and chemotherapy, the patient developed widespread metastases and succumbed to his disease 1 year after treatment began.  

**Conclusions:** Spinal PLS is a rare entity but may arise from epidural fat and should be considered in the differential diagnosis of primary spinal cord lesions.

**Keywords:** Bone invasion; Epidural fat; Pleomorphic liposarcoma; Primary spinal tumor; Spinal cord compression; Spinal liposarcoma

## Introduction

Liposarcoma is the most common soft tissue sarcoma in adults, accounting for approximately 20% of all mesenchymal malignancies. The World Health Organization classifies liposarcomas into four histologic subtypes: myxoid, mixed, pleomorphic, and dedifferentiated [1]. Pleomorphic liposarcoma (PLS) is a high-grade sarcoma that most commonly presents in the thigh and pelvis of older adults. Approximately 25% of cases present with metastatic disease, most commonly to the lungs, liver, and skeleton [2]. PLS is the rarest subtype, comprising only 5%–15% of liposarcomas [3–5]. In patients with localized disease at diagnosis, the 5-year survival rate is approximately 60% [3,4,6–8]. Recurrent disease, absence of surgical resection, and positive resection margins are independent poor prognostic indicators [2].

Primary PLS of the spine is particularly rare, with only three cases reported in the literature [7–9]. In all previously reported cases, the tumor presented with significant involvement of the vertebral body at diagnosis. We report a primary PLS of the thoracic spine that was purely epidural, extending along the height of the T5 vertebral body into the right foramen, with no apparent bony involvement on preoperative imaging or during the initial surgery. Subsequent microscopic bone invasion was later identified on histopathologic examination.

## Case Report

A 70-year-old man presented to the Emergency Department complaining of constipation. He was initially discharged after symptomatic treatment but returned 3 days later with continued constipation, back pain, and sudden onset of progressive bilateral lower extremity weakness. Neurologic examination revealed 4/5 motor strength in the bilateral lower extremities, bilateral Babinski signs, and a sensory level at T10 (American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale [AIS] D [10]).  

Emergency magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thoracic and lumbar spine revealed a hypointense solid-cystic epidural nonenhancing mass located in the right lateral aspect of the spinal canal at T5. The lesion spanned the T4–T5 and T5–T6 disc levels, with extension into the right T5–T6 foramen. No bone involvement was evident. The tumor measured approximately 2.5 × 1 × 1 cm and caused marked spinal cord compression.

## First Surgery

The patient underwent emergency T4–T5 laminectomy. Intraoperatively, the lesion appeared as a white caseating mass originating from the epidural space and extending into the right T5–T6 foramen. No macroscopic bone involvement was observed. Gross total resection was achieved via piecemeal excision followed by T5–T6 foraminotomy. The procedure was completed without complications.

## Pathologic Diagnosis and Clinical Course

Histopathologic examination revealed high-grade pleomorphic liposarcoma. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient made a complete neurologic recovery (AIS E). MRI performed 1 month later showed no evidence of residual tumor. Positron emission tomography–computed tomography demonstrated increased uptake in the T4–T6 right paravertebral area, without evidence of systemic disease.

The patient was referred for adjuvant external beam radiation therapy but delayed treatment. Three months after the initial surgery, after receiving only one radiation treatment, he returned with progressive lower extremity weakness, urinary hesitancy, sensory loss below the nipple line, and abdominal distention. Examination revealed bilateral lower limb weakness (proximal 3/5, distal 4/5) and a sensory level at T5–T6 (AIS D). MRI demonstrated extensive recurrent epidural disease with spinal cord compression from T3 to T6 and new involvement of the T5 vertebral body.

## Second Surgery, Histopathology, and Clinical Course

The patient underwent emergency partial T5 vertebrectomy, right T4 pediculectomy, and resection of the head of the fifth rib. A white caseating mass was resected from the paraspinal space. Posterior fixation from T2 to T9 was performed, and the T5 vertebral body was reconstructed with methyl methacrylate. Postoperatively, the patient achieved partial neurologic recovery (AIS D). Histopathology again confirmed PLS, now with infiltration of native bone and areas of dedifferentiation with osteoid formation.

Despite additional radiotherapy, the patient experienced further recurrence 5 months later, presenting with urinary retention, bowel incontinence, and worsening paraparesis. MRI confirmed tumor recurrence, and he underwent emergency T7–T8 laminectomy. Histopathology demonstrated PLS with extensive necrosis, possibly treatment-related.

Nine months after the first surgery, widespread metastatic disease was detected following another episode of cord compression at T1–T2. The patient was treated with additional radiation and chemotherapy. One year after the initial diagnosis, he succumbed to metastatic disease.

## Discussion

This case represents a rare example of primary spinal PLS presenting as a purely epidural lesion without initial vertebral body involvement. PLS is known for its aggressive clinical course, with a median survival of 9.1 months in patients with metastatic disease and a 1-year disease-specific survival rate of approximately 45% [2].

Primary tumors of the spine are uncommon compared with metastatic disease, multiple myeloma, lymphoma, and other neoplastic spinal conditions [11]. Primary spinal PLS is particularly rare, with only three previously reported cases [7–9], all of which demonstrated vertebral body involvement at presentation. In contrast, our case likely originated from epidural fat, allowing for initial gross total resection and complete neurologic recovery after the first surgery.

Nevertheless, recurrence occurred rapidly, likely due to microscopic residual disease. Although surgery is rarely curative, early intervention may preserve ambulation and sphincter function. The delayed initiation of adjuvant radiation therapy in this case may have contributed to early recurrence and poor overall outcome.

PLS should be included in the differential diagnosis of tumors causing epidural spinal cord compression. Awareness of this entity allows for consideration of more aggressive surgical resection and stabilization when appropriate.

## Conclusion

Pleomorphic liposarcoma is a rare but highly aggressive malignancy that may originate from epidural fat. It should be considered in the differential diagnosis of isolated epidural spinal lesions, even in the absence of bone involvement on MRI.
