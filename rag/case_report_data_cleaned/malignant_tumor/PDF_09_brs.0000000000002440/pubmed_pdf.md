# RECOMMENCEMENT OF DENOSUMAB FOR UNRESECTABLE GIANT CELL TUMOUR OF THE CERVICAL SPINE: A CASE REPORT

## ABSTRACT
### Study Design
Case report.

### Objective
To highlight a case of giant cell tumour of bone (GCTB) with cessation of Denosumab in the management of unresectable GCTB, even in cases with prior demonstration of excellent disease control. The close proximity of unresectable GCTB to vital structures makes it prudent to monitor these patients closely given its locally aggressive nature.

### Summary of Background Data
Cervical spine GCTB is extremely rare. Unresectable GCTB has historically been difficult to treat due to the lack of prospective, randomized clinical studies to guide treatment. Radiotherapy has fallen out of favor due to the risk of malignant transformation, especially since most GCTB patients are young.

In recent years, improved understanding of the receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) in the pathophysiology of GCTB has led to the use of Denosumab in patients with recurrent, unresectable, or metastatic GCTB and in patients for whom surgical resection carries a high morbidity. To date, the optimal dosage and duration of therapy in the treatment of GCTB is unknown.

## METHODS
We report a case of cervical spine GCTB in a 53-year-old male with positive surgical margins, managed with Denosumab.

## RESULTS
This is the first report of recurrence of disease post-cessation of Denosumab despite earlier satisfactory disease control and drug intervention.

## CONCLUSION
Denosumab is an excellent option in patients with unresectable GCTB or when surgery will result in excessive morbidity. However, further studies are required to determine optimal dosing, treatment duration, side effect profile, and whether Denosumab can achieve partial or complete disease remission in the long run.

## KEYWORDS
Denosumab, optimal dosing, duration of treatment, side effects.

### Level of Evidence: 4

## INTRODUCTION
Giant cell tumour of bone (GCTB) has a peak incidence between ages 20-30. Surgery is the treatment of choice for resectable GCTB with curative radiotherapy for localized disease. While radiotherapy is contraindicated, various other treatment modalities have been used. Denosumab is highly effective in the treatment of GCTB and was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2013 for recurrent, unresectable, metastatic GCTB or for patients in whom surgery will result in excessive morbidity. However, the optimal dosage and duration of treatment with Denosumab is still unknown.

## CASE PRESENTATION
A 53-year-old Indian male presented with atraumatic acute axial neck pain without neurological symptoms to a tertiary hospital in December 2011. On examination, he had midline spinal tenderness over the mid-cervical region with full range of motion of the neck. Neurological examination was normal. X-rays showed a possible C3 pathological fracture.

Cross-sectional imaging showed a vertebral body lytic lesion extending into both pedicles, suggesting metastases.

## OPERATION
C3 anterior corpectomy with bular resection was performed in January 2012. Postoperative X-rays were performed. Intraoperatively, the tumour was unresectable due to invasion of the vertebral body. Complete resection of the tumour was not possible, and corpectomy of C3 extending to the lateral body was performed.

Subsequent CT and MR angiogram showed local extension of the residual tumour invading the right transverse foramen with partial encasement and displacement of the vertebral artery, with no compromise to the patency of the artery. Denosumab was chosen over surgical resection in view of the likelihood of injury to the vertebral artery and high risk of posterior circulation stroke.

Subcutaneous monthly Denosumab 120mg injections were commenced at 4 months postoperatively. Regular CT scans at 3-month intervals showed a good response to treatment. Denosumab injections were then reduced from monthly to bi-monthly intervals for 1 year. Repeat CT scans showed no disease progression despite dose reduction, and Denosumab was ceased after 21 months.

Interval disease progression was seen at 6 months post-cessation of Denosumab, prompting recommencement of the monthly subcutaneous 120mg Denosumab injections. A good response was again seen on subsequent CT scans.

Our patient achieved complete resolution of neck pain 3 months post-surgery and no associated adverse events.

## DISCUSSION
Denosumab is an excellent option in patients with unresectable GCTB or when surgery will result in excessive morbidity. However, the optimal dose and duration of treatment remain unclear, and there are no long-term studies to assess whether GCTB can continue to remain sensitive to Denosumab or whether its efficacy decreases over prolonged use.

Denosumab, while able to control the disease to a large extent, could not completely eliminate it, which highlights the importance of complete clearance of disease at the time of surgery. Unfortunately, in cases like ours, Denosumab remains an excellent option even if started sometime after surgery. We began treatment 4 months postoperatively and were able to achieve disease control.

Long-term effects of Denosumab in patients with normal-density bone are unknown. This is especially relevant in the treatment of GCTB as most of the patients are young. There have been reports of profound osteoporosis in ANK-deficient mice and osteoporotic-like bone in a 16-year-old girl with metastatic GCTB after 20 months of Denosumab treatment. Our case demonstrated similar efficacy of the drug despite halving the dose after the disease had stabilized. This may be one way to minimize long-term side effects post-disease stabilization.
