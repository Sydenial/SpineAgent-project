# Percutaneous Radiofrequency-Targeted Vertebral Augmentation of Unstable Metastatic C2 and C3 Lesions Using a CT-Guided Posterolateral Approach

## Abstract

Percutaneous radiofrequency-targeted C2 and C3 vertebral augmentation using a posterolateral approach and high-viscosity cement.

### Summary of Background Data

Percutaneous vertebral augmentation is a minimally invasive procedure used for stabilization and pain control in vertebral compression fractures. Its use in the cervical spine, especially the upper cervical spine, is very limited mainly due to technical challenges.

### Methods

We report the first use of an ultra–high-viscosity cement and posterolateral approach with computed tomography (CT) guidance and CT fluoroscopy in a patient with lytic lesions in C2 and C3 and a pathologic fracture of C2 for the purpose of stabilization and pain palliation.

### Results

Technically successful vertebral augmentation of the C2 and C3 vertebral bodies was achieved. There were no complications. The patient reported pain relief and improved range of motion after treatment, and the hard cervical collar was removed.

### Conclusion

Computed tomography–guided radiofrequency-targeted vertebral augmentation of the cervical spine using a posterolateral approach and ultra–high-viscosity cement is a technically feasible procedure that may be used in patients with advanced osteolytic cervical spine metastases who are not surgical candidates for the purpose of pain palliation and fracture stabilization.

## Introduction

The literature on cervical vertebral augmentation is limited and includes a few reports of vertebroplasty using anterior/anterolateral, lateral, and transoral approaches [1–3]. The posterolateral approach is a recently described method with only three reports in metastatic disease [4–6]. In addition, energy-responsive, ultra–high-viscosity cement has recently been introduced for the treatment of vertebral compression fractures and has a potential role in reducing cement leakage compared with lower viscosity cements [7,8] and in increasing uniformity of cement filling [9]. There are very few reports of its application in the thoracic and lumbar spine [10] and no reports of its use in the cervical spine.

This is the first report of radiofrequency-targeted vertebral augmentation using a posterolateral approach for the management of a pathologic fracture and pain in osteolytic lesions of the upper cervical spine.

## Case Report

Cervical spine imaging of a 55-year-old female with osseous metastases from squamous cell carcinoma of the lung demonstrated unstable lesions involving C2 and C3 with a pathologic fracture of C2. The patient’s pain was disabling and poorly controlled, and she was placed in a hard cervical collar for stabilization. She was not a surgical candidate due to the potentially significant morbidity and had received maximum radiation therapy to the spine. Vertebral augmentation was requested for stabilization of the cervical spine and pain palliation.

After obtaining consent, general anesthesia was initiated, and the patient was placed in the prone position with neutral craniocervical junction positioning on the CT table. For CT guidance, a 16-row CT scanner equipped with CT fluoroscopy was used. Fluoroscopic images were acquired using a low-dose technique and displayed in real time on an in-room monitor, facilitating efficient procedural workflow.

Using a posterolateral approach, the C2 vertebral body was accessed via the right pedicle with a 10-gauge introducer needle using a technique previously described in the thoracic and lumbar spine [10], following a trajectory that avoided critical structures including the vertebral arteries. The needles were placed under intermittent CT guidance into the midline of the C2 and C3 vertebral bodies. An osteotome was used to create channels across the midline to allow cement flow to the contralateral side, thereby avoiding a bipedicular approach.

Ultra–high-viscosity cement was prepared, and under CT fluoroscopy guidance, 5 mL of cement was injected into the C2 vertebral body, filling the large lytic lesion and the left lateral mass pathologic fracture. In the C3 vertebral body, 1 mL of cement was injected to fill a lytic lesion within the anterior aspect of the vertebral body. After cement delivery, the cement-driving cannula was removed, and the stylet was reinserted to completely empty the delivery cannula. No cement leakage was identified.

The patient was observed for 24 hours and showed no clinical complications, reporting substantial pain relief with a reduction in numeric pain rating scale score from 9/10 to 5/10. The hard cervical collar was subsequently removed.

## Discussion

The posterolateral approach has the advantage of avoiding the carotid space and anterolateral vascular structures. The spinal cord and vertebral artery can be avoided by using CT guidance and respecting the cortical boundaries of the pedicle and foramen transversarium. Contrast-enhanced CT or magnetic resonance imaging, as well as CT or magnetic resonance angiography, can be used to delineate the course of the vertebral artery. In this case, contrast-enhanced cervical spine magnetic resonance imaging was used for preprocedural planning.

The combination of CT and CT fluoroscopy provides precise guidance for needle placement. CT fluoroscopy allows near real-time monitoring of cement injection and prompt detection of cement leakage. In this case, CT fluoroscopy enabled safe delivery of a larger cement volume in C2 than previously described, which is particularly important in the cervical spine given the severe consequences of cement leakage into the spinal canal or foramen transversarium.

The radiofrequency-targeted vertebral augmentation system used allows remote-controlled hydraulic cement delivery, enabling the operator to remain up to 20 feet from the patient and thereby reducing radiation exposure [10,11]. The unipedicular approach made possible by navigational osteotome-created channels may further reduce radiation dose and invasiveness compared with a traditional bipedicular approach [11].

Ultra–high-viscosity cement contains particles of varying sizes that decelerate polymerization time. High-frequency alternating current applied immediately before injection accelerates polymerization and maintains consistently high cement viscosity during remote-controlled hydraulic injection [10]. Controlled delivery rate, targeted flow through osteotome-created channels, and higher cement viscosity reduce the likelihood of cement leakage [11].

In conclusion, minimally invasive radiofrequency-targeted vertebral augmentation of the upper cervical spine using a CT-guided posterolateral approach is technically feasible and may offer advantages in stabilization and pain palliation for patients with advanced cervical spine metastases who are not surgical candidates.

## Key Points

- Radiofrequency-targeted vertebral augmentation of the cervical spine may be an option for pain control and stabilization in patients with end-stage metastatic disease who are not surgical candidates.  
- CT guidance and CT fluoroscopy may decrease the risk of cement leakage and vascular or organ injury.  
- Rate-controlled delivery of radiofrequency-activated high-viscosity cement into channels created with a navigational osteotome may reduce cement leakage.  
- The posterolateral approach may have advantages over other techniques for cervical vertebroplasty.
