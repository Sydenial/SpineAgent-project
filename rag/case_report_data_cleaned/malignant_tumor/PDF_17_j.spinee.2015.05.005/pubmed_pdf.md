# Multiple spinal myxopapillary ependymomas presented with back pain

## Abstract

A 48-year-old male presented to our clinic with severe back pain of a few months' duration. The pain was worse at night and gradually increased in severity. Previously, he had received analgesic and myorelaxant medications, but there had been no significant improvement. There was no pain in any other joint, and there was no history of systemic disease or trauma associated with the back region. T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the cervical and thoracolumbar spine showed multiple well-defined extramedullary lesions. Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted spinal MRI also revealed enhancing masses. An excisional biopsy was performed, and cytology revealed a highly cellular neoplasm composed of small clusters of pleomorphic large squamoid cells with abundant pink cytoplasm. Given the histopathological findings, the lesion was determined to be a myxopapillary ependymoma. The patient was recommended to receive radiotherapy.

## Introduction

Multiple spinal myxopapillary ependymomas (MSE) are rare, slow-growing neoplasms that typically occur in the filum terminale and cauda equina regions. They can present with symptoms related to spinal cord compression or radiculopathy. The clinical manifestations, including back pain, are often nonspecific, making early diagnosis challenging. The aim of this paper is to describe the case of a patient who presented with back pain and was diagnosed with multiple spinal myxopapillary ependymomas.

## Methods

A 48-year-old male presented with a few months' history of severe back pain. The pain was nocturnal and progressively worsened. The patient's medical history was non-contributory with no history of trauma or systemic disease. Physical examination revealed no significant findings, and the patient was referred for imaging studies.

## Results

T2-weighted MRI of the cervical and thoracolumbar spine revealed multiple well-defined extramedullary lesions (Fig. 1A-C). Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted spinal MRI also revealed enhancing masses (Fig. 2A-C). Based on the imaging findings, an excisional biopsy was performed. Cytological examination showed a highly cellular neoplasm with small clusters of pleomorphic, large squamoid cells and abundant pink cytoplasm, confirming the diagnosis of myxopapillary ependymoma.

## Discussion

Spinal myxopapillary ependymomas are benign tumors that are typically located in the filum terminale and cauda equina regions of the spinal cord. These tumors often present with symptoms such as back pain, which can sometimes be confused with other spinal pathologies. MRI is the primary diagnostic tool, as it can detect the extramedullary location of the tumors and provide details about the enhancement patterns post-contrast administration. The histopathological features of myxopapillary ependymomas are characteristic, with a highly cellular neoplasm composed of pleomorphic cells with abundant cytoplasm.

## Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of considering spinal myxopapillary ependymomas in the differential diagnosis of back pain, especially when imaging findings suggest extramedullary lesions. Early diagnosis and appropriate management, including surgery and radiotherapy, can significantly improve patient outcomes.
