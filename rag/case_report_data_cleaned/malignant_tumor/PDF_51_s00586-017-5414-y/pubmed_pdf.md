# A rare case of concomitant cervical disc herniation and intradural meningioma treated with one-stage posterior surgery

## Abstract

**Study design:** Case report.  
**Purpose:** To present a rare case of cervical disc herniation concomitant with an intradural meningioma at the same segment, successfully treated with a single one-stage posterior surgery involving tumor resection and transdural discectomy.  
**Summary of background data:** Coexistence of symptomatic disc herniation and an intraspinal tumor in the same cervical segment is extremely rare. Typically, two-stage anterior and posterior surgeries are required to treat the two conditions, respectively. One-stage posterior surgery to treat both pathologies simultaneously has not been reported in the literature.  
**Methods and results:** A 76-year-old man presented with leg weakness and numbness for 6 months and left arm pain for 2 months. Imaging revealed a C3/4 disc herniation and an intradural lesion at the same level. He underwent one-stage posterior surgery including laminectomy, intradural tumor resection, and microscopic transdural discectomy. Histological studies confirmed a herniated disc and a meningioma. Symptoms improved substantially, and functional recovery was achieved at follow-up.  
**Conclusion:** In rare cases, an intradural tumor can coexist with cervical disc herniation. When suspicious findings are present or clinical symptoms cannot be fully explained, contrast-enhanced MR imaging is helpful for differential diagnosis. Microscopic transdural discectomy is safe and may be considered as an optional procedure for cervical disc herniation in selected cases.

**Keywords:** Cervical disc herniation; Spinal tumor; Intradural meningioma; Transdural discectomy

## Introduction

Cervical disc herniation is a common spinal disorder that can lead to radiculopathy and myelopathy [1]. For those who fail to respond to conservative treatments, anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is currently the standard treatment [2]. Cervical intraspinal tumors are less common diseases that may produce symptoms similar to those of cervical disc herniation [3]. Meningioma is one of the most common intraspinal tumors in the cervical spine and typically requires surgical removal via a posterior approach [4]. Symptomatic disc herniation and intradural tumor occurring at the same cervical spinal level is extremely rare. Given the different surgical approaches for the two pathologies, two-stage surgery is a common choice. Here, we report a rare case of C3/4 disc herniation concomitant with an intradural meningioma at the same level, successfully treated using a single one-stage posterior surgery of tumor resection and transdural discectomy.

## Case Report

### Case presentation

A 76-year-old man presented with leg weakness and numbness for 6 months and left arm pain for 2 months. Six months earlier, he began to experience leg weakness and numbness. While walking, he often felt that his legs were “stepping on cotton.” Although the weakness reduced his mobility to some extent, he could live on his own and did not seek medical care.

Two months before presentation, he developed left arm pain radiating from his neck down to his left shoulder and elbow. The pain was severe and intolerable, accompanied by worsening gait. He presented to a local hospital where magnetic resonance (MR) imaging revealed a herniated disc at the C3/4 level. He received conservative treatment for 2 weeks, but the pain did not improve. By the time he consulted us, he could hardly walk independently. He reported no bladder or bowel problems.

On physical examination, there was tenderness in the upper neck. Spurling sign and Eaton sign were positive on the left. Muscle power was generally decreased in bilateral upper and lower extremities (manual muscle test grade III–IV). Reflexes at the biceps, triceps, patellar, and Achilles tendons were hyperactive. Pathological reflexes, including Hoffman sign, Babinski sign, and dynamic Romberg sign, were positive. His Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA) score was 9 out of 17.

On routine MR images, the C3/4 intervertebral disc herniated posteriorly with apparent dural sac compression. Moreover, there was heterogeneous signal in the context of the C3 spinal cord, and it appeared that cerebrospinal fluid flow was discontinued at that level. An intradural mass was suspected, and gadolinium contrast-enhanced MR imaging was performed. Contrast MR imaging revealed a hyperintense intradural lesion in the right portion of the C3 canal. Axial MR images demonstrated that the cervical spinal canal behind the C3/4 disc was occupied by a centrally and left-sided herniated disc and a right-sided intradural lesion. Spinal cord compression and left foraminal narrowing were remarkable.

### Surgical procedure

A one-stage posterior surgery was performed to treat both pathologies simultaneously, including C3/4 laminectomy, intradural tumor resection, transdural C3/4 discectomy, and C3/4 lateral mass instrumentation and fusion.

After C3/4 laminectomy, the dorsal dural sac was opened and an intradural lesion (approximately $10\times15~\mathrm{mm}^2$) was identified on the right side. Under microscopy, the mass was carefully removed from the dura mater. On the left portion of the canal, a hump compressing the ventral dural sac was observed. The spinal cord was gently pushed to the right side and protected. A needle was inserted into the hump, and intraoperative X-ray confirmed that it corresponded to the C3/4 disc level. Under microscopy, a 0.5 cm longitudinal incision was made in the ventral dura mater, through which herniated nucleus tissue was removed. Due to limited exposure, the ventral dural incision was difficult to suture and was therefore sealed using gelfoam and human fibrin adhesive. The dorsal dural sac was sutured, followed by C3/4 lateral mass screw instrumentation and fusion.

### Postoperative course and follow-up

After surgery, the patient’s left arm pain disappeared and numbness of the limbs substantially alleviated. His walking ability improved quickly, and he could walk independently by postoperative day 10 when he was discharged. Pathological studies confirmed a herniated disc and a meningioma.

At 6-month follow-up, the patient walked independently with a normal gait. He had no pain and no numbness in his arms or legs. His JOA score increased to 16. Imaging demonstrated solid C3/4 posterior fusion and an enlarged spinal canal without dural sac or nerve root compression.

## Discussion

Coexistence of symptomatic disc herniation and an intraspinal tumor in the same cervical segment is extremely rare. In the few cases reported, the spinal tumor is often an asymptomatic incidental finding when MR study is conducted for cervical disc herniation [5, 6]. In the current case, C3/4 disc herniation was mainly responsible for left arm radiculopathy, as evidenced by foraminal involvement on MR images. Both the herniated disc and the meningioma contributed to cervical myelopathy. Because both pathologies were symptomatic, treating only one might not relieve all symptoms.

Although cervical discectomy is routinely performed through an anterior approach and cervical intradural tumor resection typically requires a posterior approach, we designed a one-stage posterior surgery with intradural tumor resection and transdural discectomy to treat both pathologies simultaneously. Because the spinal cord was compressed by the tumor from the right and by the herniated disc from the left, and because the patient had severe left arm pain with corresponding foraminal narrowing, we performed a full laminectomy to achieve sufficient decompression. Although both pathologies could potentially be removed through a right hemilaminectomy and posterior instrumentation and fusion might be avoidable, discectomy through a contralateral window of hemilaminectomy is risky [7] and dependent on surgical experience. Even so, a one-stage strategy involves less overall surgical trauma and cost compared with two-stage surgery, and the patient achieved full recovery.

In the English literature, only two similar cases have been reported, and both were treated with two-stage surgeries [5, 6]. Bapat et al. reported concomitant disc herniation and intradural schwannoma at C5/6 [5]. In that case, the intradural lesion was overlooked during the first hospitalization and ACDF was performed. The intradural tumor was recognized retrospectively only after recurrent myelopathy, and a second posterior surgery was required to remove the tumor. Similarly, a coexisting meningioma was overlooked in Gandolfi’s case, necessitating a second surgery for cervical tumor resection [6].

Among spinal tumors, meningioma and schwannoma constitute approximately 90% of extramedullary intradural tumors [8]. Although typically benign [9], intradural meningiomas can cause symptoms similar to disc herniation. Meningiomas often present with isointense signals similar to the spinal cord on both T1- and T2-weighted MR images, which can obscure detection. This is particularly relevant in middle-aged adults, in whom cervical disc bulging or herniation is common and neurological symptoms may be attributed to disc pathology, as in Bapat’s case [5]. When heterogeneous signal within the spinal cord is seen on conventional MR images, when diagnosis remains unclear, when pain is exacerbated in the recumbent position, when pain persists after treatment, or when neurological symptoms cannot be explained by slight disc compression, suspicion of tumor should be raised and contrast-enhanced MR imaging is indicated [10, 11]. Strong, homogeneous gadolinium enhancement is characteristic of meningioma [9]. A posterior approach provides adequate exposure and is routinely used to remove cervical meningiomas [4].

Although ACDF is the standard procedure for symptomatic cervical disc herniation, posterior transdural removal of ventral lesions was historically attempted. In 1910, Taylor introduced laminectomy and a transdural approach to remove ventral lesions [12], but due to concerns about spinal cord injury, the technique was not widely adopted [13]. After the anterior Smith–Robinson approach was introduced in 1958, posterior transdural discectomy was largely abandoned [14]. With advances in microscopy and surgical technique, transdural discectomy may be less risky than previously thought. In a report of 30 cases of cervical spondylosis treated with microscopic transdural discectomy, no spinal cord injury or cerebrospinal fluid leakage occurred [15]. This procedure can preserve the cervical segment because fusion may not be required [15]. However, it is not familiar to most spine surgeons and has been recommended only as an alternative for selected patients, such as those with paracentral cervical disc herniation associated with multilevel canal stenosis [13]. In the present case, posterior laminectomy and dural opening were already required for the intradural meningioma, and the herniated disc was located directly in front of the ventral dura mater. Therefore, transdural discectomy was practical and spared the patient a second anterior surgery.

## Conclusion

In rare cases, an intradural tumor can coexist with cervical disc herniation. Neurological symptoms may be attributed to the herniated disc, and an intradural tumor may be overlooked. When suspicious findings are present or clinical symptoms cannot be fully explained, contrast-enhanced MR imaging is helpful for differential diagnosis. One-stage posterior surgery, including laminectomy, intradural tumor resection, and transdural discectomy, can be used to treat two pathologies simultaneously while reducing medical costs. Transdural discectomy may be an optional procedure for cervical disc herniation in selected patients with concomitant spinal disorders.
