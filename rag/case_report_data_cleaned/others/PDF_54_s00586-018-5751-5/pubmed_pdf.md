# Paget's Disease of the Lumbar Spine: Decompressive Surgery Following 17 Years of Bisphosphonate Treatment

## Abstract

**Background**  
We present a rare case of Paget's disease (PD) with involvement of the lumbar spine over a period of 18 years. We discuss the diagnostic process to rule out alternative diagnoses and medical and surgical treatment strategies.

**Case Description**  
A 58-year-old man, first diagnosed with PD in 1998 with solid involvement of the fourth lumbar vertebra, underwent periodic examinations over a period of 18 years. Since diagnosis, the patient was treated conservatively with bisphosphonates. When conservative treatment options were exhausted, surgery was indicated due to a progressively reduced ability to walk. Surgery with undercutting decompression via laminotomy was performed. PD was confirmed by biopsy. Bisphosphonate treatment was continued pre- and postoperatively. Follow-up examinations showed an improvement in clinical outcome measures.

**Conclusion**  
The patient presented with isolated lumbar involvement of PD. He remained asymptomatic on bisphosphonate therapy for almost 17 years but presented with new-onset back pain. In such cases, rare conversion into sarcoma must be ruled out, and biopsy should be performed even in the absence of signs of malignancy. Currently, there are no clear treatment recommendations available in the literature for PD with expansive growth complicated by spinal stenosis. Undercutting decompression should be considered.

## Case Presentation

A 58-year-old man presented with a 6-month history of severe low back pain. He was diagnosed with PD involving the fourth lumbar vertebra in 1998, confirmed by biopsy. Since then, the patient had been managed using conservative treatment, including medical therapy with corticosteroids, bisphosphonates, and methotrexate. He underwent periodic clinical and radiological examinations over a period of 18 years.

The patient presented with symptoms of claudication with a walking distance of 60 m. Clinical examination showed unremarkable findings of the spine and normal neurologic status, without radicular pain or paralysis and no problems with bladder or bowel control. The patient's medical history was significant for hypertension, atrial fibrillation treated with cardioversion, hyperthyroidism, hyperlipoproteinemia, and obesity (BMI 31.5 kg/m²). Physical examination was otherwise normal.

Bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (54.9; reference range 3.7–20.9 g/l), alkaline phosphatase (2.67 μkat/l), and C-reactive protein (5.01 mg/l) were elevated on the day of admission.

X-rays of the lumbar spine in two planes and a CT scan showed increased vertebral body expansion of the fourth lumbar vertebra.

## Diagnostic Imaging

Radiographs revealed a relatively lucent density of the fourth vertebral body with increased density in the periphery. CT demonstrated involvement of the entire vertebra, including the processus spinosus and vertebral body, with enlargement and abnormal vertebral shape. The vertebral body showed multiple sclerotic foci of compact bone with thorny edges and a bone-within-bone appearance. The sagittal profile revealed a biconcave deformity of the vertebral endplates with equal anterior and posterior height.

MRI demonstrated spinal canal stenosis with subtotal stenosis measuring 4 mm at L3/L4 and 6 mm at L4/L5, affecting both L3 and L4 nerve roots. The affected vertebral bodies showed diffuse bone sclerosis, marrow edema, focal fatty changes, and sclerotic spots.

Bone scintigraphy (SPECT) was performed to exclude additional lesions and demonstrated involvement of the processus spinosi at T7 and S1. No pathological collapse was observed on CT or MRI.

## Historical Review, Epidemiology, Diagnosis, Pathology, and Differential Diagnosis

Paget's disease was first described in 1877 as “osteitis deformans” by Sir James Paget. In Europe, the prevalence is approximately 0.3% and is decreasing in most regions. PD is more common in men and increases with age, with evidence of hereditary influence [7].

The spine is among the most frequently affected sites in PD [3], and most reported cases involve only one vertebra. Lumbar involvement occurs in approximately 53% of cases [8]. Although PD of the spine is typically asymptomatic, up to one-third of patients become symptomatic [2]. The lumbar spine, especially L4 and L5, is most commonly involved. Symptomatic spinal stenosis due to PD remains rare. Pain and progressive spinal stenosis are the primary symptoms when the spine is affected [4].

Pestka et al. [6] identified spinal involvement in 101 of 754 PD patients, with polyostotic disease in 61.4% and monostotic involvement in 38.6%.

Differential diagnoses include vertebral hemangioma, metabolic bone disease such as renal osteodystrophy or primary hyperparathyroidism, fibrous dysplasia, lymphoma, and metastatic disease [5].

Malignant transformation to sarcoma is rare, with reported incidences ranging from 0.3% to 10% [5]. Malignancy must be excluded by biopsy when the diagnosis is unclear, in cases of pathological fracture, or with rapid disease progression after long-term disease control. CT-guided percutaneous transpedicular biopsy is a reliable method to confirm PD [9].

## Rationale for Treatment

The patient was first diagnosed with PD in 1998 and remained asymptomatic for nearly 17 years before presenting with back pain. Conservative treatment remains the gold standard for PD with spinal involvement, and uninterrupted bisphosphonate therapy was initiated at diagnosis.

Bisphosphonate therapy can prevent the need for surgery and was effective in this case for nearly two decades. Its use aims to prevent complications such as deformity and spinal cord dysfunction. A Cochrane protocol published in 2014 evaluated the benefits of bisphosphonates in improving clinical outcomes and preventing complications in adults with PD [1]. However, long-term studies exceeding 10 years are rare, and symptomatic patients requiring decompression surgery carry a risk of major complications, including death [4].

Preoperative bisphosphonate therapy may reduce postoperative hematoma and complications, although its effect on intraoperative blood loss has not been evaluated in controlled trials.

Complications of PD include spinal stenosis with neurological deficits and vertebral collapse. There are no clear surgical treatment recommendations in the literature for PD-associated spinal stenosis. Refractory pain and severe limitation of walking distance should prompt consideration of surgical intervention.

On admission, the patient demonstrated progressive claudication and pain (VAS 6). After interdisciplinary tumor board discussion, biopsy and decompression surgery were considered the optimal treatment approach. Given reported complication rates of up to 27% with laminectomy [4], an extended laminotomy with undercutting decompression was selected.

## Operative Procedure

Surgery was performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the prone position on a Wilson frame using a dorsal midline approach from L3 to L5. The interlaminar spaces at L3/L4 and L4/L5 were exposed and dissected. Hypertrophic facet joints with cystic changes adjacent to the dura and hypertrophy of the ligamentum flavum were identified.

The thickened ligamentum flavum was carefully dissected, and decompression was performed cranially, caudally, and laterally, requiring substantial ventral decompression. The dura was mobilized, and decompression was performed bilaterally at L3/L4 and L4/L5. Sub-laminar decompression was achieved by cranial laminar dissection.

Total blood loss was 150 ml. No intraoperative or postoperative complications occurred. The patient was mobilized on the first postoperative day, and surgical drains were removed on postoperative day 2.

## Clinical Outcome

Histopathological examination confirmed Paget's disease without evidence of malignant transformation. An increased number of osteoclasts advancing at the interface of irregularly thickened bony trabeculae was observed, consistent with PD. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 7 with moderate pain (VAS 4). Postoperative management included continued bisphosphonate therapy, corticosteroids, and leflunomide. An orthosis was used for 4 weeks, and physical therapy was continued for 3 months.

Follow-up assessments at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months included medical history, patient satisfaction, pain scores, and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). At final follow-up, the patient demonstrated complete resolution of claudication, significant improvement in ODI (from 14 to 4), reduced pain (VAS 2–3), and full recovery of activities of daily living. No disease progression was observed on MRI at 12 months postoperatively. Walking distance increased to 5000 m at 24 months.
