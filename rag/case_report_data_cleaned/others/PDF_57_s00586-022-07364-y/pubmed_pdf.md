# Cervical Rotational Osteotomy for Correction of Axial Deformity in a Patient with Ankylosing Spondylitis

## Abstract

### Purpose
Spinal axial deformity in ankylosing spondylitis (AS) has received limited attention, and there are concerns about surgical treatment for axial deformity associated with AS. This case study aims to demonstrate a surgical technique for cervical rotational osteotomy.

### Methods
We present the case of a young AS patient whose neck was fixed in a left-rotational posture at $18^{\circ}$, requiring him to turn his trunk to the right to look forward visually. This made his gait appear lopsided, as though limping, and caused significant inconvenience. To correct this deformity, we performed a novel cervical rotational osteotomy through a one-stage posterior–anterior–posterior approach. First, we performed laminectomies of C7 and T1, followed by a C7/T1 facetectomy with release of the bilateral C8 nerve roots. Next, we performed C7/T1 discectomy and bony resection of the lateral body and uncovertebral joints. The patient’s head was then rotated manually so that both his face and torso simultaneously faced forward. Finally, rods spanning the screws from C6 to T2 were fixed.

### Results
Postoperatively, the patient’s axial malalignment was significantly improved, and he was able to walk normally. Surgical outcomes were well maintained at the 3-year follow-up.

### Conclusion
Through this case, we hope to draw attention to spinal axial deformity and provide a reference point for the surgical treatment of spinal axial deformity.

**Keywords:** Axial deformity · Ankylosing spondylitis · Cervical · Osteotomy

## Introduction

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory disease that primarily affects the spine and sacroiliac joints. In the late stages of AS, patients can develop significant fixed spinal three-dimensional deformity. Sagittal deformity is most often secondary to AS, such as thoracolumbar kyphosis, chin-on-chest deformity, or ear-on-shoulder deformity. This results in difficulty looking forward or lying down horizontally and may cause problems with ambulation, dysphagia, and dyspnea. Currently, there is extensive literature on osteotomy techniques for AS patients with sagittal deformity [1, 2]. However, there is little concern regarding axial deformity associated with AS, and there is a lack of reports concerning the surgical treatment of AS patients with severe axial deformity.

We present the case of a young patient with AS who complained of severe neck stiffness. Axial rotational deformity required his trunk to be turned to the right in order to look forward, causing a lopsided gait as if limping and leading to easy fatigue. We performed a novel 360-degree cervical rotational osteotomy through a one-stage posterior–anterior–posterior approach to correct the cervical axial deformity, allowing his face and torso to face forward simultaneously. Postoperatively, the patient no longer needed to walk with his trunk turned to the right, and his gait difficulty resolved. Overall, the surgery significantly improved his quality of life. Through this case, we hope to draw attention to spinal axial deformity and provide a reference for the surgical treatment of spinal axial deformity.

## Patient Information

A 24-year-old male patient with a 12-year history of AS complained of significant neck stiffness recurring over 5 years, with no neurological symptoms. He was unable to rotate or flex his neck. His horizontal gaze was well maintained, and his neck was fixed at $18^{\circ}$ of left rotation. To look forward while walking, he had to turn his body to the right, causing him to walk with difficulty, as though limping (see supplementary video 1). Due to the asymmetric manner of walking, he became tired easily and suffered from right hip pain. If he walked with his trunk facing forward, his head would be constantly turned to the left. As a result, he had to walk slowly and exercise extreme caution to avoid falling (see supplementary video 2). He had received bilateral total hip replacements 1 year prior.

He reported that symptoms initiated insidiously over recent years, with mild cervical pain. At presentation, he was incapable of rotating his head, which was fixed in a left-rotational position.

Upper and lower limb strengths were grade V (normal). Sensation was normal and symmetric in both the torso and limbs. No pathologic reflexes or signs of myelopathy were found.

The patient had no significant cervical sagittal deformity, and his global sagittal balance was well maintained. Trunk shift was measured as the horizontal distance between the central sacral vertical line and the C7 plumb line. The C7 plumb line shift to the left was 2.7 cm. Radiographic shoulder height was measured as +1.2 cm. The entire lumbar segments and most of the thoracic segments were fused posteriorly. The entire cervical spine was fused anteriorly and/or posteriorly, including the atlantoaxial joint. From CT, rotation mainly occurred in the upper cervical segments, from the occiput to C2. The angle between the axial midline of the foramen magnum (FM) and C1 was 2°. The angle between the axial midline of C1 and C2 was 9°. The angle between the axial midline of C2 and C7 was 7°. In total, the angle between the axial midline of the FM and C7 was 18°.

## Surgical Strategy

The goal of surgery was to correct axial misalignment and restore the patient’s ability to look forward without turning his body to the right. Given that his cervical facet joints were fused, the patient required a posterior osteotomy followed by an anterior osteotomy to achieve a 360-degree release, and finally posterior instrumentation [1]. Thus, one-stage posterior–anterior–posterior approach surgery was carried out with intraoperative neuromonitoring.

### Posterior Stage
The patient was placed prone on the operating table with his head secured by a Mayfield horseshoe headrest. Bilateral lateral mass screws were placed in C6, and pedicle screws were placed in C7–T2. The posterior osteotomy included laminectomies of C7 and T1, followed by a C7/T1 facetectomy with exposure of the C8 nerve roots in the neural foramina. Bilateral intervertebral foramina were enlarged by partial resection of C7 pedicles to ensure the C8 nerve roots were fully decompressed. The wound was temporarily sutured after gauze packing.

### Anterior Stage
The patient was then turned to a supine position. Because his head could not be turned upward, a regular Smith–Robinson approach was used to expose the C7/T1 intervertebral disc. Discectomy, bony resection of the lateral body and uncovertebral joints, and entry into the transverse foramen were performed. After complete release at the C7/T1 level, the head was rotated manually until the tip of the nose faced forward. A titanium plate was then fixed.

### Posterior Reinstrumentation Stage
The patient was placed prone again. The spinal cord and nerve roots at the osteotomy site were probed to confirm there was no obvious impingement. Rods spanning the screws from C6 to T2 were fixed using connectors on both sides. After rotational osteotomy, the patient’s face and torso were able to face forward simultaneously. Postoperative X-ray showed that the neck rotational deformity was largely corrected. Postoperative CT axial slices showed that while the angle between the axial midline of the FM and C7 was unchanged, the axial midline of the FM was almost parallel with that of T1.

## Postoperative Course

The patient experienced left C8 nerve root paralysis after surgery, with left upper-extremity pain and dysesthesias, and left hand grip weakness in the immediate postoperative period. These symptoms improved after methylprednisolone and mecobalamin treatment, and he fully recovered after 3 months. The patient was mobilized 2 days after surgery and wore a Philadelphia collar for 3 months. He was able to walk with his face and trunk facing forward simultaneously (see supplementary video 3). This outcome was well maintained at one-year and three-year follow-up. Cervical axial rotation had not worsened at the three-year follow-up.

## Discussion

While correction of sagittal deformity in AS patients has been extensively studied [1, 2, 9, 10, 14], correction of axial malalignment is poorly reported. In a normal person, the spine is composed of multiple kinematic segments, comparable to a flexible hose. In the axial plane, axial rotation of the spine reaches 90°–95°, averaging 60° [3], 25° [4], and 9° [5] in the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spine, respectively. During slow walking, the pelvis and thorax rotate around the supporting leg [6]. However, in this AS patient, the spine resembled a rigid stick and had lost most rotational function. To walk with his face forward, he turned his body to the right, resulting in asymmetric bilateral rotational axes. As a result, he tired easily and suffered hip pain after long walks. After surgery, he was able to walk with his face and trunk facing forward simultaneously. Although he had lost most axial rotation of the spine, his pelvis and thorax were able to rotate around two symmetric instantaneous rotational axes (the supporting leg), enabling a more normal gait with less energy cost.

Cervical osteotomies are among the most difficult procedures to perform. Selection principles for the osteotomy site include feasibility, effectiveness, and safety. Three-column osteotomy in the cervical and upper thoracic spine is powerful but associated with significant morbidity and complications, with mortality rates ranging from 1.3% to 6.7% and neurological complication rates ranging from 13.5% to 43.6% [7–10], including nerve root weakness, surgical site infection, dysphagia, and medical-related complications. In this case of cervical axial malalignment, osteotomy at thoracic or lumbar segments would not correct the axial mismatch between head and torso. Moreover, because the axial deformity extended primarily from the occiput to C7, osteotomy at completely ankylosed atlantoaxial segments could not fully correct the deformity and posed a high risk of vertebral artery (VA) injury.

Therefore, we performed osteotomy at the cervicothoracic junction for the following reasons: relatively large size of the spinal canal at C7/T1; mobility of the spinal cord and eighth cervical nerves in this region; maintenance of good hand function even in the event of C8 nerve injury; and a relatively safe position of the VA in front of the transverse process of C7 before entering the transverse foramen at C6 [11, 12]. Osteotomy at C7 is often preferred given the lower risk of devastating neurological injury and VA injury compared with more proximal cervical levels [13, 14]. Although VA angiography was not performed, cervical MRI showed that VAs were located within their transverse foramina at C4–6 and were absent in the transverse foramina at C7. Published data show that abnormal VA entrance is more often seen at C3–C5 and minimal at C7. Bruneau et al. [15] reported abnormal VA entrance in 7.0% of cases, compared with entrance into the C7 transverse foramen at 0.8%. Hong et al. [16] reported atypical VA entrance in 5.1% of cases, with entrance into the C7 transverse foramen at 0.3%. Shin et al. [17] found entry into the left transverse foramen of C7 in only 0.2%. Despite the lack of prior reports on cervical rotational correction, intraoperative monitoring was used and the patient’s head was manually rotated to about 20° intraoperatively. Although there was concern for nerve root or spinal cord injury during twisting, likely due to the relatively large space available for the spinal cord at C7/T1 [18], no severe neurological deterioration occurred except mild left C8 paralysis.

Another limitation is short-segment fixation. Many recommend long-segment fixation to counteract long lever arms in a rigid ankylosed spine [13, 19]. Long-segment screw/rod constructs offer stronger stabilization, reducing postoperative subluxation or implant failure. This patient was 24 years old, with good intraoperative bone quality. Extensive osteotomy such as wedge osteotomy or complete vertebral column resection was not performed. Thus, fixation extended 2 levels above and below the osteotomy site both anteriorly and posteriorly. Postoperatively, he wore a Philadelphia collar for 3 months, and alignment was well maintained, though longer follow-up is needed.

This is the first case report of cervical rotational osteotomy for correction of axial deformity. More case reports are needed to refine the technique and evaluate clinical outcomes. Through this case, we hope to draw attention to spinal axial deformity and provide a reference for the surgical treatment of spinal axial deformity.
