# Post-traumatic Collet-Sicard syndrome: Personal Observation and Review of the Pertinent Literature with Clinical, Radiologic, and Anatomic Considerations

## Abstract

Study design: The lesion of the lower four cranial nerves, commonly called Collet-Sicard syndrome, can be caused by blunt head and neck trauma. It may be associated with an isolated fracture of the occipital condyle or the atlas.  
Objective: The aim of this report is to assess the modality of the trauma, the type of fracture, the anatomic characteristics, the treatment, and clinical results of this syndrome.  
Summary of background data: We discuss 14 cases of fracture of the occipital condyle and the atlas, along with 1 personal case.  
Methods: We analyzed 14 cases collected from the literature between 1925 and 2013, reported a further personal case, and performed an anatomical study of the paracondylar, atlas, and styloid process regions. The anatomical dissection was performed to assess the anatomic relationships in the site of transit where nerves IX, X, XI, and XII are injured.  
Results: A total of 14 cases of post-traumatic Collet-Sicard syndrome (p-CSS) were collected: 9 caused by a condyle fracture and 5 by an atlas fracture. The patients were 13 males and only 1 female. Ten of them had blunt trauma due to axial loading.  
Conclusions: p-CSS is caused by force directed through the top of the head. Nerve injuries result from laceration due to condyle displacement or compression and stretching as they pass between the lateral mass of the atlas and the styloid process. Conservative treatment is the option of choice, with surgical intervention generally not aimed at fixing nerve damage.

## Introduction

The Collet-Sicard syndrome (CSS) is a unilateral paralysis of the lower cranial nerves (IX, X, XI, and XII). It is named after Justin Frederic Collet and Jean-Athanase Sicard, who independently described the condition. It is an uncommon syndrome caused by lesions of the skull base due to primary or metastatic tumors, vascular lesions, inflammatory processes, iatrogenic complications, and trauma.  

Closed head and neck injuries resulting in atlas or occipital condyle fractures may cause post-traumatic Collet-Sicard syndrome (p-CSS). This report presents a clinical case of such a syndrome in a patient with a C1 fracture and includes a review of literature on closed head trauma leading to p-CSS. Additionally, a cadaver dissection study of the paracondylar, atlas, and styloid region was conducted to evaluate the relationships between vascular, osteo-ligamentous structures and the cranial nerves involved in the syndrome.

## Methods

We conducted a literature review of p-CSS, reported a personal case, and performed an anatomical study on cadavers.

## Literature Review

A computer-aided search of MEDLINE using PubMed was conducted from 1925 to 2013 to collect cases of p-CSS related to closed head and neck trauma. Keywords such as "Collet-Sicard syndrome," "atlas fracture," and "condyle fracture" were used, limiting the search to human studies.

Traumatic cervical lesions associated with p-CSS were well described in the literature, with 13 cases reported. Nine were caused by condyle fractures and four by atlas fractures. We also reported a personal observation of p-CSS in a patient with a C1 fracture. The overall mean age was 44.2 years (range 18–71), with condyle fractures averaging 41.5 years and atlas fractures averaging 40.2 years.

## Personal Observation

A 58-year-old man, referred from another hospital, was seen 3 days after falling from a horse. A CT scan revealed a fracture of the right lateral mass of the atlas, involving both the anterior and posterior arches. The patient was treated conservatively with a Philadelphia cervical collar. Initial clinical examination showed neck pain, hoarseness, difficulty swallowing, tongue deviation, slurred speech, and partial right vocal cord palsy. An MRI confirmed the transverse ligament's integrity and the right articular mass fracture of C1. The patient was monitored with serial imaging. After 120 days, the fracture showed complete fusion, and his symptoms gradually improved, with residual mild dysphonia and difficulty swallowing.

## Anatomical Observation

Anatomical specimens were prepared using two different exposures: anterolateral in the supine position and a posterolateral approach in the prone position. These dissections highlighted the narrow space through which cranial nerves IX, X, XI, and XII pass near the atlas and styloid process. The study helped define the anatomical relationships in the paracondylar, atlas, and styloid regions and offered insights into potential stabilization methods for the condylar region.

## Discussion

Collet-Sicard syndrome results from unilateral lesions of cranial nerves IX, X, XI, and XII, leading to symptoms like hoarseness, dysphagia, and shoulder weakness. The condition can result from skull base lesions due to tumors, vascular lesions, or trauma. Trauma to the occipital condyle or atlas may cause p-CSS, where the fractures affect the cranial nerves' transit area. A review of the literature suggests that condyle fractures are more often associated with severe nerve damage compared to atlas fractures.

The mechanisms of trauma causing condyle or atlas fractures are typically related to falls or impacts that direct force through the top of the head. The fractures can lead to compression, stretching, or laceration of the cranial nerves.

## Atlas Fractures

Atlas fractures are typically caused by axial load or vertical compression. Studies show that while a purely axial load on C1 is rare, it is often combined with other forces such as flexion, extension, and rotation. In fractures involving lateral displacement of the atlas, the space through which cranial nerves pass is reduced, leading to potential nerve damage.

## Occipital Condyle Fractures

Condyle fractures, especially those with displacement, are more likely to cause severe cranial nerve damage due to the tight anatomical space around the condyle. Surgical treatment of cranial nerve injuries in p-CSS is generally not considered effective. Conservative treatments, such as immobilization with a cervical collar or halo vest, are typically preferred.

## Conclusion

Post-traumatic Collet-Sicard syndrome is primarily caused by occipital condyle fractures or atlas fractures. In condyle fractures, the nerve injuries are severe, while in atlas fractures, the nerves are more likely to be stretched than torn. Conservative treatment remains the preferred approach, and surgical intervention is typically reserved for spinal stabilization rather than nerve repair.
