# Anomalous Cervical Spinous Process Leading to Myelopathy

## Abstract
### Study Design
A case report and literature review.

### Objective
To present a rare case of anomalous spinous process of the sixth cervical vertebra invaginating within the spinal canal, causing myelopathy and being successfully managed with surgical excision.

### Summary of Background Data
Although anomalous development of the posterior arch of the atlas and axis has been documented to cause spinal cord impingement, there has been no documented literature on impingement from an anomalous free-floating spinous process of the subaxial spine causing compressive myelopathy.

### Methods
A 42-year-old female with features of cervical myelopathy was investigated and found to have an anomalous free-floating spinous process of the sixth cervical vertebra impinging on the cord.

### Results
The patient underwent surgical excision of the anomalous bone and had a satisfactory clinical outcome.

### Conclusion
Though previously unreported, this rare anomaly of an anomalous spinous process should be considered when evaluating a relatively young patient with myelopathy. Surgical excision of the free-floating anomalous spinous process yields a satisfactory outcome.

### Keywords
anomalous spinous process; cervical; spine; myelopathy; anomaly; invaginated spinous process; congenital cause of myelopathy

## Introduction
Anomalies of the posterior arch of the cervical spine are relatively rare [1-9]. Although such reports have pertained to C1 and C2, anomalous development of the posterior arch in the subaxial spine leading to myelopathy has not been previously reported. The case presented here involves an anomalous free-floating spinous process of the C6 vertebra, invaginating into the spinal canal and causing myelopathy.

## Case Report
A 42-year-old female presented with insidious-onset, gradually progressive gait clumsiness of 6 months’ duration. Bladder and bowel continence was preserved. Examination revealed bilateral lower-limb hyperreflexia with an inverted supinator jerk, positive Babinski sign, and a positive Romberg sign with eyes closed. She was unable to perform tandem gait. She had difficulty with fine motor function in the upper limbs; her finger grip-and-release counts were 12 and 13 on the right and left sides, respectively, over 10 seconds [1].

Plain radiographs, MRI, and CT of the cervical spine demonstrated an anomalous spinous process of the C6 vertebra invaginating within the spinal canal and causing compressive myelopathy (Figure 1A and 1B). Notably, the other subaxial cervical spinous processes lacked the normal bifid tips (Figure 2).

The patient underwent surgical decompression. Intraoperatively, the anomalous spinous process was free-floating. Careful dissection allowed complete excision without necessitating laminectomy at that level (Figure 3A and 3B). The non-bifid tips of the other subaxial cervical spinous processes seen on preoperative CT were also evident intraoperatively (Figure 3A).

Histopathological examination of the excised specimen revealed well-formed bone. Postoperatively, the patient’s neurological status improved, with reduction in gait unsteadiness. At 1 month, her finger grip-and-release counts improved to 24 and 25 on the right and left sides, respectively, over 10 seconds. Postoperative MRI showed satisfactory decompression of the cervical spinal cord (Figure 4), and postoperative CT confirmed complete excision of the anomalous bone (Figure 5).

## Discussion
Embryologically, four posterior vertebral arch chondrification centers unite to form two ossification centers on each side of the midline in the tenth week of gestation [1,3,6,8]. Each ossification center forms a pedicle, a lateral mass, and one half of the lamina. The two ossification centers fuse posteriorly when the child is 2 to 3 years old. The pathogenesis of rare laminar anomalies is hypothesized to involve failure of normal fusion of these chondrification or ossification centers, leading to a separated or free-floating lamina or spinous process, as in the present case; a similar mechanism has been hypothesized in spina bifida occulta [8].

Although congenital, compressive myelopathy due to posterior arch anomalies of the cervical spine has typically been reported only in or after the fourth decade of life [8]. Sakai et al. suggested that this late onset may result from developmental adaptation of the spinal cord, whereas Asakawa et al. considered that age-related changes progressively narrow the cervical canal, culminating in cord compression. Reports, though rare, have described similar anomalies of the atlas and axis [8]. To our knowledge, however, this is the first report describing an anomalous spinous process in the subaxial spine leading to compressive cervical myelopathy. Preoperative imaging, including CT, was essential for identifying this previously unreported entity. Surgical excision of the free-floating anomalous spinous process resulted in resolution of the patient’s symptoms.

## Key Points
- A case of anomalous free-floating spinous process of the sixth cervical vertebra is reported.
- Preoperative MRI and, particularly, CT scanning identified this extremely rare anomaly.
- The anomaly was associated with non-bifid spinous processes of other subaxial cervical vertebrae.
- Surgical excision of the free-floating anomalous spinous process led to satisfactory improvement in myelopathy.
