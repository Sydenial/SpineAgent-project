# Surgical correction of kyphotic deformity in a patient with Proteus syndrome

## Abstract

### Keywords:
Proteus syndrome; Kyphosis; Spinal instrumentation; Spinal deformity; Overgrowth; Spinal osteotomy

Proteus syndrome (PS) is an extremely rare congenital disorder causing asymmetric overgrowth, with skeletal deformities being common and often necessitating amputations. Only a few cases associated with spinal deformities have been described. This report discusses a rare case of PS associated with spinal deformity and its surgical management.

A 12-year-old boy with PS causing vertebral hypertrophy and kyphoscoliotic deformity, which was surgically corrected, is presented. The patient was assessed clinically and with whole spine radiographs, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Satisfactory correction of the deformity was achieved by posterior spinal fusion with instrumentation from T4–L5, five Ponte osteotomies (T8–L1), and an L2 pedicle subtraction osteotomy. The kyphosis was corrected from 87° to 55°, with improvement in all spinopelvic parameters. One year after surgery, the deformity correction was maintained with no deterioration of the sagittal balance, and the patient was free of pain and had no loss of neurologic function.

Proteus syndrome can be associated with spinal stenosis and deformity. Although the syndrome can be progressive, symptomatic spinal pathology should be treated appropriately.

## Introduction

A patient with the signs of Proteus syndrome (PS) was first described in 1979. The syndrome was named after the Greek god of the sea, Proteus, who could change his form at will to avoid capture. The etiology remains unclear, but it is believed to result from a mutation of the oncogene AKT1, which encodes a kinase involved in cell proliferation and apoptosis. This syndrome is extremely rare, and its diagnosis is still controversial due to high interpatient variation and overlap with other asymmetric overgrowth syndromes.

Clinical presentation is typically asymmetric partial gigantism and hemihypertrophy, affecting various body tissues, most commonly bone, connective tissue, and fat. Clinical findings include macrodactyly, subcutaneous tumors, plantar or palmar hyperplasia, cranial exostosis, kyphoscoliosis due to vertebral abnormalities, and other skeletal anomalies. The clinical course is usually progressive during childhood, though it may stabilize during early adolescence. Patients are often prothrombotic, which can lead to premature death from deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism.

## Case Report

A 12-year-old boy attended the spinal outpatient clinic with back pain and progressive kyphotic deformity. He had been diagnosed with PS at age 5. His visual analog scale pain score was 6/10. He had a flexed left knee and hip with a plantigrade foot, compensating for his left leg hemihypertrophy and balancing his pelvis. His shoulders were level. He had a right rib prominence and thoracic kyphosis, which were not correctable, along with mild waist asymmetry and trunk shifting.

Whole-spine radiographs showed a global kyphotic deformity of 63° (T4–L3) and a long C-shaped scoliosis measuring 25° (T2–L3). CT scans revealed severe dystrophic changes, with marked vertebral hyperplasia and incomplete segmentation of the vertebral bodies, especially around the cervicothoracic junction. MRI revealed syringomyelia at the C6-C7 level. The kyphotic deformity had progressed to 87° by age 14, and scoliosis to 36°. The patient experienced increased back pain, with right L5 radiculopathy and clinical imbalance of shoulders and pelvis.

## Procedure

A posterior correction of the deformity was performed under general anesthesia with dual-modality spinal cord monitoring. Intermittent calf pneumatic compression devices were used for perioperative thromboembolic prophylaxis. Five Ponte osteotomies (T8–L1) were carried out, and at L4–L5, a discectomy and decompression were performed. Bilateral segmental pedicle screws were placed (T4–L5) using a freehand technique. A pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO) was performed at L2, and definitive contoured rods were secured to the lumbar and thoracic regions to close down the osteotomies and stabilize the deformity.

The total blood loss was 1,170 mL. No perioperative bleeding or thromboembolic complications occurred. Postoperative anticoagulant prophylaxis with enoxaparin was started on the day of surgery and continued for 3 months.

## Outcome and Follow-up

At 3 months post-surgery, radiographs showed significant correction of the kyphosis to 55°, lumbar lordosis to 36°, and scoliosis to 13°. At 12 months, the patient was able to walk with walking sticks and experienced a significant reduction in back pain. Radiographs showed maintenance of the deformity correction with no deterioration of sagittal balance. The patient was free of neurologic deficits.

## Discussion

The diagnosis of Proteus syndrome is based on clinical and radiological evaluation. Diagnostic criteria include a mosaic distribution of the phenotype, progressive disease behavior, and specific clinical signs, such as cerebriform connective tissue nevus, disproportionate overgrowth, and vascular malformations. Proteus syndrome is associated with various tumors, including parotid adenomas and ovarian cystadenomas. The rate of premature death due to thromboembolic disease is high, making perioperative anticoagulant prophylaxis essential.

In the present case, the patient met the general diagnostic criteria for PS, including disproportionate overgrowth and connective tissue nevus. The patient underwent surgery to correct the kyphoscoliotic deformity and prevent further progression, as the deformity was causing significant pain and clinical imbalance.

The surgical approach involved multilevel osteotomies and spinal fusion, which resulted in satisfactory deformity correction. The posterior-only approach was preferred due to its lower risk of thromboembolic complications and the need for multilevel decompressions and osteotomies.

## Conclusions

Proteus syndrome is a rare disorder that can be associated with spinal stenosis and deformity. Although the syndrome may be progressive, symptomatic spinal pathology should be treated adequately. In this case, surgical intervention led to significant improvement in the patient's pain and spinal alignment, with maintenance of correction over time.
