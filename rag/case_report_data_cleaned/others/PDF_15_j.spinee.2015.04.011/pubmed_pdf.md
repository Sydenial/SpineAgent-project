# Polyetheretherketone (PEEK) Intervertebral Cage as a Cause of Chronic Systemic Allergy: A Case Report

## Abstract

**Background Context:** Polyetheretherketone (PEEK) is an organic polymer thermoplastic with strong mechanical and chemical resistance properties. It has been used in industry to fabricate items for demanding applications such as bearings, piston parts, composite valves, and cable insulation. Since the early 1980s, polyetheretherketone polymers have been increasingly used in orthopedic and spinal surgery applications. Numerous studies and years of clinical experience have confirmed the biocompatibility of this material.

**Purpose:** The purpose of this study was to report a case of chronic systemic allergy after anterior cervical decompression and fusion (ACDF) and implantation of an intervertebral PEEK cage, with resolution of symptoms after removal of the PEEK cage.

**Study Design/Setting:** This study is a case report with clinical evidence for allergy to PEEK.

**Methods:** The methods involve clinical findings and a review of the current literature.

**Results:** After ACDF and implantation of an intervertebral PEEK cage, the patient developed a dermatitis-like urticarial rash, severe itching, swelling of the tongue, and skin thickening. A skin patch test was positive for PEEK. Removal of the implant resulted in resolution of his allergy symptoms shortly after surgery.

**Conclusions:** Tissue reactions to PEEK are extremely rare. Herein, we present the first report of a chronic allergic response to interbody PEEK material.

**Key Words:** Polyetheretherketone; PEEK; Spine; Implant; Allergy; Hypersensitivity

## Introduction

Polyetheretherketone (PEEK) is an organic polymer thermoplastic with a variety of applications across multiple fields of industry, including mechanical and biomedical engineering. There is a wide range of chemical modifications and composites commercially available for PEEK that make it suitable for applications in orthopedic and neurological surgeries [1–4]. The favorable biomechanical properties, radiolucency, longevity, and biocompatibility have made PEEK implants ideally suited for spinal surgical applications, particularly as a material for intervertebral cages.

Multiple studies have demonstrated the longevity and mechanical advantages of PEEK interbody cages since their inception in the 1990s [5,6]. Biocompatibility studies and years of clinical application have demonstrated the material to be relatively well tolerated [7–11], although some reports note a minimal inflammatory response [8,12]. Despite widespread use, no case of hypersensitivity or allergy related to PEEK has previously been reported. This case presentation reports the first confirmed delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction to PEEK material.

## Case Report

### History

A 54-year-old man presented with neck pain and significant left arm pain. He had a history of multiple cervical spine procedures. His initial surgical intervention consisted of an anterior cervical discectomy, followed by a C5–C7 laminectomy. One year later, he underwent a C4–C5 anterior discectomy with insertion of an interbody device secured with screws. Five years later, he underwent a C4–C5 revision discectomy with an uninstrumented interbody fusion using allograft. He tolerated each of these procedures without complication.

He subsequently underwent an anterior cervical discectomy at C6–C7 with insertion of a PEEK interbody cage secured to the cervical spine with four small titanium screws. A small amount of local bone graft was inserted into the device before placement. No other osteobiologic material was used.

Postoperatively, the patient reported mild improvement of his neck pain. However, within four weeks of surgery, he developed generalized weakness and fatigue, followed by diffuse erythema and itching over most of his body, swelling of the tongue, redness in his throat, and swelling under both eyes. He described a constant sensation of a swollen tongue and throat redness.

Given the unexplained onset of clearly allergic symptomatology, the patient underwent broad-spectrum allergic patch testing by an allergist. The strong temporal correlation between implantation of a foreign material and symptom onset prompted inclusion of PEEK in the testing panel. Skin patch testing using PEEK material was performed three times and was positive, causing severe erythema and blistering at the site of skin contact. The allergist concluded that the patient was experiencing an allergy to PEEK and recommended removal of the C6–C7 interbody device.

### Examination and Imaging

On examination, the patient exhibited generalized blanching erythema, most prominent on the face, arms, and palms, thickened skin, and macroglossia. Neurological examination was unremarkable. Plain radiographs of the cervical spine demonstrated a lack of osseous bridging at C6–C7 with moderate prevertebral soft tissue swelling. There was no evidence of osteolysis, fracture, hardware failure, infection, or cervical instability on flexion-extension views.

### Clinical Course and Operative Intervention

The patient underwent anterior cervical re-exploration. There was no evidence of abnormal tissue reaction or infection at the surgical site. The C6–C7 interbody device was only partially fused to the adjacent vertebrae and was removed without difficulty. A structural graft was placed into the C6–C7 interspace and secured with a titanium plate and screws. Histological analysis of the explanted device demonstrated minimal new bone formation without evidence of significant inflammatory response.

### Postoperative Course

Within hours of surgery, the patient reported subjective improvement in itching. Examination revealed significant improvement in erythema, periorbital swelling, and macroglossia. He was discharged the following day. At his eight-week follow-up visit, he reported near-complete resolution of itching, erythema, periorbital swelling, and tongue swelling.

## Discussion

This case represents, to our knowledge, the first reported instance of a chronic systemic allergic reaction to a PEEK spinal implant. The temporal relationship between implantation, onset of allergic symptoms, positive skin patch testing, and rapid resolution of symptoms following implant removal provides compelling evidence of PEEK-related hypersensitivity.

The use of PEEK spinal implants has increased significantly over the past decade. In addition to intervertebral cages, PEEK has been used in fixation systems and disc replacement devices. Numerous biocompatibility studies have demonstrated that systemic allergic reactions to PEEK are exceedingly rare [7–11].

Given the exceptional rarity of this reaction, we do not suggest abandoning PEEK in spinal implant applications. Rather, this case highlights the importance of including PEEK hypersensitivity in the differential diagnosis for patients presenting with unexplained allergic symptoms following implantation. As PEEK technology continues to evolve, including the development of new composites designed to enhance osseointegration, awareness of potential bioreactivity remains important [8,12,13].

## Conclusions

Polyetheretherketone materials have extensive biological applications and are widely used in spinal surgery. Although generally well tolerated, this material may, in rare individuals, be the source of a chronic allergic reaction. Allergy to PEEK should be considered in patients presenting with unexplained systemic allergic symptoms after implantation of PEEK-based devices.
