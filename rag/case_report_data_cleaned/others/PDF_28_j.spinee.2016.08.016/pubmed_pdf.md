# Bilateral C1 Laminar Hooks Combined with C2 Pedicle Screw Fixation in the Treatment of Atlantoaxial Subluxation After Grisel's Syndrome

## Abstract

Atlantoaxial subluxation is an uncommon condition. In Grisel's syndrome, this subluxation occurs spontaneously after infectious or inflammatory processes of the head and neck. Diagnosis is typically based on clinical history and a strong suspicion of this syndrome. Non-surgical treatment most often resolves the symptoms; however, in some cases surgical treatment is necessary to repair the subluxation. Various surgical techniques and instrumentation systems have been used to treat atlantoaxial subluxation, although there is no consensus regarding the best treatment method, particularly in the pediatric population.

**Purpose.** To describe a case of atlantoaxial subluxation in a child with Grisel's syndrome treated surgically with an alternative construct.

**Study Design/Setting.** Case report and literature review.

**Methods.** Our case study involves a five-year-old female with a six-month history of unresolved Fielding type II atlantoaxial subluxation due to Grisel's syndrome. Despite conservative treatment, the patient's symptoms continued to progress. After two failed closed reduction attempts, open reduction and C1–C2 fusion was performed with atlas laminar hooks and axis pedicle polyaxial screws. A literature review of the surgical treatment of Grisel's syndrome was also performed.

**Results.** After surgery, the patient exhibited clinical and functional recovery with complete resolution of symptoms. At the 36-month follow-up examination, there was continual evidence of satisfactory reduction and fusion. No complications were observed. Upon completion of the literature review, eight Grisel's syndrome cases were found to have been treated surgically, with the minimum patient age being nine years.

**Conclusions.** Conservative management of Grisel's syndrome is the most common and effective treatment; however, a few surgical cases have been reported in the literature with good results. Satisfactory clinical results and fusion at 36 months post-surgery were achieved using atlas laminar hooks and axis pedicle polyaxial screws.

**Key words:** Atlantoaxial subluxation; atlas laminar hook; axis pedicle polyaxial screw; C1–C2 arthrodesis; Grisel's syndrome; Goel-Harms arthrodesis.

## Introduction

C1–C2 subluxation is an uncommon condition in clinical practice. If spontaneous subluxation of the atlantoaxial joint occurs secondary to infectious or inflammatory processes of the head and neck, it is known as Grisel's syndrome (GS) and is most often seen in children. In 1830, Sir Charles described the first case of atlantoaxial subluxation and Pierre Grisel described three patients with pharyngitis and torticollis who also experienced dislocation of the C1–C2 joint nearly a century later [1;2].

Conservative treatment of GS is the gold standard and involves anti-inflammatory, antibiotic, and muscle relaxant therapies as well as bed rest, physiotherapy, and immobilization with soft collars [3]. These methods typically offer relief of all symptoms and are considered first-line treatment prior to considering surgery [4]. Surgical intervention is rare and generally only undertaken when symptoms progress and initial treatments are no longer effective [5]. Occipitocervical fixation, C1–C2 wiring with bone graft (e.g., Brooks-Jenkins fusion), C1–C2 transarticular screws (Magerl technique), or C1 lateral mass screws and C2 transpedicular screw-rod constructs (described by Goel and modified by Harms) [6–9] have been described to achieve intraoperative reduction and subsequent fixation to maintain correct alignment. Potential growth and the possibility of postoperative spinal deformity is a concern associated with cervical arthrodesis in children, especially those younger than ten years.

This case study describes a five-year-old girl with a six-month history of unresolved type II atlantoaxial subluxation classified according to Fielding-Hawkings [10]. Surgical treatment involving open reduction and C1–C2 fusion with atlas laminar hooks and axis pedicle polyaxial screws was performed. Based on the 36-month outcome of this case, we report the surgical treatment of atlantoaxial subluxation and instability secondary to inflammatory processes (Grisel's syndrome) in a child.

## Case presentation

A five-year-old female was admitted to the emergency room of our center with a 15-day history of neck pain, right-sided head inclination and rotation, cervical muscle spasm, and electric pain behind her right ear, without any mechanism of injury. One week prior to admission, the patient had started micro-nebulization treatment for a broncho-obstructive syndrome.

Physical examination revealed cervical adenopathy, pain on palpation of the left side of the neck, and restriction in all cervical movements. Radiographic investigations identified right lateral inclination of the head and rectification of the cervical lordosis. A CT scan revealed an asymmetrical odontoid–lateral mass relationship. Based on these findings, initial treatment involved a Philadelphia soft collar and rest.

At a two-week follow-up examination, the patient presented with acute sinusitis and persistent impairment of cervical range of motion. Treatment with a cervical traction device (1 kg) was started and continued on a daily basis in combination with gentle soft tissue manipulation. Amoxicillin was prescribed for sinusitis treatment, along with physiotherapy. After 10 days of this inpatient treatment regimen, the deformity persisted. Under general anesthesia, closed reduction under CT guidance was attempted without success.

A follow-up MRI revealed fluid in the C1–C2 joint. After discussion among spine surgeons, the diagnosis of Grisel's syndrome was reached and further monitoring with home treatment including the use of a soft collar, analgesia, and physical therapy was recommended. There was no evidence of improvement in symptoms by the five-month clinical follow-up. Further CT imaging confirmed progression of the dislocation, and MRI revealed progression of C1–C2 subluxation and right rotation toward the C2 vertebral body, right C1–C2 facet dislocation, loss of relationship between the atlanto-odontoid joint, and scoliosis.

A new treatment intervention was proposed involving a cervical traction halo-traction system. This device was fitted under general anesthesia with the traction level starting at 1.23 kg (equivalent to 5% of total weight) and progressively increasing to 20% of total weight over 10 days. A second attempt of closed reduction under CT examination was performed using the transoral method according to Jeszenszky [11], achieving partial reduction. Further halo-traction was undertaken for 8 days; however, the reduction was not maintained. Consequently, surgical reduction was considered.

## Surgical technique

The patient, stabilized with the halo-traction device, was placed in the prone position and neurophysiological monitoring (including somatosensory- and motor-evoked potentials) was performed throughout the surgery. A posterior midline approach was performed and C1–C2 was exposed subperiosteally. Upon further exposure of the posterior arch of C1 and the lamina of C2, evidence of right C2 facet indentation was apparent.

Real-time fluoroscopy was used to place axis pedicle polyaxial screws (3.5 mm in diameter). Reduction was performed under C-arm fluoroscopy to verify alignment and positioning. Laminar hooks were placed on the posterior arch of the atlas, and connection of both constructs was made with posterior rods (3.5 mm in diameter). The laminae of C1 and C2 were then decorticated posteriorly and a demineralized bone matrix allograft was applied. No intraoperative complications were experienced.

Postoperative radiographs demonstrated proper alignment of the cervical spine and appropriate implant placement. After surgery, symptoms and limitations subsided. A rapid early postoperative recovery was observed, and the patient was discharged five days post-surgery with upper cervical spine soft collar stabilization for 8 weeks. Thereafter, physical therapy was initiated, including pain management techniques and exercises to regain cervical spine mobility. At the 36-month follow-up, C1–C2 spinal fusion was obtained based on radiographic evaluation without any spinal deformity; there were no further reports of neck pain, and gait abnormalities and urinary disturbances were absent.

## Discussion

This case demonstrates the use of C1 laminar hooks and C2 transpedicular screws united with connecting rods in the pediatric population. This construct was proposed by Ni et al. as an alternative surgical technique to treat atlantoaxial instability secondary to odontoid fracture, os odontoideum, or traumatic rupture of the transverse ligament [12]. A satisfying clinical result and fusion at the 36-month follow-up were achieved with this technique. The construct may minimize the risk of neurological or vascular injuries.

When a static CT scan is performed using the characteristic cock-robin posture of the head, there is a significant chance of misinterpreting the findings [13]. Static CT may facilitate the diagnosis of a subluxation or dislocation of C1 on C2, yet it does not demonstrate the dynamic relationship between this pair [14]. Dynamic relationship between C1 and C2 can be better seen with a Dynamic CT Scan (DCTS) [13,15]. Currently there is a lack of high-quality studies recommending its routine use to specifically evaluate rotatory subluxation. Alanay et al. found that DCTS has poor reliability and reproducibility in the diagnosis of atlantoaxial subluxation in patients with acute torticollis; as a result, they suggest that routine use of DCTS is a costly choice in patients with acute torticollis as the deformity usually resolves with simple cervical mobilization [16]. In our case, sequential CT scans were performed, and progression of subluxation was evident.

There is no formal consensus advocating the optimal surgical treatment method, particularly since conservative treatment is most often effective. Analysis of the literature revealed only eight cases of GS treated with arthrodesis where the youngest patient was nine years old [8;17–19]. One case was treated with the Goel-Harms technique [8], one with occipito-C3 fixation and wires [17], five cases were treated with C1–C2 sub- and interlaminar wires with an interspinous allograft [18], and one case was treated with C1–C2 transarticular screws combined with C1 lateral mass screws [19]. For our knowledge, this construct using atlas laminar hooks and axis pedicle polyaxial screws has not previously been described in the pediatric population for the treatment of GS.

In the pediatric population, the Goel-Harms technique (bilateral insertion of polyaxial head screws in the lateral mass of C1 and the pedicle of C2) has been an effective method for the management of atlantoaxial instability. Heuer et al. used this technique for six pediatric patients (age range 7.5 to 17 years) with instability secondary to os odontoideum [20]. All patients were reported with complete fusion and normal alignment after an average of 14 months follow-up. Only two patients experienced postoperative paresthesia in the C2 dermatome, which resolved spontaneously within two weeks postoperatively; there were no reports of vertebral artery injuries, infections, or wound dehiscence.

Limitations of the Goel-Harms construct are related to technical difficulty as well as the inherent risk associated with placing screws in the lateral masses of C1. This is due to immature bone, small bony structures, and extensive anatomical variability. In some patients, neurotomy of the spinal nerve must be performed to achieve suitable positioning of the screw in the lateral masses of C1 [12]. Based on these observations, Ni et al. propose arthrodesis using laminar hooks in C1 and transpedicular screws in C2 connected with rods for pediatric patients. C1 laminar hooks avoid the neurological risk caused by sublaminar cables passed under the C1 arch, as well as vascular injury arising from screw positioning within the C1 lateral masses [12]. A disadvantage of this construct is that the C1 level only has one fixation point with limited biomechanical stability; nevertheless, outcomes for five patients were reported as excellent, with fusion rates of 100% [12]. In line with this, we considered the use of laminar hooks in C1 as safe and effective for the management of atlantoaxial subluxation in pediatric patients.

To perform this technique, the C1 posterior arch is required to bear fixation with hooks. Patients with complete or incomplete congenital absence of the C1 posterior arch (1.5–5% of the population) [20] or those requiring its surgical removal cannot be considered for this type of surgery; these patients would undergo the original Goel-Harms technique. Posterior arch hypoplasia is a relative contraindication that is more commonly found in some patients with atlantoaxial subluxation or instability [20]. The presence of a short C2 pedicle may also limit screw placement, although Sim et al. considered the possibility of using these screws in such anatomical variants; they reported stable fixation using long and short pedicle screws with a low occurrence of vascular or neurological injury [21].

This construct may also be applicable to traumatic injuries, rheumatologic diseases, connective tissue disorders, and congenital malformations such as Chiari malformation, Down syndrome, and os odontoideum, in which C1–C2 fixation is required.

## Conclusion

Based on the outcome of the presented patient, we report an effective and safe alternative surgical construct—atlas laminar hooks, axis pedicle polyaxial screws, connecting rods, and bone allograft—in the pediatric population for the treatment of atlantoaxial subluxation and instability. A satisfying clinical result and fusion at the 36-month follow-up were achieved with this technique. As Grisel's syndrome is a rare diagnosis in children, it may be of interest to evaluate outcomes in children with atlantoaxial subluxation or instability secondary to traumatic injuries, rheumatologic diseases, connective tissue disorders, and congenital malformations such as Chiari malformation, Down syndrome, or os odontoideum, where C1–C2 fixation is required.
