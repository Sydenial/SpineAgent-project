# Intraosseous pseudomeningocele of the mobile spine: a case report and review of the literature

## Abstract

A 64-year-old male presented with back, left lateral thigh, and left knee pain lasting several months. MRI revealed multilevel degenerative disc disease and facet arthropathy, along with severe spinal canal stenosis from L1–L2 to L3–L4 and moderate stenosis at L4–L5. A T2 hyperintense lesion in the posterior aspect of the L1 vertebral body was noted, suggesting an intraosseous pseudomeningocele. A CT myelogram confirmed the lesion's contiguity with the cerebrospinal fluid and a defect in the posterior longitudinal ligament. The patient opted for non-operative management due to medical comorbidities.

**Keywords**: Radiograph, Magnetic resonance imaging, CT myelogram, Intraosseous pseudomeningocele

## Introduction

Spinal pseudomeningoceles were first described by Hyndman and Gerber in 1946, defined as a dural tear leading to CSF extravasation, typically posterior to the thecal sac. These lesions can occasionally result in intraosseous CSF collections. Pseudomeningoceles can originate congenitally, iatrogenically, or as a result of prior trauma. In this report, we discuss the presentation, imaging appearance, and literature surrounding a case of spinal intraosseous pseudomeningocele in a 64-year-old male.

## Case

A 64-year-old male with a history of congestive heart failure, type II diabetes, hypertension, coronary artery disease, and gout presented with left leg pain. This pain, radiating down the left anterior lateral thigh and knee, worsened over the past several months. MRI of the lumbar spine showed severe stenosis from L1–L2 to L3–L4, moderate stenosis at L4–L5, and a T2 hyperintense lesion at the posterior aspect of the L1 vertebral body. CT myelogram confirmed the diagnosis of an intraosseous pseudomeningocele at the L1 level.

## Discussion

Intraosseous pseudomeningoceles are rare lesions typically caused by trauma or surgical procedures. This case highlights an instance without prior surgery or trauma, making it an exceptionally rare occurrence. MRI and CT myelogram are crucial for diagnosing these lesions. Treatment usually involves surgical intervention, including debridement and dural repair. In this case, the patient opted for non-operative management due to the chronic and stable nature of the lesion.

## Conclusion

This report describes a rare case of an intraosseous pseudomeningocele involving the L1 vertebral body in a patient with no prior surgery or trauma. Intraosseous pseudomeningoceles are best diagnosed through CT myelogram. Surgical intervention remains the treatment of choice when necessary.
