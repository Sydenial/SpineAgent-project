# The Need for Multidimensional Stratification of Chronic Low Back Pain (LBP)

## Abstract

**Study Design:** Secondary analysis of a primary care cohort.

**Summary of Background Data:** Low back pain (LBP) is among the most frequent reasons for visits to primary care and is a major cause of disability worldwide. To improve treatment response and outcomes, primary care stratification systems have been proposed to provide more tailored treatment with the intent of improving outcomes; however, group-specific risk factors and outcomes remain incompletely characterized.

**Methods:** Stratification was performed by (1) dominant mechanical pain patterns, (2) risk of chronicity, and (3) disability severity. Multinomial logistic regression was used to test associations with age, sex, obesity, and comorbidity. Once identified, the remaining schemas were incorporated into the model.

**Results:** $N=970$; mean age 50 years (range: 18–93); 56% female. Stratification by pain pattern revealed the greatest variability in associations. Increasing age was associated with back-dominant extension and intermittent leg-dominant pain (OR: 1.02 and 1.06; $p<0.01$). Being male was associated with leg-dominant pain $(\mathrm{ORs}>2;\ p<0.01)$. Overweight/obesity was associated with extension-based leg-dominant pain $(\mathrm{OR}=2.6;\ p<0.02)$, and increasing comorbidity with extension-based back-dominant pain $(\mathrm{OR}=1.3;\ p<0.01)$. Severe disability was associated only with constant leg pain $(\mathrm{OR}=3.9;\ p<0.01)$, and high chronicity risk with extension-based leg-dominant pain $(\mathrm{OR}=0.4;\ p=0.03)$.

**Conclusion:** Dominant mechanical symptom stratification may enable further discrimination of a multidimensional stratification approach for chronic LBP.

**Level of Evidence:** 3

## Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) imposes a substantial burden through healthcare utilization and disability. At the primary care level, most current clinical practice guidelines dichotomize LBP into “specific” and “non-specific” presentations and advocate management strategies accordingly. More recently, stratification approaches—such as severity-based stratification of pain and disability—have been recommended, with the intent of improving outcomes through more tailored care.

However, LBP is heterogeneous. Multiple dimensions (mechanical pain pattern, disability severity, and risk of chronicity) may contribute independently to clinically meaningful subgrouping. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate whether common epidemiological variables and clinical factors contribute independently to grouping within a multidimensional stratification schema for chronic or persistent LBP.

## Materials and Methods

### Patients

Data were derived from patients who sought care from their primary care provider for persistent or recurrent chronic or subacute LBP and were referred to the Inter-professional Spine Assessment and Education Clinics (ISAEC; www.isaec.org).

ISAEC is an interdisciplinary shared-care model intended to provide diagnosis and stratified education and self-management recommendations. Based out of three cities in Ontario, Canada (Toronto, Hamilton, and Thunder Bay), participating primary care providers referred patients to ISAEC network providers. Referred patients were evaluated by geographically linked community-based, inter-professionally trained advanced practice clinicians (chiropractors and physiotherapists).

Patients were recruited from November 2012 to February 2014, completed a health questionnaire, and underwent a standard physical assessment at the initial visit. The study was approved by the University Health Network Research Ethics Board (12-5477-BE; 14-7776-BE).

### ISAEC Stratification Schema (performed prior to diagnostic imaging)

#### LBP pattern

Grounded in the work of Wilson et al. and Hall et al. $^{10,15}$, patients with mechanical “non-specific” LBP were categorized as:

- **BD-F:** back-dominant pain aggravated by flexion (i.e., worse with sitting, bending, or flexing the lumbar spine)
- **BD-E:** back-dominant pain aggravated by extension (i.e., worse with standing/walking and better with sitting or lumbar flexion)
- **C-LD:** constant leg-dominant pain (i.e., lumbar radiculopathy)
- **I-LD:** intermittent leg-dominant pain (i.e., neurogenic intermittent claudication)

Intertester reliability demonstrated $\kappa=0.61$ $(p<0.001)$ with overall agreement of 78.9%. Therapist experience level did not significantly affect reliability. Among experienced therapists, $\kappa=0.61$ $(p<0.001)$ with 80.2% agreement; among novice therapists, $\kappa=0.60$ $(p<0.001)$ with 76.9% agreement. **BD-F** was chosen as the referent group for analytical modeling.

From an etiologic perspective, ISAEC operationalized these patterns as the most likely source of dominant symptoms:  
$\mathrm{BD\!-\!F}=\mathrm{discogenic\ LBP}$, $\mathrm{BD\!-\!E}=\mathrm{facetogenic\ LBP}$, $\mathrm{C\!-\!LD}=\mathrm{radiculopathy\ due\ to\ disc\ herniation}$, and $\mathrm{I\!-\!LD}=\mathrm{neurogenic\ claudication\ due\ to\ spinal\ stenosis}$.

#### Severity of disability (health questionnaire)

The Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) was used. Established cutoff values were applied to stratify severity: **0%–20% minimal**, **20%–40% moderate**, and **≥40% severe disability**. Minimal disability was used as the referent group.

#### Risk of chronicity

The Keele STarT Back Screening Tool (SBST; www.keele.ac.uk/sbst/startbacktool/) is a 9-item tool assessing physical and psychosocial factors and bothersomeness. It groups patients into **low**, **medium**, and **high** risk of poor outcome (persistent disabling symptoms). For analytical purposes, **low risk** was designated the referent group.

### Additional study measures

The health questionnaire collected age, sex, height, and weight, from which body mass index (BMI; kg/m²) was calculated. BMI was categorized as overweight/obese $(\mathrm{BMI}\ge 25)$ versus normal (BMI < 25).

An 11-point numerical pain rating scale was used to capture back and leg pain at rest and with activity. Anchors were “No pain” (0) and “Worst possible pain” (10). Overall back and leg pain intensity scores were based on the maximum response among the two respective items.

### Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics are presented for each study measure overall and by clinical pain pattern subgroup (owing to subsequent findings). Comparisons across pain pattern subgroups were made using analysis of variance or chi-square tests.

Multinomial logistic regression modeling evaluated associations between patient factors and stratification outcomes. The remaining two stratification systems were additionally entered into the model as potential correlates, along with back and leg pain intensity scores.

## Results

### Overall sample characteristics

The sample included 970 patients. By dominant pain pattern, 42% were classified as BD-F, 31% BD-E, 17% C-LD, and 10% I-LD. By chronicity risk, 24% were deemed high risk, 31% medium risk, and 45% low risk. Overall mean age was 50 years (range: 18–93).

### Differentiation between stratification schema

Multinomial regression analyses indicated the greatest variability in associations for the dominant pain pattern subgroups, where age, sex, BMI, and comorbidity count were each significantly associated with subgrouping. In contrast, BMI and comorbidity count were not similarly associated with disability severity or chronicity risk. Given this, dominant pain pattern was treated as the primary outcome of the final multivariable multinomial logistic model.

### Sample characteristics by pain pattern subgroupings

The I-LD and BD-E groups had higher mean ages (63 and 52 years, respectively) than the BD-F and C-LD groups (46 and 47 years). The proportion of females was highest in BD-E and BD-F (61% and 59%) versus 46% in C-LD and I-LD.

In I-LD, 76% were categorized as overweight/obese compared to 67% in C-LD, 59% in BD-E, and 53% in BD-F. I-LD and BD-E had higher mean comorbidity counts (1.9 and 1.4) compared with BD-F and C-LD (mean 1.0).

A similar proportion of patients with severe disability was found for BD-F and BD-E (33%–35%), compared with 42% in I-LD and 58% in C-LD. As expected, mean back pain intensity scores were higher (worse) in back-dominant pain groups and mean leg pain intensity scores were higher (worse) in leg-dominant pain groups.

### Examination of pain pattern subgroupings with multivariable adjusted analyses

From the adjusted model, increasing age was associated with increased odds of being in BD-E and I-LD (OR: 1.02 and 1.06; $p<0.01$) compared with BD-F. Men had odds more than twice that of women for being in C-LD and I-LD $(p<0.01)$.

Being overweight/obese (compared with normal BMI) was associated with increased odds of being in the I-LD group $(p<0.02)$ compared with BD-F. Each unit increase in comorbidity count was associated with a 27% increased odds of being in BD-E $(p<0.01)$ compared with BD-F.

Higher (worse) back pain intensity was associated with decreased odds of being in leg-dominant groups compared with BD-F, while higher (worse) leg pain intensity was associated with increased odds of being in leg-dominant groups compared with BD-F.

Severe disability was only associated with increased odds of being in C-LD relative to BD-F $(\mathrm{OR}=3.9;\ p<0.01)$, while high chronicity risk was associated with decreased odds of being in I-LD relative to BD-F $(\mathrm{OR}=0.36;\ p=0.03)$. The multiple degree-of-freedom test for the overall effect of chronicity risk was not significant $(p>0.30)$.

## Discussion

Our results demonstrate significant heterogeneity in a primary care population with persistent LBP. Stratification by dominant pain pattern revealed the greatest variability in associations with common epidemiological factors. From adjusted analyses, increasing age was associated with greater odds of having back-dominant extension (BD-E) and intermittent leg-dominant (I-LD) symptoms.

Stratifying at the primary care level provides an opportunity for the development and delivery of more targeted clinical assessment and management strategies for chronic LBP. A multidimensional approach may better reflect the complexity of chronic LBP than relying on a single stratification dimension.

### Study considerations and limitations

This was a cross-sectional study evaluating associations within a stratification schema rather than testing effectiveness of specific interventions. Further study with more comprehensive consideration of patient and societal factors is required to explore the distinctness of the potential subgroups identified. Although uncommon, in some instances it may be difficult to establish which symptom area is most limiting, which may affect determination of the dominant pattern. The concurrent presence of some conditions may be under-recognized, and the duration of conditions was not considered. Data in these regards were not available for study.

## Conclusion

LBP patients are heterogeneous and may require primary care-initiated stratification beyond symptom severity alone. Distinct and readily identifiable epidemiological profiles were observed across dominant pain pattern groups, against a backdrop of comparatively limited variability in disability severity and chronicity risk. This supports the need for multidimensional stratification to better inform clinical care and to advance clinical and epidemiological research.
