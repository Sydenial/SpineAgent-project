# A Case of Fourth Atlanto-Axial Facet Joint: Anatomical Description

## Abstract

**Purpose** To report a rare fourth atlanto-axial joint.  
**Methods** A cadaveric specimen of a young male adult from occiput to C4 was dissected for anatomical study of the craniocervical region. A true fourth atlanto-axial joint was confirmed, and its morphological characteristics were described.  
**Results** The fourth atlanto-axial joint is rarely seen. The possible embryogenesis is discussed. This case raises an anatomical possibility of a new variant in this region.  
**Conclusions** A unique case with an anatomically proven fourth atlanto-axial joint is reported. This anomaly can lead to misdiagnosis. CT scan coupled with MRI can facilitate accurate diagnosis.

**Keywords** Anomaly · Facet joint · Atlantoaxial

## Introduction

The articulations between the atlas and axis vertebrae are composed of one midline atlanto-odontoid joint and two lateral atlantoaxial (AA) joints. Congenital osseous anomalies in this region, such as occipitalization of the atlas, os odontoideum, and basilar invagination, can lead to an increased risk of segmental instability and neurological implications. To the best of our knowledge, there have been only two case reports of a fourth AA joint confirmed during an operation [1, 2]. In this report, we present a true accessory facet joint between the posterior C1 arch and the C2 lamina during an anatomical study of the lateral AA joint. The possible embryogenesis of this anomaly and its clinical significance are discussed in light of the literature.

## Anatomical Report

A fresh cadaveric specimen consisting of the skull base and C1 to C4 was provided by the Department of Anatomy at Southern Medical University. The specimen was from a Chinese young male adult. An anatomical and biomechanical study was conducted on the lateral AA joint. The skin was removed first, and the posterior cervical muscles were separated in layers until the occiput, posterior arch of C1, and lamina of C2 were exposed. At this stage, a surgical microscope was introduced (magnification $5–10\times$). A subperiosteal dissection was performed along the posterior arch of C1 to the lateral mass to expose the vertebral artery. Dissection was then continued along the posterior lamina of C2 to expose the lateral AA joint.

The C2 nerve root on the right side was normal as it coursed laterally between the posterior C1 arch and the C2 lamina. On the left side, however, the C2 nerve root could not initially be confirmed. After the joint capsule was opened, an extremely small facet joint on the left side was demonstrated compared to that on the right. At this point, the possibility of a congenital anomaly of the facet joint was proposed.

All muscles were then completely removed. The oropharynx and longus capitis muscles were transected. The anterior longitudinal ligament between C1 and C2 was cut. The transverse ligament of the midline atlanto-odontoid joint was sectioned via the foramen magnum from a superior direction, and the tectorial membrane was cut from the posterior direction. The joint capsule of the lateral AA joint was then carefully opened without damaging the joint space. At this stage, the atlas was completely separated from the axis.

The fourth AA joint was located between the posterior C1 arch and the C2 lamina on the left side, situated at the pars interarticularis. This joint was parallel to the lateral AA joint, with synovium present instead of a typical intralaminar space. The fourth AA joint was oval in shape and smaller than the lateral AA joint. Normal endplate cartilage and a joint capsule were observed. The left C2 nerve root exited between the lateral AA joint and the fourth AA joint. No abnormality of the lateral AA joints or the odontoid process was observed. No arthritis or joint degeneration was identified.

## Discussion

The existence of a fourth AA joint is a rare congenital anomaly, and its embryological pathogenesis remains unclear. The formation and development of the spine occur during the fourth week of gestation. Errors in cellular proliferation, migration, differentiation, and segmentation can result in congenital osseous anomalies. During C1–C2 segmentation, the dense zone of the lateral C1 sclerotome forms the posterior arch and lateral masses of C1, the hypochordal bow forms the anterior C1 arch, and the dense zone of the lateral C2 sclerotome forms the C2 lamina and superior articular processes [3]. A possible explanation for this anomaly is normal segmentation of the C1–C2 sclerotomes followed by the formation of a predifferentiated group of cells that develop into an interzone posterior to the normal lateral AA joint, ultimately forming a true accessory synovial joint.

Accessory articulations are most commonly reported in the sacroiliac region. In the craniocervical region, they have been described in the midline atlanto-occipital joint [4]. Osteoarthritis has been speculated to play a role in the formation of osseous contact zones and hyaline cartilage between the basiocciput, atlas, and axis. Salunke et al. reported pseudofacet joint formation between C1 and C2 in some cases of congenital AA dislocation [5], hypothesizing genetic aberrations of HOX genes and abnormal C1–C2 movement as contributing factors. In the present case, no joint degeneration or osteophyte formation was noted. The joint surface was covered with smooth endplate cartilage and surrounded by a joint capsule, suggesting a congenital true facet joint rather than an acquired or degenerative process.

Only two other cases of a fourth AA joint with similar characteristics have been reported [1, 2]. These young patients experienced dorsal spinal cord compression, and their symptoms improved after resection of the abnormal joint. In all cases, including the present one, the fourth AA joint was situated posterior to the C2 pars interarticularis and was covered by hyaline cartilage.

Congenital facet disorders are typically asymptomatic and may be discovered incidentally during routine radiographic examination. However, the AA joints account for nearly 60% of cervical spine rotation [6]. It is conceivable that the presence of a fourth AA joint alters load-bearing at the AA region by increasing the contact surface area. Increased stability between C1 and C2 may transfer shearing forces to the subaxial cervical spine, potentially leading to cervical neuralgia or degeneration. In cases where spinal cord compression results from accessory joint hypertrophy, surgical removal may be necessary.

Because of its rarity, this anomaly has a high potential for radiographic misinterpretation and inappropriate clinical intervention. In recent years, direct posterior reduction and fusion techniques have been widely used to treat AA dislocation. The presence of a fourth AA joint may obscure visualization of the true lateral AA joint and cause confusion during C2 pedicle screw insertion.

The bony and neural anatomical anomalies in this case were identified during cadaveric dissection. Although limited by the absence of radiological and histopathological evaluation, this report highlights the possibility of a previously underrecognized anatomical variant that should be considered during surgical procedures in the craniocervical region.

## Conclusion

We report a unique case with an anatomically proven fourth atlanto-axial joint. Spine surgeons unfamiliar with this anomaly may misdiagnose it as an osteoma, fracture, or other pathology. CT scanning combined with MRI can facilitate accurate diagnosis. This anomaly should be kept in mind during surgical interventions in the craniocervical region.
