# Acute Complete Paraplegia of an 8-Year-Old Girl Caused by Spinal Cord Infarction Following Minor Trauma Complicated With Longitudinal Spinal Cord Signal Change

## Abstract
Spinal cord infarction following minor trauma in pediatric patients is rare and can cause severe paralysis. Fibrocartilaginous embolism (FCE) is a possible diagnosis; however, consecutive magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) reports are scarce. We report an 8-year-old girl with acute complete paraplegia and spinal cord infarction with longitudinal spinal cord signal change after minor trauma. She presented 2 h after a handstand fall with complete loss of pain, temperature, and vibration sensation below the bilateral anterior thighs. Four hours after trauma, T2-weighted MRI showed no spinal cord compression or vertebral body signal change. She was managed with bed rest and rehabilitation. At 1 week, T2-weighted MRI revealed longitudinal signal abnormality from T11 to the C6 level with ring-shaped signal change, and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) showed increased signal below C6. At 2 weeks, T2*-weighted images showed minor bleeding at the T11 level and spinal cord edema below C6. At 4 weeks, a minor lesion remained at C6, but spinal cord atrophy was evident at T11 without disc signal change. At 13 weeks, the cervical cord was almost intact, and severe atrophy persisted at T11. At 1 year, complete paraplegia with sensory loss below T11 remained. Clinical presentation, exclusion of other etiologies, and serial MRI suggested FCE at T11 with pencil-shaped softening, with additional minor cervical cord injury. This case highlights the utility of consecutive MRI in double-level spinal cord lesions with longitudinal signal change.

## Case Report
An 8-year-old girl fell during a handstand, striking her back and neck in hyperflexion. She stood and walked immediately but experienced minor back pain and bilateral leg weakness. Two hours later, she presented with complete paraplegia. Past medical history was unremarkable; family history was notable only for an uncle with complete paralysis due to spina bifida. Sensory testing showed absence of pinprick, light touch, pressure, and vibration bilaterally below the T11 level. Four hours after trauma, T2-weighted MRI showed no spinal cord compression or vertebral body signal change. No definite spinal cord signal change was seen on sagittal images (Fig. 1a, b). She denied upper extremity symptoms but had bradycardia (heart rate 20-40). Because a minor cervical spinal cord lesion and spinal shock could not be excluded, she was admitted to a pediatric intensive care unit for close monitoring, including respiratory function.

Neurological findings persisted the next day. Lumbar spine MRI showed increased T2 signal within the cord from T11 cranially. Gadolinium-enhanced MRI did not reveal tumors or inflammatory disorders (Fig. 2). CT angiography showed no vascular malformation. Laboratory studies, including cerebrospinal fluid testing, showed no evidence of infection, autoimmune, inflammatory, or coagulation abnormalities.

On day 7, cervical MRI showed longitudinal T2 hyperintensity extending cranially to the C6 level with ring-shaped signal change, and DWI showed increased signal below C6 (Fig. 3). Apart from nighttime bradycardia, her general condition was good; vital signs stabilized by day 11. At 2 weeks, T2*-weighted images showed minor bleeding at the T11 level and spinal cord edema below C6. At 4 weeks, a minor lesion persisted at C6 (Fig. 1b), while spinal cord atrophy was evident at T11 without disc signal change. At 13 weeks, the cervical spinal cord was almost intact, and severe spinal cord atrophy persisted at T11 (Fig. 1a). At 1 year, complete paraplegia remained with sensory loss below T11.

## Differential Diagnosis
The clinical course suggested transverse spinal cord infarction at T11 following minor back and neck trauma. Spinal cord infarction after minor trauma can occur in children without vertebral fracture or dislocation [1]. Differential diagnoses of acute progressive paraplegia in children include acute transverse myelitis, Guillain-Barre syndrome, anterior spinal cord syndrome, spinal cord injury without radiographic abnormalities (SCIWORA), and FCE [2]. Acute transverse myelitis and Guillain-Barre syndrome were excluded by cerebrospinal fluid studies, including viral testing. Anterior spinal cord syndrome was considered less likely because CT angiography showed no embolic changes and there was loss of vibration sense. SCIWORA is defined as spinal cord injury in children without fracture or dislocation [3]; patients should not be classed as SCIWORA if any pathology is detected on MRI [4]. Transverse cord ischemia following minor trauma was observed, and FCE can affect both anterior and posterior spinal cord circulation [5]. A review of pediatric spinal cord infarction after minor trauma concluded that FCE is a principal etiological factor [6].

This case matched a schematic approach to diagnosing FCE [7]. After excluding traumatic, compressive, and inflammatory causes, it met one major criterion (clear vascular distribution on MRI) and three minor criteria: new-onset neck or back pain, progression to nadir in less than 4-8 h, and an initially unremarkable spinal cord MRI with subsequent evolution of an intraparenchymal lesion [7]. Because there are no noninvasive tests to confirm FCE [8], consecutive MRI may help elucidate its pathology.

## Diagnostic Imaging and Pathology
The absence of T2 abnormalities in acute ischemia may reflect the time course of cytotoxic edema, which takes hours to develop; repeated MRI can then reveal abnormalities [9]. In the subacute phase, spinal cord ischemia typically manifests as focal cord swelling and pencil-like T2 hyperintensities [9, 10].

For FCE, emboli are generally thought to originate from the intervertebral disc, but the route into the vasculature is debated. One hypothesis is lateral disc rupture damaging an adjacent radicular artery with entry of disc fragments into the arterial circulation. Another posits that high axial loading increases intradiscal pressure without rupture, injecting semifluid nucleus pulposus into small arteries with retrograde spread to a radicular artery, causing ischemia [11]. Disc signal change or Schmorl nodes on MRI have been reported as characteristic for FCE [8], but degenerative disc disease and Schmorl nodes were observed in only 32% and 17% of FCE cases, respectively [7]. In this case, serial MRI showed no disc signal change or Schmorl nodes.

At 1 week, we considered the T11 lesion a spinal cord infarction and did not perform thoracolumbar DWI. Because the cervical diagnosis was uncertain, cervical DWI was obtained and showed high signal; however, the cervical abnormality largely resolved by 4 weeks, coinciding with resolution of bradycardia. Early restricted diffusivity on DWI can support the diagnosis of spinal cord infarction by detecting cytotoxic edema [10], but technical limitations (motion, small cord size) constrain its use [9].

Considering the injury and serial MRI, the main lesion—spinal cord infarction with subsequent atrophy—was at T11, with a simultaneous minor cervical injury. When double-level spinal cord lesions occur, serial MRI can be useful in understanding the pathology. The T2 hyperintensity in the cervical cord may have represented ascending edema or necrotic tissue from the T11 level via the central cord, a pattern described as pencil-shaped softening. Pencil-shaped softening is considered an expansion of necrotic tissue possibly caused by spinal cord infarction; however, it does not always produce overt neurological deficits [12, 13]. In this case, it diminished by 4 weeks.

## Outcome and Follow-up
The patient was managed in an intensive care unit to prepare for potential progression of paralysis, including respiratory muscle involvement, given reported mortality from thoracolumbar lesions [14] and cases with damage extending to the entire spinal cord after minor trauma [15].

Most children with complete and severe lesions on admission do not recover [16]. At 1 year, complete paraplegia, loss of bladder control, and sensory loss below T11 persisted, and follow-up MRI showed severe spinal cord atrophy. Reisner et al. reported that follow-up MRI in pediatric FCE often shows mild atrophy and cystic myelomalacia, with ASIA grade improving from B to D [2]. The severity of spinal cord atrophy may correlate with neurological outcome. This case documents consecutive MRI in a double-level spinal cord lesion with longitudinal signal change; further case series are needed to improve diagnostic accuracy.
