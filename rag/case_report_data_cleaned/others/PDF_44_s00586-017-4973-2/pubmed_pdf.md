# Excision of an anterior intradural arachnoid cyst of the cervical spine through central corpectomy approach

## Abstract
Anterior cervical intradural arachnoid cyst is a rare entity which has been mostly approached posteriorly, commonly resulting in incomplete resection. Incomplete resection is associated with recurrence; hence, we describe the anterior central corpectomy approach with complete neurologic recovery in a twenty-year-old with an anterior cervical intradural arachnoid cyst in front of the third and fourth cervical vertebra, who had presented with spastic quadriparesis.

## Introduction
Intradural extramedullary arachnoid cysts (Type III meningeal cysts) [1] are rare entities, found mostly in the thoracic spine in a posterior location [2,3]. Anterior cervical intradural arachnoid cysts are extremely infrequent with only 26 cases reported in the English literature till 2013 [4–8]. Most of these cases have been treated by a posterior approach by laminectomy and cyst fenestration, with only four previous reports of anterior cervical approach, corpectomy, and excision of the lesion. A 20-year-old with an anterior cervical intradural arachnoid cyst at the C3/4 level with spastic quadriparesis was operated through a central corpectomy approach to full neurologic recovery; consequently, the surgical steps and the pros and cons of the various approaches are discussed here.

## Case Report
A 20-year-old right-handed gentleman presented with complaints of pain at the nape of the neck for a year and progressive spastic quadriparesis of one month duration, with intact sphincters and no obvious history of trauma. Neck movements were moderately restricted in all directions, and pain and temperature sensation was decreased markedly below the neck; however, joint position and vibration sensation were preserved. Power was MRC grade 2/5 in all limbs; deep tendon reflexes were brisk with presence of ankle clonus and upgoing plantars.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the cervical spine showed a well-defined, non-enhancing intradural extramedullary cystic lesion at the C3/C4 level with posterior spinal cord compression, suggestive of an arachnoid cyst. In view of a severely compromised cord, awake fiber-optic bronchoscopic intubation was performed, as any undue cord manipulation could make the patient quadriplegic. The neck was adequately extended for upper cervical exposure, the position of the lesion confirmed by C-arm, and the patient was then put to sleep.

A right transverse skin-crease incision was made, the platysma undermined, and the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle mobilized to expose the anterior bodies of C3 down to C5. The C3–C4 disc space was confirmed radiologically. A Cloward retractor was placed below the mobilized medial edges of the longus colli muscles bilaterally to retract the esophagus and trachea medially and the carotid sheath laterally. A Cloward drill (13.5 mm) was centered on the C3–C4 disc space to drill down to the posterior cortical plates of the adjacent C3 and C4 vertebral bodies, and the gutter was widened on both sides using a high-speed drill.

A 1-mm angled Kerrison rongeur was used under the microscope to remove the posterior cortical plate, leaving a circumferential posterior cortical rim to prevent posterior slippage of the planned autologous tricortical iliac crest bone graft. The posterior longitudinal ligament and dura were opened longitudinally and held back with stay sutures. The thin-walled, tense cyst was exposed and aspirated, yielding clear cerebrospinal fluid. The collapsed cyst wall was mobilized by dividing intervening fibrous strands adherent to the arachnoid, and most of the cyst was excised, leaving a small portion densely adherent to the cord along the midline to avoid injury to the anterior spinal artery.

A watertight primary dural closure was performed with continuous 7-0 polypropylene sutures under the microscope. An autologous iliac crest bone graft of appropriate dimensions was harvested, trimmed, and countersunk into position under manual neck traction. Plates and screws were used to secure the graft and prevent anterior displacement. The wound was closed over a suction drain, and the neck immobilized with a Philadelphia collar.

In the immediate postoperative period, neurology worsened temporarily, but the patient made steady progress and was walking with support by the fourth postoperative day. Postoperative MRI revealed complete resolution of the arachnoid cyst, and at six-month follow-up, the myelopathic symptoms had completely resolved.

## Discussion
Spinal intradural arachnoid cysts are mostly present in the thoracic region (80%) and rarely in the cervical (15%) and lumbar (5%) regions, with approximately 20% located anterior to the spinal cord [9]. They are usually congenital, arising from sequestration of an enclosed chamber in the loose mesenchymal tissue around the neural tube that normally differentiates into pia and arachnoid mater [10,11]. Less commonly, they may be acquired following trauma or meningitis secondary to subarachnoid hemorrhage, adhesions, contrast media, anesthetic agents, spinal surgery [12], or tumors [13]. In the present patient, there was no history of trauma or meningitis.

Neck pain is often overlooked, and gradually progressive myelopathy is the most common presenting complaint [3]. Delay in surgical intervention may lead to rapid neurological deterioration, as seen in this case.

Successful treatment of spinal arachnoid cysts generally requires total excision, which is unlikely with a posterior approach when the cyst is anteriorly located [2,8]. Recurrence has been reported after incomplete resection [14]. Muhammedrezai et al. reported successful treatment of an anterior cervical arachnoid cyst via corpectomy after failed posterior surgery [15]. In the present case, a posterior approach would have risked injury to the cervical cord and the C3 and C4 nerve roots, which are essential for respiratory function; hence, an anterior approach was chosen.

Although posterior approaches such as laminectomy, laminotomy, fenestration, and shunting have been described [17], they may not provide adequate exposure for complete excision of anteriorly placed cervical arachnoid cysts. An anterior cervical approach offers direct visualization of lesions anterior to the spinal cord and allows for safer and more complete resection. Previous reports have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of this approach, supporting its use when feasible.

## Conclusion
Anterior cervical intradural arachnoid cysts are rare lesions that may cause progressive myelopathy. When the cyst is located anterior to the spinal cord, an anterior cervical corpectomy approach provides adequate exposure for complete excision and may result in excellent neurological recovery with minimal risk of recurrence.
