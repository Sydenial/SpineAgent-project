# The etiologically vonital report of three monozygotic twin cases

## Abstract

Congenital scoliosis presents a unique opportunity to explore the relative roles of genetic versus environmental etiologic factors. The authors describe three cases of congenital scoliosis in monozygotic twins. The first pair included two 4-year-old girls presenting with mirror curves, one of whom had an associated type I Chiari malformation. The second pair consisted of a 4-year-old girl who presented with thoracic scoliosis, a T10–11 hemivertebra, and multilevel failure of segmentation in the lumbar spine, while her identical sibling was unaffected. The third pair included a 4-month-old boy with T9 and L4 hemivertebrae whose brother was also unaffected. All three cases were managed conservatively with observation and remained asymptomatic throughout the duration of follow-up. There were no associations with extraspinal deformities, although one patient presented with concomitant type I Chiari malformation. The variable presentation of congenital scoliosis in a genetically identical population highlights the complexity of its development, likely involving both environmental factors and a genetic predisposition.

## Introduction

Congenital scoliosis (CS) is a relatively common congenital spinal deformity with an incidence of 0.5–1 per 1000 births [1]. Its natural history varies, with approximately 25% of cases remaining non-progressive, 25% mildly progressive, and 50% ultimately requiring treatment. Disease progression depends on the type of anomaly, the site of occurrence, and the individual’s overall growth potential [2]. Preventative efforts remain difficult because the etiology of CS is likely multifactorial and poorly understood. Current theories suggest that CS results from insults to the developing fetal spine during the fifth to eighth weeks of gestation. Whether these insults are genetic, environmental, or a combination of both remains unclear despite ongoing investigation [3]. Monozygotic twins, though poorly represented in the literature, may provide unique insight into the interaction of these factors. This report presents three cases of congenital scoliosis in monozygotic twins to add to the limited existing literature.

## Case Report

All patients’ families provided consent for reporting their cases. These cases have not been previously presented or published elsewhere.

### Pair 1

A pair of monozygotic twin girls were born at 34 weeks’ gestation and presented at the age of 4 years and 9 months after spinal deformities were found incidentally. Prenatal and birth histories were unremarkable. On physical examination, both twins demonstrated mild thoracic curves with otherwise normal findings. Radiographs of twin A revealed a right T1 hemivertebra and a left T8 hemivertebra with metameric shift, both fused to adjacent vertebrae. Twin B’s radiographs demonstrated a left T1 hemivertebra and a right T7 hemivertebra, also with metameric shift and fusion to adjacent vertebrae. In both cases, overall coronal and sagittal balance was preserved. Magnetic resonance imaging of twin B demonstrated a type I Chiari malformation. After consultation with a pediatric neurosurgeon, the decision was made to pursue careful observation without surgical intervention.

### Pair 2

A second pair of monozygotic twin girls were born at 31 weeks’ gestation and presented at 4 years and 10 months of age after twin A was referred for congenital scoliosis noted at 2 months of age. On examination, twin A demonstrated a left lower thoracic rib hump, with a midline plumb line and an otherwise normal examination. Screening examination and radiographs of twin B were normal. Radiographs of twin A demonstrated a left T10–T11 hemivertebra, block vertebra with failure of segmentation from L1 to L3, and a mild thoracic scoliosis from T9 to T11 measuring 35 degrees. The decision was made to observe curve progression. Over five years of follow-up, twin A remained asymptomatic, well aligned, and without significant curve progression on repeat imaging.

### Pair 3

A pair of monozygotic twin boys presented at 4 months of age after twin B was evaluated for congenital torticollis. On examination, twin B demonstrated a lumbar hump and a visible right sternocleidomastoid contracture. Radiographs revealed congenital scoliosis with a left T9 hemivertebra and a fused right L4 hemivertebra. Twin A was unaffected, with normal examination and imaging. The decision was made to manage twin B with close clinical and radiographic follow-up.

## Discussion

Monozygotic twins provide a valuable model for examining the relative contributions of genetic and environmental factors in the development of congenital scoliosis. Despite this, such cases are rarely reported in the literature [4–12]. In this report, three cases of congenital scoliosis in monozygotic twins are presented. Two involved only one affected twin, suggesting a potential role for extrinsic factors, while one involved mirror-image curves, suggesting intrinsic influences or a combination of genetic and environmental etiologies.

When only one monozygotic twin is affected, it is reasonable to suspect an extrinsic insult rather than a purely genetic cause. Various environmental factors, including hypoxia, carbon monoxide exposure, valproic acid, hyperthermia, and maternal alcohol consumption, have been associated with congenital scoliosis [13–19]. Experimental models have demonstrated that gestational hypoxia during critical periods of somitogenesis can induce vertebral and rib malformations [13–15, 20]. These effects may be mediated through disruption of fibroblast growth factor signaling and downstream Notch pathway activity, both of which are essential for coordinated somitogenesis [22–24].

Hypoxia during early vertebral development also affects vascularization and the distribution of intersegmental arteries, which are required for proper resegmentation and chondrification. Abnormalities in this process can impair vertebral body formation and result in deformity [21]. Variability in vascular distribution may explain why transient hypoxic insults affect only one twin or one side of the spine. If such asymmetry occurs at the same vertebral level bilaterally, mirror-image curves may result. While this mechanism remains speculative, the presence of mirror curves also raises the possibility of cytogenetic contributions to the development of congenital scoliosis.

## Conclusion

This report adds three cases to the limited literature on congenital scoliosis in monozygotic twins. One case demonstrated mirror curves with associated intraspinal pathology, while two cases involved a single affected twin. Together, these findings support the involvement of both genetic and environmental factors in the development of congenital scoliosis. Further epidemiologic and molecular investigations are needed to better understand how these factors interact during early spinal development and contribute to this common congenital deformity.
