# Anterior cervical intradural arachnoid cyst, a rare cause of spinal cord compression: a case report with video systematic literature review

## Abstract

**Purpose**: Mostly seen at the thoracic level, arachnoid cysts are a very rare cause of cervical spinal cord compression. Generally treated by laminectomy and cyst fenestration, this approach does not allow removing the cyst in its entirety without manipulating the weakened spinal cord. The aim of this report is to present the case of a cervical intradural arachnoid cyst surgically removed by an anterior approach with corporectomy.

**Methods**: This case involves an 18-year-old amateur boxer presenting with a voluminous cervical intradural anterior arachnoid cyst, extending from C2 to C5. Symptoms were cervical pain, quadriparesis, and clumsiness of both arms which appeared just after a traffic accident. An anterior approach was chosen, through a C5 corporectomy.

**Results**: The patient fully recovered from his sensitive symptoms at discharge and from his motor symptoms 6 weeks later. Early as well as 3-years post-operatively, MRI confirmed expansion of the spinal cord without any centro-medullar signal. The patient remained asymptomatic 3 years after surgery.

**Conclusion**: Using an anterior approach is a safe procedure that allows resection of a cervical arachnoid cyst without manipulating the weakened spinal cord, while providing the best possible view.

**Keywords**: Anterior approach, Arachnoid cyst, Cervical corporectomy, Intradural, Spinal cord

## Introduction

Arachnoid cysts are rarely symptomatic and represent a rare cause of spinal cord compression. They correspond essentially to type 3 of Nabors' classification (intradural cyst). Only 17 cases of spinal cord compression by cervical anterior intradural arachnoid cysts have been reported. Since they are symptomatic, these cysts must be treated. Three different options have been described: two surgical and one percutaneous under MRI guidance. Surgically, most authors performed a posterior approach. After laminectomy, the cyst, anterior to the cord, was punctured and partially resected. Only one case of an anterior approach with complete resection has been reported.

We present the case of a young patient operated by anterior approach, with a C5 corporectomy. He experienced spinal cord compression due to a cervical anterior arachnoid cyst. This report is followed by a systematic literature review.

## Case Report

### History of the Disease

An 18-year-old man, amateur boxer, was admitted to the emergency department for cervical pain, diffuse paresthesia, and weakness of both arms. History shows that he had been suffering from cervical pain for 3 months, poorly relieved by step 2 analgesics. Two weeks prior, he presented diffuse paresthesiae and clumsiness of both hands just after a traffic accident. At that time, standard X-rays of the cervical spine were interpreted as normal.

### Clinical Examination and Pre-operative Investigations

The clinical examination showed a deficit in the profound sensibility of both legs. He had clumsiness of both hands and a deficit quoted at 4/5 of the right thumb and index. He had no sign of pyramidal irritation.

A cervical spine CT scan was performed initially. It showed an enlargement of the spinal canal from C2 to C4, and on parenchymal windows, a hypodense collection extending from C2 to C5, pressing the spinal cord flat against the laminae.

The diagnosis of anterior cervical intradural compressive arachnoid cyst, with the spinal cord pressed against the laminae, was confirmed by MRI.

### Surgery

An antero-lateral approach was chosen to avoid manipulating the weakened spinal cord and provide a better view. After a standard anterior cervical spine approach, C4–C5, C5-C6 discectomies, and C5 corporectomy were performed under an operative microscope. The anterior longitudinal ligament was thoroughly resected from C4 to C6, and the dura mater was vertically opened. The cyst, which was under pressure in the intradural compartment, immediately came out of the dural incision, facilitating its wall dissection. The cyst was extracted with gentle traction after all adherences were released and the cyst was opened to empty it. The last arachnoid bridles were coagulated and sectioned to visualize the emptied sub-arachnoid space, and a good flow of the cerebrospinal fluid was seen in front of the newly decompressed spinal cord.

Closure of the dura was performed with interrupted silk sutures reinforced with biological glue. Reconstruction was performed using an iliac graft with a plate. Further information about the surgery is available in the video.

### Early Post-operative Evaluation

Post-operative MRI showed expansion of the spinal cord without any centro-medullar hypersignal. The cerebrospinal fluid's flow was recovered at the anterior part of the canal, as shown by flow MR sequences. Histopathological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of a benign arachnoid cyst.

The post-operative phase was marked by a regression of the paresthesia at discharge. Complete motor recovery was achieved 6 weeks later. The patient presented a post-operative Claude Bernard Horner syndrome, which completely regressed within 3 months.

### Later Post-operative Evaluation: 3-year Follow-up

The patient underwent clinical check-ups every year after surgery, and post-operative imaging at 3 years. At this time, he remained asymptomatic, and X-rays and MRI performed 3 years after surgery showed a well-positioned reconstruction plate, with no signs of pseudo-arthrosis or disc degeneration. However, T2 CISS sequences showed a small cyst residuum, probably communicating with the subarachnoid spaces, but non-compressive, with no mass effect on the spinal cord and no centro-medullar hypersignal. There was still a good cerebrospinal fluid flow at the anterior part of the canal.

## Discussion and Literature Review

Spinal arachnoid cysts are most commonly found posteriorly and in the thoracic region. Only 15% are located at the cervical level. The pathophysiology of these cysts remains unclear, but they are thought to be idiopathic in most cases. Some are post-traumatic, post-infectious (meningitis), or congenital. Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain their formation, including a defect in the dura mater trapping cerebrospinal fluid and the effects of anti-reflux valves.

The majority of reported cases involve motor symptoms, such as progressive para- or quadriparesis. In this case, however, sensitive symptoms led to the discovery of the cyst. No preferential localization was found, as all levels from C0 to T1 can be affected.

Most treatments described in the literature involve a posterior approach (laminectomy), which has led to incomplete resections in most cases. The anterior approach has only been described in one previous case with complete resection, and our case supports its efficacy in removing anterior intradural arachnoid cysts.

## Conclusion

Spinal arachnoid cysts are a rare cause of spinal cord compression. Anterior cervical arachnoid cysts are even rarer. Most are treated by posterior laminectomy with partial cyst resection. We have presented the case of a patient operated via an antero-lateral approach, without manipulation of the spinal cord. The patient completely recovered from his sensitive symptoms at discharge and motor symptoms 6 weeks later and remained asymptomatic 3 years later, despite a small communicating cyst residuum.
