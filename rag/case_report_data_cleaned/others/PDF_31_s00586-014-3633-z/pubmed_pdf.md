# Thoracic cord herniation and associated intra-operative nuances: a report

## Abstract
Background Thoracic cord herniation is a well-established entity in the literature. The majority of published reports focus on surgical management as mere detethering of the cord. However, little is written about the degree of herniation, the presence of ectopic cord tissue, and its management.

Case summary A 58-year-old male presented with progressive difficulty in walking. Imaging revealed a cord herniation at the T7-8 level. Surgical detethering was planned. Intra-operatively, a significant amount of ectopic cord tissue was found outside the dural defect, making simple detethering and repositioning difficult. The ectopic tissue was excised under neurophysiologic monitoring without major intra-operative or post-operative changes.

Conclusions Thoracic cord herniation surgery may require more than simple detethering and cord repositioning. If encountered in similar situations intra-operatively, surgeons may excise ectopic tissue without grave post-operative deficits under neurophysiologic monitoring. Neuronal plasticity probably plays an important role in the pathophysiology of long-standing cord herniation.

Keywords: Thoracic cord herniation; Ectopic cord; Resection

## Introduction
Ventral thoracic cord herniation (TCH) is a rare but well-described entity in the literature [1, 2]. Occasionally, herniations through the dorsal dura can also be encountered [3, 4]. However, none of the existing case reports has dealt with the extent of cord herniation and the resultant intra-operative nuances. Ventral TCH usually presents with features of myelopathy with gradual symptom progression. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the investigation of choice, with the most common finding being ventral displacement of the cord with absence of the anterior subarachnoid space. This typically makes the dorsal subarachnoid space prominent; hence, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow artifacts are commonly seen at the involved level, most often between T5 and T7. Phase-contrast MRI can further elucidate dorsal CSF flow artifacts and may help differentiate from the most common differential diagnosis of a dorsal arachnoid cyst.

## Case Report
A 58-year-old male presented with progressive difficulty in walking over the last few months. On examination, he had upper motor neuron signs in the lower limbs, right leg weakness, and hemisensory changes in the left hemibody with an approximate level of T8-T10. MRI of the spine suggested TCH at the T7-T8 disc level, with prominent dorsal CSF flow artifacts (Fig. 1). The diagnosis was straightforward on imaging. The patient consented for surgical detethering and underwent T7-T9 laminectomy. The surgery was performed under neurophysiological monitoring, with motor evoked potentials (MEPs) and somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs) recorded. Novak et al. [5] emphasized the role of MEPs in their series of four patients with TCH, demonstrating a correlation between intra-operative D wave amplitude drop and post-operative motor deficits.

Intra-operatively, the cord was flattened at the T7-T8 level, and a large ventral dural defect was seen after the cord was partially released, with a sizable ectopic tissue mass (approximately 2 x 2 cm) arising from the ventral surface and herniating through the defect (Fig. 2). The relatively large size of the mass made it impossible to preserve without causing cord compression, and to our knowledge, this association has not been reported with TCH to date. Therefore, partial resection was performed under close neurophysiological monitoring. Any drop in SSEPs or MEPs was considered the endpoint of resection. No attempt was made to resect the entire mass, and a safe margin was preserved adjacent to the cord. The ventral defect was covered with artificial dura, and dorsal dural closure was performed in the usual manner. The patient improved neurologically and did not develop any new deficits. Histopathology confirmed normal glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)-stained neural tissue in the excised specimen (Fig. 3).

## Discussion
Large masses of ectopic tissue have not been previously reported in association with TCH. Surgical details in published reports are often limited [6, 7], and most describe simple delineation and release from surrounding arachnoid, after which the cord can be repositioned within the dural sac. When substantial ectopic tissue is found ventral to the cord, management dilemmas arise. Prior reports have not quantified the amount of herniated ectopic tissue. Although some authors suggest that resection of ectopic tissue is not advisable [8], if simple intra-operative repositioning is not possible, resection may be attempted under neuro-monitoring. There is some evidence that long-standing giant nerve sheath tumors arising from eloquent roots may have minimal function and can be excised completely in select cases [9]. Neuronal plasticity probably contributes in such scenarios, allowing resection of long-standing ectopic cord substance when its size does not permit simple repositioning within the thecal sac.

## Conclusion
Thoracic cord herniation surgery may involve more than simple detethering and cord repositioning. In similar intra-operative situations, surgeons may excise ectopic tissue under neurophysiologic monitoring without causing grave post-operative deficits. Neuronal plasticity likely plays an important role in the pathophysiology of long-standing cord herniation.
