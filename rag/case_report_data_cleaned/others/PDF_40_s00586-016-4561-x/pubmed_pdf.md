# The Use of a Pedicle Screw–Cortical Screw Hybrid System for the Surgical Treatment of a Patient with Congenital Multilevel Spinal Non-Segmentation Defect and Spinal Column Deformity: A Technical Note

## Abstract

### Introduction
This technical note presents, to the authors' knowledge, the first reported case of a hybrid pedicle–cortical screw system for instrumented fusion in a patient with congenital vertebral column deformity.

### Case
Cortical screws were navigated using stereotactic guidance to extend a prior non-segmented fusion mass, facilitating instrumentation in a circumstance with completely distorted anatomy. This technique provided a safe trajectory with excellent cortical purchase in an anatomically deformed spine.

### Discussion
Cortical screw fixation may serve to be helpful in augmenting pedicle screw fixation and in circumstances in which the bone quality is suboptimal or the pedicles are compromised. Cortical screw fixation is a relatively new technology, but it may prove to be invaluable in providing an adjunct to pedicle screw constructs in anatomically distorted or osteoporotic spines.

## Introduction

The introduction of pedicle screw instrumentation in 1984 by Roy-Camille has greatly increased the success rate for fusion and stabilization in the lumbar spine, with a mean fusion rate of 93% [1, 2]. A more recent approach for instrumented stabilization is cortical screw placement. These screws function to maximize the amount of cortical bone in the screw trajectory when compared to pedicle screws. Several studies have examined the biomechanics of cortical screw fixation on cadaveric specimens and have demonstrated a 1.71-times higher torque strength in cortical screws, an indicator of good cortical purchase [3, 4]. The bicortical purchase of cortical screws provides a stronger fixation and demonstrated a 30% increase in uniaxial yield pullout strength as compared to pedicle screws [5, 6]. In addition, the screw trajectory offers a 100% increase in interface strength per unit screw length due to the denser juxtaposed osseous tissue with a smaller screw length [5].

One such instance of unique anatomy is presented in this case report. It constitutes, to the authors' knowledge, the first reported case of a hybrid pedicle–cortical screw system for instrumented fusion in a patient with congenital vertebral column deformity.

## Case Report

### History and Presentation

This 68-year-old man presented with progressively worsening gait and balance problems. Two weeks prior, he developed urinary and fecal incontinence and was unable to move his legs. He complained of severe back pain and difficulties controlling his legs due to severe spasm. At the age of 6 months, he underwent spinal surgery when, according to the patient, his “mother's bone was used to fix the spine.” On examination, he had 2/5 motor function in both lower extremities and a sensory level at T10. Rectal tone was decreased with severe spasticity, bilaterally present Babinski’s sign, and clonus. The patient had no evidence of motor or sensory deficits in the upper extremities.

### Imaging Studies

Magnetic resonance imaging and CT scans of the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spine demonstrated congenital non-segmentation of C3 and C4 and partial non-segmentation of C5 with an underdeveloped intervertebral disk at C4–5. Counting down with the cervical spine as a reference, there was severe spinal canal stenosis at T8–9 and T9–10 with a vacuum sign in the disk space and an ossified disk herniation toward the right side at T7–8. At that level, there was cord compression with signs of myelomalacia and cord edema. Only ten thoracic vertebrae were identified, and L5 was sacralized. Between T7–8 and L5, there was a large block vertebra with a large posterolateral fusion mass. The block vertebra consisted of anatomically identified thoracic and lumbar vertebrae that corresponded to the thoracolumbar junction. The conus medullaris was within the normal anatomic position based on the counting algorithm, and there was no fatty filum or tethering of the spinal cord visible. Based on clinical and radiographic findings, a T8–9 transpedicular discectomy, T9–T10 laminectomy, and fusion from T6 to L2 were recommended.

### Operation

The patient was taken to the operating room and positioned prone on an open Jackson table. The incision was planned over the previous midline incision site and extended cephalad and caudal for adequate exposure. After exposure, an intraoperative stereotactic CT was performed, with a fixated hip pin in the iliac crest serving as a navigation reference frame. Intraoperative navigation was especially helpful for localizing the T7–8 level, where the transpedicular discectomy was performed. Once the anterior compressive elements were successfully removed, a laminectomy from T8 to T10 was performed using a high-speed 6-mm coarse diamond drill bit.

After adequate decompression, pedicle screws were placed with the assistance of stereotactic navigation at T6 and T7 on the right and T6–T8 on the left. Attention was then turned to the area of posterolateral fusion at the level of the block vertebra. Cortical screws were placed with stereotactic navigation to maximize cortical fixation within the fusion mass. This proved especially challenging because of the underlying congenital malformation of the spinal column and the underdeveloped pedicular system. Instrumented arthrodesis was performed from T6 to L2 with the placement of a cross-link to connect the pedicle–cortical screw hybrid stabilization system.

### Postoperative Course

The patient had a slow recovery with considerable improvement in motor strength in both legs at 6-month follow-up, although he was not ambulatory. He was able to void spontaneously and no longer required intermittent catheterization.

## Discussion

The landscape of spinal instrumentation has dramatically changed over the past 50 years. Spinal fixation with pedicle screws evolved from its limited use for fracture treatment starting in 1963 to its current use as internal fixation to correct instabilities of the spine. Although pedicle screw fixation has become the present-day gold standard, it has several limitations. A recent study indicates an overall complication rate of 17.8% in thoracolumbar spine fixation [7, 8]. Another limitation includes use in patients with osteoporosis or osteopenia, conditions affecting approximately 44 million Americans, which may weaken pedicle screw purchase in the vertebral body [7, 9]. Oncological invasion, infectious erosive changes, and congenital deformities with abnormal vertebral anatomy may also compromise pedicle screw fixation.

Prior to pedicle screw fixation, laminar hooks and wiring were widely used for spinal fusion. Laminar hooks could provide cortical fixation and may be beneficial in osteopenia and osteoporosis [10]. However, the biomechanical pullout strength of pedicle screws has been shown to be stronger than that of laminar hooks, wires, or tape [11]. The three-column fixation provided by pedicle screws is stronger, even when compared to smaller thoracic pedicles [10]. For these reasons, hooks have largely been replaced by pedicular fixation systems.

Cortical screws have evolved as an instrumentation method that may avoid some limitations of pedicle screw fixation. They are inserted in a caudocephalad path and directed laterally in the transverse plane, engaging primarily cortical bone without involvement of the vertebral body trabecular space [1, 5]. CT scan analysis has shown that cortical screws engage bone with density four times higher than that of traditional trajectories [12]. Avoiding the trabecular portion of the pedicle increases overall screw strength, which is particularly important in severe osteoporosis. Ueno et al. reported deformity correction using multilevel pedicle screw fusion reinforced with bilateral cortical screw placement [13]. However, such constructs raise economic concerns and prolong operative time, and the biomechanical superiority of dual fixation over single systems has not been definitively demonstrated.

Cortical screws have also been successfully used in previously instrumented pedicles using CT guidance [14, 15], and hybrid systems have been applied in anatomically intact spines. Takata reported use of a dual-hybrid system for degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis with minimal failure rates, although follow-up was limited to 3 months [16].

In the present case, cortical screws were used to augment and extend a prior non-segmented fusion mass. A hybrid system was applied with pedicle screw fixation in the cephalad portion and cortical screw fixation in the caudal portion, where anatomy was severely distorted by prior surgery and a block vertebra spanning approximately four levels. Pedicle size precluded safe placement of pedicle screws, even with stereotactic guidance. Despite suboptimal bone quality, navigated cortical screws provided the best fixation by maximizing purchase in sclerotic bone. Cortical screw purchase was confirmed intraoperatively by strong tactile feedback and resistance to pullout. Stereotactic navigation was mandatory due to the absence of clear anatomic landmarks. This approach allowed stabilization with a robust hybrid construct while avoiding neural injury in a highly complex anatomy. The use of a hybrid pedicle–cortical screw system may also be valuable in revision surgery or when pedicles are compromised or bone quality is poor.
