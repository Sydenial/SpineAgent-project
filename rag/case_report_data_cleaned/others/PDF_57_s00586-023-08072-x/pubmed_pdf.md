# Abdominal lymphocele following multi-level anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALiF) managed with a laparoscopic peritoneal window: case report and review of the literature

## Abstract

**Purpose:** To report a case of abdominal lymphocele following a multi-level ALIF managed with a laparoscopic peritoneal window through a minimally invasive approach.

**Methods:** Case report.

**Results:** A 74-year-old male with a history of prostatectomy and pelvic radiation underwent a staged L3–S1 ALIF (left paramedian approach) and T10-pelvis posterior instrumented fusion with L1–5 decompression/posterior column osteotomies for degenerative scoliosis and neurogenic claudication. Three weeks after surgery, swelling of the left abdomen and entire left leg was reported. Computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a large retroperitoneal fluid collection, and fluid analysis indicated a lymphocele. Percutaneous drainage for 4 days was ineffective. The patient underwent an uncomplicated laparoscopic creation of a peritoneal window, leading to complete resolution of the lymphocele. At 3-year follow-up, the patient had no back or leg pain and had achieved spinal union, with no recurrence of the lymphocele.

**Conclusions:** Laparoscopic creation of a peritoneal window was safe and effective for managing abdominal lymphocele following multi-level ALIF.

**Keywords:** Lymphocele, Anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF), Laparoscopy, Peritoneal window

## Introduction

Anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) is a well-established surgical technique to treat lumbar degenerative pathology, correct spinal deformity, and achieve spinal fusion. Though relatively safe, it carries potential complications such as incisional hernia, retrograde ejaculation, vascular and ureteral injury, and lymphocele formation. While up to 25% of lymphoceles are asymptomatic, symptomatic lymphoceles can present with abdominal pain, wound drainage, and lower extremity swelling due to compression of pelvic structures. Management options for symptomatic lymphoceles include percutaneous drainage, sclerotherapy, and surgical interventions, but more durable solutions are preferred. The use of a peritoneal window to treat lymphoceles, commonly applied after renal transplantation, is explored here for the first time in the context of ALIF.

## Case Presentation

### Initial Presentation

A 74-year-old male with a history of prostate cancer treated with radical prostatectomy and pelvic radiation presented with progressive back and leg pain. Radiographs revealed multi-level lumbar spondylosis, grade 1 L4–5 spondylolisthesis, degenerative scoliosis, and loss of lumbar lordosis/flatback. MRI confirmed stenosis from L1 to S1.

### Index Spinal Operations

The patient underwent a L3–S1 ALIF without complications, followed by a T10-pelvis posterior instrumented fusion with L1–5 decompression and posterior column osteotomies. The ALIF was performed through a left paramedian approach. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, with no abdominal swelling or pain. He was discharged on postoperative day 6.

### Postoperative Complications

Three weeks after surgery, the patient reported swelling of the left leg and a mass in the left abdominal wall. Examination showed a fluctuant, fluid-filled mass around the left paramedian incision, and the left leg exhibited pitting edema. CT demonstrated a large retroperitoneal fluid collection with compression of the left ureter and common iliac vein. Fluid analysis confirmed the diagnosis of a lymphocele.

A percutaneous drain was placed, but drainage continued at a high volume (~1 L/day), prompting the consideration of further interventions, including sclerotherapy, marsupialization, and a laparoscopic peritoneal window.

### Laparoscopic Peritoneal Window

The patient underwent laparoscopic surgery, where a peritoneal window was created using a 4 cm × 6 cm opening in the peritoneum. This allowed lymphatic fluid to drain into the abdomen, facilitating its resorption by the peritoneum. Postoperatively, the patient reported improvement in both abdominal and leg swelling, and the drain was removed. There was no recurrence of the lymphocele.

### Three-Year Follow-up

At 3 years, the patient was free of back and leg pain, with no abdominal or leg swelling. Radiographs confirmed spinal union and alignment, and advanced imaging showed resolution of the lymphocele.

## Discussion

Lymphoceles are typically caused by disruption of lymphatic vessels, often following pelvic or abdominal surgery. In the case of ALIF, lymphatic injury can lead to fluid accumulation and the formation of a lymphocele. While most lymphoceles are asymptomatic and resolve spontaneously, symptomatic cases may require intervention. Traditional management includes percutaneous drainage and sclerotherapy, but more invasive approaches, such as laparoscopic fenestration, may be necessary when these measures fail.

In this case, the laparoscopic peritoneal window was a safe and effective solution. This minimally invasive procedure allowed for passive drainage of lymphatic fluid into the peritoneal cavity, leading to the resolution of the lymphocele without the need for more invasive surgery.

## Conclusions

Laparoscopic creation of a peritoneal window is a safe and effective method for managing abdominal lymphocele following multi-level ALIF. This technique offers a minimally invasive approach with minimal morbidity and should be considered as a treatment option for similar cases in the future.
