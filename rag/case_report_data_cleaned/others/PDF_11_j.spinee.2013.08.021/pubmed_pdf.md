# Hypokalemia Mimicking a Herniated Vertebral Disc

## Abstract

**Background Context:** A herniated vertebral disc is a common cause of paralysis. Other causes include infections, tumors, and neurologic diseases. A rare and dangerous but in most cases easily treatable cause is hypokalemia. Clinically, the acute symptoms may resemble a herniated vertebral disc, but hypokalemia per se is life-threatening by causing heart arrest through ventricular tachycardia or fibrillation.

**Purpose:** A patient with back pain and neurologic deficit in the lower extremities after a history of a herniated vertebral disc presented, who finally received the diagnosis of hypokalemia.

**Study Design:** Case report.

**Methods:** A 25-year-old female patient presenting after a fall with muscle weakness in both legs was followed clinically and radiographically.

**Results:** Neurological examination showed lower extremity muscle weakness with three-fifths muscular strength of the quadriceps and tibialis anterior muscle on both sides. Reflexes were diminished bilaterally, and anal sphincter tone was normal. Plain radiography suggested a posterior rim fracture of L5, but computed tomography did not confirm this diagnosis. Laboratory investigation revealed hypokalemia of 1.7 mEq/L. After electrolyte replacement, the patient recovered immediately.

**Conclusion:** This report describes a misleading diagnostic case of back pain and neurologic deficit after trauma and highlights the possible life-threatening diagnosis of hypokalemia, which is rare but easily treatable.

**Keywords:** Hypokalemia; Herniated vertebral disc; Muscle weakness; Life-threatening

## Case Report

This case report presents a 25-year-old nurse who fell down a set of stairs while visiting a local restaurant. After the minor fall, the patient felt generalized weakness in her lower extremities. She was immediately referred to the hospital, and because a traumatic spinal cord injury was suspected, she was immobilized. On admission, the patient was awake and alert with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15, showing no signs of external trauma or distress.

She reported an episode of lumbar pain during the preceding week in combination with pain in her legs. A lumbar herniated disc had previously been diagnosed. According to her profession, she was familiar with the clinical picture of lower back pain and weakness in the lower extremities, which she interpreted as a lumbar herniated disc.

Neurological examination showed good sensation to light touch and revealed proximal weakness with three-fifths muscular strength of the quadriceps and tibialis anterior muscle bilaterally. Reflexes were diminished on both sides, and anal sphincter tone was normal. Strength of the upper extremities was normal. During palpation, the right sacroiliac joint and the spinous processes of L3–L5 were painful.

A small fracture of the posterior rim of L5 was suspected on plain radiographs, but computed tomography of the lumbar spine did not confirm this diagnosis. There was no evidence of nerve root compression, spinal stenosis, or spinal tumor.

Laboratory tests at admission revealed an elevated leukocyte count of 14.4 G/L and a decreased potassium level of 1.7 mEq/L. Blood ethanol level was 0 mg/dL, and drug screening was negative. On further questioning, the patient admitted abuse of diuretic medications and benzodiazepines.

The patient was immediately admitted to an intermediate care unit for cardiac surveillance and underwent slow potassium replacement for 17 hours through a central venous catheter at a rate of 15 mmol/h. Once serum potassium levels returned to the normal range, the patient was transferred to an internal medicine ward and discharged the following day without further treatment.

## Discussion

This report presents a rare case of hypokalemia mimicking spinal cord compression in a young nurse with abuse of diuretic medications, presenting after trauma and with a history of lower back pain. Hypokalemia leads to multisystemic effects and is an uncommon cause of isolated extremity muscle weakness. Patients suffering from hypokalemia often present with cardiac arrhythmias [1]. Muscular weakness and rhabdomyolysis can also be signs of very low serum potassium levels (<2.5 mEq/L) [2,3], although these symptoms usually occur as part of a broader clinical syndrome.

Hypokalemia during diuretic therapy is a common side effect, with an incidence of approximately 20% [4–6], but it is usually moderate when appropriately monitored (3.0–3.5 mEq/L). Chronic use of diuretics without compelling medical indication should raise suspicion for psychiatric disease [7]. Intermittent abuse of diuretics in young women, such as stewardesses, ballet dancers, or models, and in bodybuilders is not rare [8,9], although incidence data are lacking. Gastrointestinal potassium loss due to frequent vomiting, enteral fistulas, or antibiotic use should also be included in the differential diagnosis [10,11].

Treatment of severe hypokalemia consists of parenteral potassium substitution with a maximum infusion rate of 20 mmol/h under continuous monitoring conditions [12,13].

## Conclusion

In this case, the patient presented with the misleading symptom of lower back pain, a common complaint in modern society and particularly prevalent among nursing staff [14]. The history of trauma, low back pain, and a previously diagnosed lumbar herniated disc further simulated a spinal cord injury or compression. Typically, lumbar disc herniation presents with paresthesia and muscular weakness in a radicular distribution. Bilateral muscular weakness, as seen in this patient, is more typical of a median herniated disc but remains relatively uncommon [15].

Although hypokalemia is a known cause of paralysis [16], it may be easily misdiagnosed. This case illustrates the importance of considering hypokalemia in the differential diagnosis, as missing the diagnosis can be life-threatening. Immediate treatment is necessary and leads to full recovery of symptoms, even though the condition is rarely encountered in routine clinical practice.
