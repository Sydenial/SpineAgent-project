# Occipital Condyles Congenital Dislocation and Condylus Tertius

## Abstract

Congenital variations in the craniocervical junction (CVJ) are rare and frequently symptomless. Mild trauma may rouse symptoms that unveil such anomalies through radiological investigations. A 73-year-old woman developed a monoparesis of the right arm after a mild craniofacial trauma. Neurological examination revealed hyperreflexia in the upper limbs, confirming strength impairment in the right arm. Radiology showed a post-traumatic bulbo-medullary contusion sustained by a unique and unstable association of the first occipital condyles congenital dislocation, never reported before, with a rare condylus tertius. The patient underwent posterior decompression and occipitocervical screw-rod fixation and fusion. Clinico-radiological follow-up highlighted gradual recovery of the neurologic impairment and resolution of the spinal cord contusion. Although apparently stable, hyperostosis and irregularly shaped condylar surfaces behind the three-point mechanism of skull base support played a critical role in determining axial instability. A surgical strategy providing posterior decompression and fixation satisfied the need to solve both bulbo-medullary constriction and skull base instability.

## Introduction

Anomalies of the craniocervical junction arouse great interest because numerous skeletal variants may result in neural compression, vascular compromise, alteration of cerebrospinal fluid dynamics, and even death. Among the rarest variations is the condylus tertius, occurring as a midline projection of the clivus located at the anterior margin of the foramen magnum. Sometimes expressed as a simple rounded tubercle, in more structured cases it receives the tip of the odontoid process as an articular facet. Herein we describe the case of a post-traumatic discovery of an extremely developed condylus tertius associated with the first ever reported occipital condyles congenital dislocation.

## Case Report

A 73-year-old woman developed a sudden monoparesis of the right upper limb soon after a domestic craniofacial trauma. No loss of consciousness was reported. Neurological examination at admission revealed strength impairment graded 3/5 in the right arm with distal paresthesias, hyperreflexia in the upper limbs, and bilateral Babinski sign. The patient reported no functional limitations in head movement, no neck pain or stiffness before the trauma, no history of dystocic birth, rheumatic diseases, or previous head or cervical spine trauma.

Urgent head and neck CT scan showed a thickened clivus with a threefold occipital condylar configuration attached to its lower surface. One condyle articulated with the odontoid process and two with the posterior arch of C1. These latter constituted two pseudo-diarthroses with aberrant condyles sprouted where clival hyperostosis reached the occipital bone and displaced postero-medially from the normal position.

MRI of the cervical spine outlined severe bulbo-medullary compression, mainly from the dorsal aspect, highlighting an altered T2 signal of the right hemimedulla compatible with acute contusion rather than chronic myelomalacia. The patient underwent C1 laminectomy for posterior decompression and occipitocervical screw-rod fixation and fusion. The condylus tertius was left in place, as this was not an acute dislocation but a stabilized cranio-cervical malpositioning.

Postoperative CT documented wide dorsal bulbo-medullary decompression. Five days after surgery, paresthesias completely resolved and partial recovery of strength in the right upper limb was observed. Six-month follow-up MRI demonstrated resolution of the bulbo-medullary contusion with posterior shift and relief from spinal cord compression. Functional assessment using the Benzel modified JOA scale showed improvement from an initial score of 14 to 16 at six months and stabilization at 17 by nine months, maintained at 21 months after surgery.

## Discussion

Congenital anomalies of the CVJ are rare and frequently symptomless, often detected incidentally during radiological evaluation after trauma. CVJ abnormalities can be classified into assimilation of the atlas, anomalies of the atlas, manifestations of the occipital vertebra, platybasia and occipital dysplasia, and degenerative manifestations imitating variants. From an embryological perspective, the association of a condylus tertius with postero-medial dislocation of the occipital condyles suggests combined features of occipital vertebra manifestations.

Hyperossification may support the association of a structured third condyle with exuberant occipital condyles displaced postero-medially, differing from classical post-traumatic slippage and resembling a postero-medial variation of the processus condylicus. The third condyle may restrict or block rotational and flexion-extension movements of the skull. In more developed cases, marked ossification sustains a three-legged head-bearing mechanism, resulting in an occipital-atlanto-odontoid joint.

Paradoxically, this articulated connection may contribute to axial instability due to irregular cartilaginous interfaces and secondary effects on ligamentous support. In this patient, osseous imbrication between the odontoid tip and ossified clivus did not form a proper diarthrosis; instead, it produced an incongruent pseudo-joint prone to instability. Although circumferential medullary constriction was present, posterior decompression and fusion were deemed sufficient to relieve the predominant dorsal compression.

Morphologically, the small and irregular occipital condyles were displaced posteriorly and articulated along the medial profile of the posterior arch of the atlas. Resulting imbalance, combined with lack of stable joints, exposed the patient to high risk of axial instability and acute neurological manifestation even after minor trauma. Progressive ossification and osteophyte formation may alternatively lead to gradual myelopathy. Surgical decompression and fixation should be considered, particularly in cases with aberrant articular facets, to prevent spinal cord constriction and skull base instability.

## Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of familiarity with CVJ craniometry, anatomical relationships, pathological conditions, and embryological mechanisms underlying congenital abnormalities of this region. Given the rarity and variability of these anomalies, clinical awareness is essential. Future studies on developmental control genes and their regulatory roles may offer perspectives for early diagnosis and targeted treatment before neurological damage occurs.

## Key Points

- Occipital condyles congenital dislocation is an unstable CVJ anomaly not previously reported in the literature.
- Apparent stability due to hyperostosis and threefold condylar support may mask significant axial instability.
- Incongruent condylar joints contribute to progressive bone overgrowth and spinal cord narrowing.
- Posterior decompression and fixation are essential to address both bulbo-medullary constriction and skull base instability.
