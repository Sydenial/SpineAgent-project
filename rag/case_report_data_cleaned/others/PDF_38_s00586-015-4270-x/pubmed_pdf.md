# Munchausen syndrome: a forgotten diagnosis in the spine

## Abstract

**Purpose**: To present the case of a patient with Munchausen's syndrome who underwent multiple surgeries in the spine before the diagnosis was made and, therefore, to highlight the importance of this obscure condition that can result in unnecessary surgical treatment.

**Methods**: A 44-year-old businesswoman presented with multiple episodes of low back pain and weakness in both lower limbs over the past 11 years. Her medical history included multiple hospitalizations and three surgeries on her lumbar spine at different hospitals, with dramatic improvement in symptoms after each surgery. Clinical examination showed inconsistent and nonspecific neurological findings. Imaging studies like X-rays, magnetic resonance imaging, and all neurophysiological studies were within normal limits.

**Results**: A multi-disciplinary evaluation by a team of orthopedists, neurologists, psychiatrists, and rehabilitation specialists diagnosed her with Munchausen syndrome. Only one previous report of this fictitious disease in the spine was found in the literature (Association AP, Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders: DSM-IV-TR®, 2003).

**Conclusions**: A history of multiple surgical interventions at different hospitals, often followed by dramatic improvement and then relapse, should raise suspicion for Munchausen syndrome, particularly in the case of normal imaging studies. Diagnosing this rare condition in the spine is crucial to avoid unnecessary surgery.

**Keywords**: Munchausen syndrome, Fictitious disorder, Lumbar spine, Normal imaging

## Introduction

Richard Asher coined the term "Munchausen syndrome" in 1951, naming the condition after Karl Friedrich Hieronymus, also known as Baron Munchausen (1720–1797), a man who traveled widely and was renowned for telling fantastic and exaggerated stories about his life [1]. Meadow later introduced the term "Munchausen syndrome by proxy" to describe the fabrication of an illness on behalf of another person, typically a parent on behalf of a child [2].

In our review of the literature, we found only one report of a patient undergoing multiple spinal surgeries for factitious illnesses of the spine [3]. Therefore, we report another such case to document this rare condition, which requires careful attention.

## Case Report

A 44-year-old businesswoman presented with low back pain and weakness in both lower limbs for the past 5 months. She had an extensive medical history dating back 11 years related to her spinal symptoms. At the time of presentation, she did not have imaging studies or hospital records from the initial phase of her treatment.

She reported being involved in a road traffic accident in her home country in 2002, after which she developed progressive weakness in both lower limbs along with bowel and bladder incontinence. However, no imaging studies from that hospitalization were available. She recalled undergoing a "discectomy," which resulted in near-complete relief of all symptoms, and she returned to normal function within a few weeks.

In 2006, she again developed low back pain similar to the previous episode, which gradually worsened along with weakness in the lower limbs. She was referred to a neighboring country, where she underwent "instrumented posterior L5-S1 stabilization," according to her hospital records. Once again, no imaging studies were available. She reported immediate relief of pain and returned to normal function within a few weeks.

In 2010, she experienced a recurrence of low back pain and lower limb weakness. This time, she traveled overseas, where, according to available records, she underwent a diagnostic facet block. She reported immediate pain relief and was then advised to undergo a third surgical procedure—an exchange of her L5–S1 fusion implants with a dynamic stabilization system. As before, she recalled immediate relief of pain after the surgery and gradually returned to normal function.

In May 2013, she reported a recurrence of symptoms (back pain and weakness in both legs), which progressively worsened, requiring assistance to walk. An orthopedic surgeon in her home country recommended removal of her dynamic stabilization implants followed by "fusion with cages," and referred her to us for further management.

When she presented to us, she reported severe back pain (Visual Analog Score of 8/10) and was unable to stand due to weakness in both lower limbs. She did not report symptoms of bowel or bladder dysfunction.

On examination, she appeared as a pleasant and articulate middle-aged lady. The general physical examination was unremarkable. Examination of the spine revealed multiple healed surgical scars with superficial tenderness in the L5-S1 region. Movements of the spine were significantly restricted due to pain.

Neurological examination revealed hypoesthesia to fine touch in the L3, 4, and 5 dermatomes on the left side, and L4 and 5 dermatomes on the right side. Motor examination showed mild decreases in the bulk and tone of the lower limb muscles bilaterally. Muscle power was graded as 5 in all groups according to the MRC scale, with significant variation between direct examination of volitional power and functional assessment. Deep tendon reflexes were sluggish in both lower limbs, and there were no pathological reflexes. Both upper limbs were normal on examination.

All blood parameters were within normal limits. Plain radiographs did not show significant abnormalities, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) did not reveal any vertebrodiscal pathology or spinal canal compromise. Nerve conduction studies showed normal compound motor action potentials (CMAP) from the peroneal and tibial nerves, as recorded from the extensor digitorum brevis and abductor hallucis muscles, respectively, accompanied by a normal needle electromyography (EMG) study.

Given her conflicting clinical findings and non-contributory imaging studies, a neurology and psychiatric consultation was sought. The neurology consultation confirmed the unlikelihood of organic weakness in her lower limbs, considering the clinical examination and electrophysiological findings. Although the patient was reluctant to undergo a detailed psychiatric evaluation, she agreed to a preliminary consultation. The psychiatrist's evaluation revealed unresolved grief over her deceased mother, an unfulfilled need to be cared for by her family and society, and symptom expression driven by resentment towards the medical profession. She exhibited significant knowledge about the anatomy of the spine and surgical options, and insisted on specific surgical procedures for relief.

In the psychiatrist’s opinion, the patient exhibited a persistent pattern of intentional symptom production to assume the sick role, without clear external motives, fulfilling the criteria for a diagnosis of factitious disorder according to the DSM-IV [3]. The key features supporting this diagnosis included:

1. Absence of an organic etiology for the degree of pain and dysfunction as evident from clinical evaluation and imaging.
2. Intentional symptom production to assume a sick role, as evident from her unfulfilled psychological needs and behavioral patterns involving sudden pain onset, surgical relief, and subsequent relapse.
3. The absence of external incentives, ruling out malingering.

A differential diagnosis of dissociative disorder was also considered, with a hypothesis that symptom production could stem from unconscious conflict. However, the patient rejected this explanation.

Over the following days, the patient was persuaded to avoid further surgery and agreed to a facet block instead. She underwent a bilateral L5–S1 facet block under image intensifier, reporting dramatic pain relief within hours, walking independently the same evening. Conversations with the family revealed stressors in her life prior to the onset of back pain.

A detailed physiotherapy program was developed, and the patient was discharged with analgesics, anxiolytics, and neural supplements, with follow-up scheduled if her symptoms recurred. Her resistance to acknowledging the psychological origin of her symptoms was acknowledged, and a multi-modal approach to symptom relief was emphasized.

## Discussion

The DSM-IV defines Munchausen's syndrome as "intentional production or feigning of physical or psychological signs or symptoms," with the motivation being to assume the sick role, unlike malingering, which involves external incentives. The patient's behavior is often driven by compulsion, worsened by life events, stress, or chronic depressive states. Features of the syndrome include difficulty forming stable relationships, multiple hospital admissions, falsified symptoms, and extensive medical knowledge.

Munchausen's syndrome has been documented with symptoms such as factitious dermatitis, false symptoms produced in children by parents, baroreflex failure, and feigned psychiatric histories [4-7]. A similar case of a 28-year-old female with multiple spinal surgeries and psychiatric evaluation supporting factitious disorder is also reported in the DSM-IV [3].

Psychiatric management focuses on engagement in therapy without confronting the "falseness" of symptoms. Treatment principles include avoiding unnecessary invasive procedures, preventing self-harm, and maintaining consistency in care.

The prognosis for patients with Munchausen's syndrome is typically poor, as they often become dependent on the therapeutic team to fulfill their sick role or disengage if their demands are unmet [9].

A diagnosis of Munchausen syndrome should be considered when the following characteristics are present:

- Dramatic history of severe illnesses.
- Multiple hospitalizations, often across different hospitals and cities.
- Vagueness or inconsistency in medical details.
- Absent or incomplete hospital records.
- Inconsistent clinical findings and imaging studies.
- A history of multiple surgeries for the same issue with temporary improvement and relapse.
- A strong desire for repeat surgery.

## Conclusion

A history of multiple surgical interventions, often followed by dramatic improvement and then relapse, should raise suspicion of Munchausen syndrome, especially when imaging studies are normal. Full investigations should be conducted to rule out structural pathology before making this diagnosis.

A psychiatric evaluation is essential, although patients may resist this approach. Reassurance and confidence-building are crucial for helping the patient and family navigate this difficult experience. Educating the family about functional symptoms is a key component of the treatment process.

History-taking and examination remain vital tools in clinical medicine, especially for conditions like Munchausen syndrome, which can help avoid unnecessary surgery.
