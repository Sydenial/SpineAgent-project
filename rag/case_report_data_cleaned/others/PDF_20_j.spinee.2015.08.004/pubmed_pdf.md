# Massive lumbar pneumorrhachis after severe cranial trauma

## Abstract
A 25-year-old man was referred to our Emergency Department following a motor vehicle accident. His mental state was confused, and there was no traumatic wound to the spinal column. Computed tomography (CT) was performed, showing massive lumbar pneumorrhachis and pneumocephalus (Figure 1). In our case, air in the cranium likely spread along the subarachnoid space, eventually appearing in the spinal canal. Pneumorrhachis is typically a benign condition and resolves with conservative therapies. However, it can cause symptoms of cord compression, making early diagnosis and follow-up important.

## Introduction
Pneumorrhachis, the presence of air in the spinal canal, is a rare condition. It can occur after trauma, particularly cranial trauma, where air from the cranial cavity can track down the spinal canal. The development of pneumorrhachis can be associated with pneumocephalus, as seen in this case. Although pneumorrhachis is often benign and resolves without intervention, it can lead to complications such as cord compression, requiring careful monitoring and management.

## Case Report
A 25-year-old male presented to the Emergency Department with confusion after a motor vehicle accident. His physical examination was otherwise unremarkable, with no visible signs of trauma to the spine. A CT scan was performed, revealing massive pneumorrhachis and pneumocephalus. The air likely spread from the cranial cavity into the subarachnoid space and subsequently into the spinal canal. The patient was treated conservatively, and the condition resolved without the need for surgical intervention.

## Discussion
Pneumorrhachis is a rare phenomenon typically seen in patients with significant cranial trauma. It occurs when air from the cranial cavity tracks along the subarachnoid space into the spinal canal. In the present case, the patient’s pneumorrhachis was likely caused by the pneumocephalus, a condition in which air accumulates within the cranial cavity, typically due to trauma. Pneumorrhachis often resolves with conservative treatment, but close monitoring is essential to detect potential complications such as cord compression.

## Conclusion
This case highlights the importance of recognizing pneumorrhachis in trauma patients, especially those with associated pneumocephalus. Although the condition is typically benign, early diagnosis and follow-up are crucial to avoid complications, such as cord compression, that may require more invasive treatment.
