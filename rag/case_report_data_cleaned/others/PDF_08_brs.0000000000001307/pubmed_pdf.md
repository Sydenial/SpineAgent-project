# Cardiac Emboli Documented by Intraoperative Transesophageal Echocardiogram During Administration of a Topical Hemostatic Agent Prior to Pedicle Subtraction Osteotomy

## Abstract

**Study Design.** This is a case report.

**Objective.** To document cardiac emboli observed on transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) after the injection of a topical hemostatic agent into a vertebra prior to performing a pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO).

**Summary of Background Data.** Hemostasis during spinal surgery is critical for adequate visualization and to reduce the risk of perioperative complications. Adult spinal deformity surgery can involve performing PSOs, which are useful in cases of fixed spinal deformities and are associated with increased blood loss secondary to epidural and cancellous bleeding. Prior to performing a PSO, a topical hemostatic agent can be injected into the vertebra through the pedicle screw pilot holes in an attempt to decrease cancellous bleeding. Injected hemostatic agents can pressurize the vertebral body similar to cementation in vertebroplasty and during fracture reaming and prosthetic implantation in the femur. Patients with cardiac defects such as patent foramen ovale or atrial septal defect may be more prone to systemic embolic events resulting in morbidity or mortality.

**Methods.** We injected a topical hemostatic matrix agent through the pedicle screw pilot holes into the L1 vertebral body prior to performing a PSO while simultaneously recording with TEE.

**Results.** The TEE recorded large visible emboli traveling through the heart into the pulmonary vasculature. The patient remained stable throughout the remainder of the case, and a postoperative spiral computed tomography (CT) scan was negative for filling defects. The patient had an uneventful hospital course.

**Conclusion.** Questions remain about the exact consistency of these emboli, when they are most likely to occur, how much cardiopulmonary insult can be tolerated without resulting in complications, or how to prevent their occurrence. Patients undergoing spinal surgery with the plan to inject hemostatic matrix agents into the vertebral body may benefit from a preoperative TEE to reduce the risk of complications associated with embolic events, especially in patients with undiagnosed patent foramen ovale or atrial septal defect.

## Introduction

Hemostasis during spinal surgery is critical for adequate visualization and to prevent complications. Spinal deformity corrective surgery can involve one or more osteotomy types. Pedicle subtraction osteotomies (PSOs) are extremely useful in cases of fixed spinal deformities, but are associated with increased intraoperative blood loss [1].

Multiple hemostatic matrix agents are available for use during spine surgery [2–4]. Prior to a PSO, topical hemostatic matrices such as SurgiFlo can be injected through the pedicle screw pilot holes, pressurizing the vertebral body in an attempt to decrease the cancellous bleeding associated with the procedure. Similar documented increases in pressure occur in the femoral canal during reaming and intramedullary nail placement, and in the vertebra with cementation in vertebroplasty, with documented embolic events entering the pulmonary vasculature [5–7]. However, the exact makeup of the embolic material seen on transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE), as well as their effect on patients’ cardiopulmonary physiology, is unknown.

We are unaware of any previously published reports of cardiac emboli occurring after the pressurization of a vertebral body with a hemostatic matrix agent while performing a PSO.

## Case Report

A 72-year-old man with a previous L2–L4 decompression and fusion presented with a positive sagittal imbalance of +14 cm, complaining of worsening back pain and the sensation of constantly leaning forward. The patient had failed all nonsurgical measures, and his symptoms were significantly affecting his activities of daily living. His medical history was significant for severe congestive heart failure with an ejection fraction of 30%, left bundle branch block, and dilated cardiomyopathy.

The cardiac anesthesia service continuously monitored the patient’s cardiac function utilizing a TEE throughout the case. The patient underwent a revision posterior spinal fusion from T4 to the ilium, an L1 PSO, T12–L2 and L5–S1 Smith-Petersen osteotomies, an L5–S1 transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion with cage placement, and a T3 prophylactic vertebroplasty.

A topical hemostatic matrix was injected through the pedicle screw pilot holes prior to the PSO procedure. Immediately after the matrix was injected, TEE recorded large emboli moving from the right atrium through the right side of the heart into the pulmonary artery. No abnormal findings in heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory status, or oxygen saturation were associated with the emboli. The emboli were visualized immediately after the pressurization of each pedicle and then visualized for one full cardiac cycle. No additional emboli were visualized during the remainder of the procedure, including the T3 vertebroplasty.

An immediate postoperative spiral CT scan of the chest did not demonstrate any abnormalities. The patient experienced an uneventful postoperative hospital course without evidence of cardiopulmonary or systemic embolic complications.

## Discussion

Cardiac emboli have been documented during pressurization of long bones such as the femur [5,6]. Likewise, fat embolism syndrome has been described in both long bone fractures and vertebroplasty, with associated fatalities [7,9–14]. However, to our knowledge, cardiac emboli resulting from pressurization of a vertebra with a hemostatic agent during a PSO have not been previously reported.

The patient in this case had significant baseline cardiopulmonary compromise but experienced no adverse events related to the embolic material observed in the heart despite poor cardiopulmonary reserve. However, the degree of cardiopulmonary insult that can be tolerated remains unknown.

Echogenic embolic material found in previous intramedullary femoral nailing studies has demonstrated the presence of fat and fresh thrombotic material [8]. Large fat droplets may be released into the systemic circulation following increased intravertebral pressure, which could potentially obstruct end capillary beds mechanically. Without tissue sampling, the exact composition of the emboli in this case is unknown but is likely consistent with fat and thrombotic material, similar to that described by Bisignani et al. [8].

Patent foramen ovale (PFO) has been reported in approximately 25–27% of individuals, both at autopsy and by TEE [15–17]. Right-to-left cardiac shunts such as a PFO or atrial septal defect (ASD) are concerning because paradoxical emboli may travel directly into the systemic circulation, potentially resulting in severe complications such as stroke, ischemic bowel, or limb ischemia.

Prone positioning, positive pressure ventilation, and intravascular fluid resuscitation may further potentiate emboli crossing intracardiac defects and becoming systemic [15–17]. Although this patient did not have a septal defect and showed no signs of systemic embolization, patients with preexisting PFO or ASD may be more susceptible to complications associated with embolic events during vertebral body pressurization.

## Conclusion

Emboli appear to be more common during procedures that result in internal pressurization of the skeleton [5–7]. Questions remain regarding the consistency of these emboli, when they are most likely to occur, how much cardiopulmonary insult can be tolerated, and how best to prevent their occurrence. Fat embolism syndrome, although rare, is a potentially fatal complication associated with skeletal pressurization.

Surgeons must be aware of the potential complications associated with vertebral body pressurization and should carefully consider preoperative TEE evaluation for PFO or ASD to reduce the risk of embolic complications. Further prospective analysis is warranted to better assess the physiological effects of vertebral body pressurization during pedicle subtraction osteotomy.

## Key Points

- Emboli have been documented during procedures involving pressurization of the skeleton.  
- Questions remain regarding the amount of cardiopulmonary insult that can be tolerated during vertebral body pressurization.  
- Asymptomatic patent foramen ovale and atrial septal defect are common and can result in paradoxical emboli entering the systemic circulation.  
- Patients with preexisting PFO or ASD may be more susceptible to complications associated with embolic events.  
- Preoperative TEE should be considered to reduce the risk of embolic complications in high-risk patients.
