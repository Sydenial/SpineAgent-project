# Esophageal Perforation in a Cervical Fracture Patient

## Abstract

**Study Design.** Case report and review of relevant literature.  
**Objective.** To report a rare complication of esophageal perforation in a cervical fracture patient with progressive ankylosing spondylitis (AS) and review relevant literature.  
**Summary of Background Data.** Esophageal perforation in cervical fracture is extremely rare and has not previously been reported in patients with AS. This report presents key points in early diagnosis and management of this severe complication.  
**Methods.** A 61-year-old male with AS sustained a C6 fracture and dislocation after a vehicle accident. Initial treatment was planned as combined anterior–posterior fixation and fusion. During anterior surgery, an esophageal perforation was detected. After debridement, the lesion was closed with resorbable interrupted sutures. The incision was irrigated without primary closure, and the cervical fracture was treated with long-level posterior fixation alone.  
**Results.** The esophageal perforation healed completely, neurological function improved significantly, and fracture union with cervical stability was confirmed radiologically at one-year follow-up.  
**Conclusion.** Esophageal perforation in cervical fracture patients with advanced AS is an extremely rare but potentially life-threatening complication. Surgical debridement, drainage, and intensive postoperative care are essential for a good outcome.

**Key Words:** ankylosing spondylitis, cervical fracture, complication, esophageal perforation.

## Case Report

A 61-year-old man with a 25-year history of ankylosing spondylitis was admitted to the emergency department 12 hours after a traffic accident. His chief complaints were neck pain, left upper limb pain, and numbness. Muscle strength in the upper extremities was 3/5 on the left and 4/5 on the right. Hypertonia and hyperreflexia were observed in the left upper extremity. The Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA) score was 11 points.

Preoperative radiographs revealed a typical “bamboo spine” with C6 fracture and dislocation. Computed tomography demonstrated a transverse fracture through the C6 vertebral body with laminar and spinous process fractures. Magnetic resonance imaging showed prevertebral edema, rupture of the anterior longitudinal ligament, and compression of the spinal cord.

Skull traction was initiated immediately after admission. Three days later, the patient developed persistent fever (approximately 39 °C) and throat pain that worsened with swallowing. Laboratory tests revealed a leukocyte count of $20.8 \times 10^{9}/\mathrm{L}$ with 93.6% neutrophils. After treatment with moxifloxacin, body temperature returned to normal.

Radiography showed no fracture reduction after traction. The initial strategy was combined anterior–posterior fixation and fusion. During the anterior approach, yellowish viscous pus was observed in the prevertebral space. An esophageal perforation approximately 0.5 cm in length, caused by a sharp fracture fragment, was identified on the dorsal wall of the esophagus. A nasogastric tube was inserted, and after thorough debridement, the lesion was closed with resorbable interrupted sutures. The surgical field was irrigated with saline and hydrogen peroxide without primary closure. Posterior surgery was then performed, and fracture reduction was achieved with fixation from C4 to T2.

Postoperatively, the patient received total parenteral nutrition and broad-spectrum antibiotics. The anterior incision was irrigated with normal saline twice daily and was closed three weeks later, healing uneventfully. Flexible endoscopy performed four weeks after surgery showed no abnormalities. The patient was immobilized with a hard cervical collar for two months. Neurological symptoms improved significantly, with the JOA score increasing from 11 preoperatively to 15 at final follow-up. Radiographs confirmed satisfactory reduction and fixation, and fracture union with segmental stability was demonstrated at one-year follow-up.

## Discussion

Esophageal perforation is an extremely rare complication in patients with cervical fracture. A review of the literature identified only 10 reported cases with detailed clinical information between 1960 and 2013. The mean patient age was 51.2 ± 20.1 years (range 19–79 years). Vehicle accidents and falls were the most common causes, with injuries typically involving C4 to C6. None of the previously reported cases involved patients with ankylosing spondylitis.

This case represents a unique presentation of esophageal perforation associated with cervical fracture in a patient with advanced AS. Diagnosis can be challenging due to atypical signs and symptoms. Clinical indicators include increasing anterior cervical pain and tenderness, cervical swelling or edema, dyspnea, and dysphagia. Prevertebral edema or emphysema on CT or MRI should raise suspicion. Barium esophagography and esophageal endoscopy are useful for confirming the diagnosis.

Management strategies emphasized in the literature include prompt surgical debridement, adequate drainage, and intensive postoperative care. Continuous wound irrigation may be useful in cases with purulent discharge. Early diagnosis allows for primary repair of the esophageal laceration. Postoperative antibiotic therapy is critical to prevent wound and mediastinal infections. Mortality remains high, as three of the 10 reported cases resulted in death, underscoring the severity of this complication. In the present case, intraoperative recognition and appropriate modification of the surgical strategy contributed to a favorable outcome.

## Key Points

- Esophageal perforation complicating cervical fracture in patients with advanced ankylosing spondylitis is extremely rare and potentially life-threatening.  
- Attention to clinical signs such as dysphagia, anterior cervical pain, and prevertebral edema is essential to avoid delayed diagnosis.  
- Surgical debridement, adequate drainage, and intensive postoperative care are crucial for achieving a good outcome.
