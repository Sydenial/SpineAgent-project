# Success in Esophageal Perforation Repair With Open-Wound Management After Revision Cervical Spine Surgery  
A Case Report

## Abstract
**Objective:** To share our successful experience in treating 1 case of esophageal perforation after anterior revision cervical spine surgery with open-wound management.  
**Summary of Background Data:** Early diagnosis and surgical treatment are widely adopted in the management of esophageal complications after anterior cervical spine surgery, but the management of wounds after surgical repair of esophageal perforation is rarely discussed.  
**Methods:** One patient underwent revision anterior cervical spine surgery due to displaced hardware and poor alignment. Esophageal perforation occurred intraoperatively and was discovered on the first postoperative day. Repair surgery was performed, where the perforation was closed with sutures and reinforced with a sternocleidomastoid muscle flap. The wound was loosely closed with aspirating drainage. Two days after surgery, the patient exhibited signs of recurrent esophageal leakage and severe secondary wound infection. The wound was reopened, and a continuous irrigation and drainage system was set up.  
**Results:** In 12 weeks, the esophageal perforation healed without complications or loosening of instrumentation.  
**Conclusion:** Open-wound management succeeded in this patient after surgical repair of esophageal perforation caused by revision anterior cervical spine surgery.

## Introduction
Although many strategies have been described to manage esophageal perforation caused by anterior cervical spine surgery, there has been no report of a similar encounter published in the literature. By sharing our experience of a special case, we hope to add new information to the literature.

Our case involved a 24-year-old male who experienced complete spinal cord injury due to a fracture and dislocation at cervical spine (C5) and underwent anterior surgery. Tetraplegia was not recovered post-surgery, but the patient could breathe normally as the function of the phrenic nerve remained intact. Two years later, the patient was admitted again due to displacement of instrumentation. The preoperative screening, including contrast esophagography, showed no abnormalities. He underwent revision cervical spine surgery. On the first postoperative day, the patient complained of odynophagia with swelling around the right anterolateral incision. Saliva-like fluid and air were seen in the anterolateral incision drainage tube. A radiographic examination after swallowing soluble contrast revealed esophageal extravasation in the right anterolateral incision at the C4 vertebra level. Immediate repair surgery was carried out.

## Methods
Under general anesthesia, a nasogastric tube was placed with the assistance of fiber-optic esophagoscopy. The anterolateral incision wound was opened and extended. A posterior esophageal wall laceration was found just anterior to the cervical spine stabilization plate. Esophageal perforation was then repaired using absorbable sutures. A sternocleidomastoid muscle flap was mobilized and wrapped around the perforation site, securing it to the esophageal wall. A suction drainage was positioned in the prevertebral space, and the skin was loosely closed.

On the second day post-surgery, the patient developed fever, neck swelling, and signs of infection. Despite parenteral administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics, cardiac arrest occurred on the third postoperative day. After successful resuscitation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation was used emergently. On suspicion of recurrent esophageal leakage, an emergent surgery was performed for inspection. The anterolateral incision was reopened, and a thorough debridement was carried out. A suction-irrigation draining system consisting of two plastic tubes was placed in the prevertebral space—one for irrigation and the other for drainage.

## Results
Saline was used for continuous irrigation, with daily use of low-dose hydrogen peroxide. The wound was kept open for six weeks, and the irrigation-drainage system was left in place for an additional four weeks. Leakage was monitored using a leak test by asking the patient to drink water. The drainage tube was removed when the leak test became negative. No esophageal leakage was found on the contrast swallow radiograph (Figure 3). The patient resumed a soft oral diet and was discharged. At the six-month follow-up, the patient had fully recovered and was on a normal diet. Neither CT nor MRI scans indicated signs of infection or loosening of instrumentation (Figures 4 and 5).

## Discussion
In our case, esophageal perforation was treated similarly to other reported methods, including early surgical repair with reinforcement using a sternocleidomastoid muscle flap. However, the initial repair failed as the esophagus could not prevent leakage of alimentary fluid after surgery. The vacuum drain also failed when the leaked fluid accumulated along with inflammatory fluid in the closed wound.

Our experience suggests that open drainage with irrigation can salvage such situations. Irrigation with isotonic saline around the esophageal leak helps dilute the leaked alimentary fluids and clear dead spaces in the neck. An open wound allows better control over infections caused by anaerobic organisms.

## Conclusion
Open-wound management creates an environment that promotes the healing of esophageal perforation and reduces the risk of infection. This method is superior in comparison to the standard techniques recommended in the literature, as it eliminates the need for instrumentation removal.

## Key Points
- Early surgical treatment is essential for managing esophageal complications.
- Special attention should be paid to wound management after repair.
- Open-wound management plays a critical role in healing esophageal perforation and resolving secondary infections by facilitating drainage.
- Open-wound management also negates the need for instrumentation removal.
