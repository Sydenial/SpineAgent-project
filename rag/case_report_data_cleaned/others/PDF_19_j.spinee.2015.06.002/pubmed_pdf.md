# 'Snake Eye' Appearance on a Teenage Girl with Spontaneous Spinal Ischemia

## Case Report

A 17-year-old girl was admitted to our hospital with the complaint of sudden difficulty in walking. On neurological examination, bilateral weakness and hypoesthesia of pain and temperature sensation with relative sparing of proprioception and vibratory sense below the level of C4 were observed. Laboratory examination results were unremarkable.  

On the second day of admission, she developed bladder and bowel dysfunction. Magnetic resonance imaging of the cervicothoracic spine was performed. Axial T2-weighted images revealed the “snake eye” appearance in the anterior horn of the spinal cord. Sagittal T2-weighted images demonstrated spinal cord hyperintensity in the anterior part of the cervical and thoracic spine in multiple segments, extending from the C4 level to the T6 level. Diffusion-weighted images showed hyperintense signal changes consistent with diffusion restriction in the cervicothoracic spine. No contrast enhancement was observed after gadolinium administration. Computed tomography angiography was normal.  

The sudden onset of symptoms, “snake eye” appearance, anterior column involvement, and restricted diffusion on diffusion-weighted imaging suggested an initial diagnosis of spontaneous spinal ischemia. Anticoagulant therapy was initiated. Except for bladder and bowel dysfunction, all symptoms resolved within 12 days of admission. After two months, all symptoms had completely resolved, and neurological examination was entirely normal.  

Spontaneous spinal ischemia is an extremely rare condition in the pediatric population and is generally associated with a poor prognosis. Early diagnosis is crucial for timely treatment, which can optimize clinical outcomes.
