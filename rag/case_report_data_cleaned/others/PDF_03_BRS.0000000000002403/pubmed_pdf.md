# Spinal gout without spinal symptom in a junior school student: a case report

## Abstract

**Study Design:** Case report.

**Objectives:** We report a case of a 16-year-old boy with intermittent and migratory polyarthralgia, who presented a diagnostic dilemma.

**Summary of Background Data:** Spinal involvement without spinal symptoms in gout seems to be rare. However, the relationship of spinal gout to symptoms is poorly understood.

**Methods:** Description of the case report.

**Results:** Laboratory findings could not explain his symptoms; however, computed tomography (CT) of the pelvis revealed a space-occupying lesion involving the left side of the spine at the L5–S1 level, and biopsy revealed urate crystals, which helped establish the diagnosis of spinal gout.

**Conclusions:** Gout can be a cunning disease with various manifestations, and spinal involvement can be asymptomatic.

**Key Words:** Albuminuria; Asymptomatic urate deposition; Gout; GSDI; Hyperuricemia; Kelley–Seegmiller syndrome; Metabolic syndrome; Partial HPRT deficiency; Polyarthralgia; Spine; Spinal gout.

**Level of Evidence:** 4.

## Introduction

Spinal gout has been reported in limited numbers of cases. In a review by Toprover et al [1], a total of 131 published spinal gout cases were found by 2015. Similarly, a study by De Mello et al [2] found that 12 of 42 gout patients (29%) had CT evidence of spinal gout. Thus, the actual prevalence of spinal gout is likely underestimated, and caution is needed when dealing with patients with gout and spinal pain.

We report a unique case of spinal gout with intermittent and migratory polyarthralgia, in which spinal symptoms or typical gout manifestations were absent.

## Case Report

A 16-year-old boy of Han nationality was admitted to our hospital because of intermittent and migratory polyarthralgia with local warmth and swelling of 14 weeks’ duration, concomitant with low-grade fever. There was no chill, no morning stiffness, and no back pain or pain radiating down the lower limbs. Physical examination revealed nothing other than overweight (Body Mass Index $=31.5$ kg/m$^{2}$). Additionally, no neurologic disorder or positive family history was found.

The patient had reportedly been in normal health, with the exception of a history of first metatarsophalangeal joint pain and swelling about 2 years earlier, occurring after football playing the day before, with a normal serum uric acid (SUA) level; trauma was considered. Thereafter, intermittent migratory joint pain occurred and healed spontaneously within 1–2 days.

No positive diagnosis was made at that time, and treatment was symptomatic with Qufengzhitong Capsule (a compound preparation of Chinese herbs). All symptoms were relieved within about one week.

Four weeks before presentation, the symptoms arose again and he came to our clinic. Laboratory findings were significant for elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) 7.7 mg/L (N $<5$), complement C3 2.53 g/L (N 0.9–1.8), and complement C4 0.87 g/L (N 0.1–0.4). In addition, the full blood count, anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP), antiperinuclear factor (APF), antikeratin antibody (AKA), antinuclear antibodies (ANA), IgA, IgM, IgG, IgE, and human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-B27 were all within normal ranges.

Considering pain and swelling in multiple peripheral joints with negative rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP, juvenile ankylosing spondylitis was suspected. CT scanning of the pelvis was performed and revealed a space-occupying lesion involving the left side of the spine at the L5–S1 level; schwannoma was considered. However, subsequent biopsy revealed urate crystals.

The patient was admitted as an inpatient. His blood pressure was 130/85 mmHg. Laboratory findings showed white blood cells 15,000/mm$^{3}$ with 85% neutrophils; total cholesterol 5.92 mmol/L (N $<5.2$); triglycerides 2.7 mmol/L (N $<1.7$); low-density lipoprotein cholesterol 3.5 mmol/L (N $<3.12$); SUA 804 μmol/L (N 223–416); 24 h urate excretion 4861 μmol/24 h (N 1500–4400); proteinuria was positive with “++” on urinalysis; 24 h urinary protein 2.87 g/24 h (N $<0.14$); estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) 57.97 mL/min/1.73 m$^{2}$ (N $>90$); creatinine and blood urea nitrogen were still in the normal range; CRP 7.3 mg/L (N $<5$); erythrocyte sedimentation rate 25 mm/h (N $<20$). In addition, tests for human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis, and tuberculosis were negative. Color Doppler ultrasound examination of the liver, spleen, and kidneys showed no abnormal changes. Dual-energy CT was also used to confirm urate crystals in the spine. Finally, the diagnosis of spinal gout was made.

A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug and febuxostat were prescribed, and he was discharged as his clinical symptoms markedly improved within one week. He was followed up in the outpatient clinic with urate-lowering therapy over the next 6 months; he remained well, with no new arthritis or spinal symptoms. He was encouraged to adhere to urate-lowering therapy, with a plan for CT review one year later. Renal function was monitored, and a healthy diet and lifestyle were recommended.

## Discussion

Gout is an inflammatory arthritis due to deposition of monosodium urate crystals, which form in a milieu influenced by serum uric acid, temperature, salt concentration, and cartilage matrix components [4].

The exact aetiology of crystal accumulation in the spine is also unknown. Degenerative disease of the spine, erosion of tissues, previous injuries, or spinal osteoarthritis may contribute [3]. Volkov et al [5] suggested that poor vascularization in the axial skeleton creates an optimal environment for crystal formation. Combined with hyperlipidemia, obesity, and minimal physical activity, together with small caliber vasculature, inadequate filtration of uric acid load may lead to deposition and eventual crystal formation. This explanation seems fitting for our case, given overweight with hyperlipidemia and little physical activity, which commonly occurs in senior school students under heavy schoolwork.

Clinical manifestations of spinal gout range from asymptomatic radiographic lesions [6] to back pain or paraparesis [7], and even encephalopathy. Toprover et al [1] found that among previously reported cases of spinal gout, back pain was the most common symptom, and neurological symptoms were the second most common complaint.

Furthermore, two cases were diagnosed incidentally on autopsy, while spinal symptoms were absent when the patients were alive. Both were middle-aged men with poorly controlled gout for several years, combined with hypertension, and died of renal failure [9,10]. Except for spinal involvement, urate crystals could also be found in peripheral joints. One of them even had severe deforming arthritis and obvious subcutaneous urate deposition because of 32-year chronic gout.

To the best of our knowledge, our patient would be the third case with no spinal symptoms diagnosed by biopsy. However, different from the previous cases, our patient presented with intermittent and migratory polyarthralgia for 14 weeks’ duration, and no joint deformation or subcutaneous urate deposition had emerged at this stage.

Asymptomatic spinal gout seems to be rare, but some studies [2,10] indicated that there was no correlation between symptoms (pain or neurological syndromes) and spinal gout. It may be missed because CT is often not performed when no spinal symptoms are present.

Intermittent, mild polyarthralgia healing spontaneously within 1–2 days and negative rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP can be seen in palindromic rheumatism (PR). However, before PR can be diagnosed, other arthritides such as rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, or gout should be excluded [11]. Many tests were performed for differential diagnosis, and a pelvic CT scan provided an important clue.

Atypical symptoms often make diagnosis difficult. In our case, this 16-year-old boy had a normal SUA level at his first visit and no definite history of gout.

Normal SUA levels in spinal gout have been reported [14]. SUA can fall into the normal range during acute gout in a substantial proportion of patients [4]. This phenomenon may relate to increased urinary excretion of urate, and inflammatory factors such as IL-6 might play a role [15]. Our patient’s SUA was normal at the first visit; thus, SUA may not sufficiently represent the body urate pool in some episodes.

What caused the development of hyperuricemia and gout at such a young age? Autosomal dominant tubulointerstitial kidney disease (ADTKD) should be suspected in early-onset hyperuricemia and gout [16]. Fractional urate excretion is usually decreased ($<5\%$) in uromodulin-associated ADTKD, leading to early-onset hyperuricemia and gout. In this patient, 24 h urate excretion was elevated, suggesting overproduction rather than impaired excretion, although renal histological examination or genetic testing for ADTKD was not performed.

Hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HPRT) deficiency can cause hyperuricemia and hyperuricosuria, accompanied by severe neurologic disorders such as choreoathetosis, mental retardation, and self-injurious behavior [17]. In some reports [18], neurologic disorder can be mild (Kelley–Seegmiller syndrome). Enzyme activity testing and purine metabolism evaluation would help, but evidence for HPRT deficiency was not strong in this patient.

Glycogen storage disease type I (GSDI) is associated with hyperuricemia and hyperlipidemia in early life, and presents with hepatomegaly and renomegaly due to glycogen metabolism defects. Long-term complications of untreated GSDI include gout, renal disease, poor growth, and delayed puberty [19]. However, no hepatomegaly or renomegaly was found in our patient, and he had normal growth and puberty development with a stature of 178 cm; therefore, GSDI was unlikely.

We also considered that high SUA might contribute to albuminuria and decline in eGFR. Takae et al [20] reported that higher uric acid levels are a significant risk factor for developing renal insufficiency and albuminuria. Another study [21] concluded that hyperuricemia could cause glomerular sclerosis and renal arteriolar damage through activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system or inflammatory reactions. Hyperuricemia is also associated with a greater risk of kidney stones, which can damage renal function, although ultrasound did not identify stones in our patient.

## Conclusion

In summary, gout is widely recognized, but it remains a disease with various manifestations. Spinal involvement seems rare, but its incidence and prevalence may be underestimated because crystal accumulation in the spine can be asymptomatic. Tests of albuminuria and eGFR should be performed for gout patients with hyperuricemia to comprehensively evaluate renal function.
