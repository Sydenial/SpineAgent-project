# Proximal Junctional Kyphosis After Posterior Spinal Fusion for Severe Kyphoscoliosis in a Patient With PIEZO2-Deficient Arthrogryposis Syndrome

## Abstract

**Study Design:** Case report.

**Objective:** To describe the clinical and radiological outcomes of surgical treatment for severe kyphoscoliosis in a patient with PIEZO2-deficient arthrogryposis and to report the occurrence of proximal junctional kyphosis after posterior spinal fusion.

**Summary of Background Data:** Spinal deformity is a characteristic feature of arthrogryposis due to PIEZO2 gene deficiency. Surgical correction is indicated when the deformity is progressive to avoid neurological deficits and respiratory impairment. However, few reports have described surgical treatment for spinal deformity in patients with PIEZO2 deficiency.

**Methods:** We retrospectively reviewed a case of proximal junctional kyphosis following posterior spinal fusion for severe kyphoscoliosis.

**Results:** The patient was a 13-year-old girl with PIEZO2-deficient arthrogryposis who underwent posterior spinal fusion with an all-pedicle screw construct from T2 to L2. The preoperative main thoracic curve Cobb angle was 78 degrees, and thoracic kyphosis was 83 degrees. Postoperatively, the Cobb angle improved to 11 degrees and thoracic kyphosis to 34 degrees. Proximal junctional kyphosis developed postoperatively, leading to neurological symptoms that required revision surgery. At final follow-up, the patient was ambulatory with crutches and showed improvements in clinical questionnaire scores.

**Conclusions:** Posterior spinal fusion represents a viable treatment option for severe spinal deformity in PIEZO2-deficient arthrogryposis. Careful selection of fusion levels is essential to prevent proximal junctional kyphosis.

**Key Words:** Posterior spinal fusion; PIEZO2; arthrogryposis; kyphoscoliosis; radiological findings; proximal junctional kyphosis.

**Level of Evidence:** 5.

## Key Points

- Spinal deformity is a characteristic feature of arthrogryposis caused by PIEZO2 gene deficiency.  
- This report describes a case of proximal junctional kyphosis after posterior spinal fusion for severe kyphoscoliosis in PIEZO2-deficient arthrogryposis.  
- Posterior spinal fusion can be an effective treatment for severe spinal deformity in this condition, but fusion levels must be selected carefully.

## Introduction

Arthrogryposis is a condition characterized by congenital joint contractures and muscle weakness [1]. Deficiency of the PIEZO2 gene, which encodes a mechanosensitive ion channel expressed in dorsal root ganglion neurons and sensory endings, has been implicated in a distinctive form of arthrogryposis accompanied by severe scoliosis [2–5]. Although several reports have described the overall clinical characteristics of PIEZO2-deficient arthrogryposis, detailed descriptions of sagittal spinal deformity and spinal measurements, such as Cobb angles, remain limited.

Yamaguchi et al. previously reported the clinical characteristics and disease course of a patient with PIEZO2-deficient arthrogryposis [10]. To date, few studies have addressed surgical correction of spinal deformity in this condition, and no standard treatment strategy has been established. Here, we describe the clinical and radiological outcomes of surgical treatment for severe kyphoscoliosis in a patient with PIEZO2-deficient arthrogryposis.

## Case Presentation

A 13-year-old girl was diagnosed with PIEZO2-deficient arthrogryposis in early childhood and had used crutches for ambulation since that time. Scoliosis was first noted at approximately four years of age, and the deformity progressed with associated gait disturbance by the age of 12 years. At presentation, her height was 131 cm, weight was 30 kg, and body mass index was 17.5 kg/m². Lumbar spine and total hip bone mineral densities were low at 0.15 g/cm² and 0.722 g/cm², respectively.

Preoperative Scoliosis Research Society-22 (SRS-22) domain scores were 4.4 for function, 4.8 for pain, 2.4 for self-image, 4.0 for mental health, and 3.9 for subtotal score. Radiographic evaluation demonstrated a main thoracic curve (T3–11) with a Cobb angle of 78 degrees and thoracic kyphosis (T1–12) of 83 degrees. No obvious vertebral anomalies were observed.

Posterior spinal fusion was performed using an all-pedicle screw construct from T2 to L2. Ponte osteotomy was not performed. The operative time was 307 minutes, and estimated blood loss was 650 g. Postoperatively, the main thoracic Cobb angle improved to 11 degrees, and thoracic kyphosis improved to 34 degrees.

Three months after surgery, proximal junctional kyphosis measuring 70 degrees was identified without neurological deficits. Seven months postoperatively, the patient developed progressive lower extremity weakness and became unable to walk. Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging revealed spinal cord compression due to increased kyphosis at the proximal adjacent fusion level. Revision posterior spinal fusion from C6 to T2 was performed. Operative time for the revision was 238 minutes, with blood loss of 100 g.

Two months after revision surgery, the patient was able to walk with crutches. Lower extremity weakness fully resolved by one year postoperatively. Computed tomography confirmed solid bone union nine months after revision surgery. At two years and three months after the initial surgery, SRS-22 scores showed improvement across all domains, including satisfaction. The patient reported improvements in posture, appetite, weight, and breathing, with no radiological deterioration. Her visual analogue scale score for low back pain was 0, and she remained ambulatory with crutches.

## Discussion

In this case of PIEZO2-deficient arthrogryposis treated with posterior spinal fusion for severe kyphoscoliosis, pedicle screw fixation achieved substantial correction of spinal deformity. However, proximal junctional kyphosis developed postoperatively and resulted in neurological deterioration that necessitated revision surgery.

Previous reports indicate that the majority of patients with PIEZO2-deficient arthrogryposis present with severe scoliosis [6–9], yet detailed evaluations of sagittal alignment and spinal measurements are scarce. Therefore, management strategies are often extrapolated from the broader literature on arthrogryposis multiplex congenita.

Spinal instrumentation in arthrogryposis is challenging because of associated muscle weakness, joint stiffness, and perioperative respiratory risks. Although adequate correction was initially achieved in this patient, severe proximal junctional kyphosis developed shortly after surgery. Preservation of the T1–2 interspinous ligament was attempted, but proximal junctional kyphosis still occurred. Selection of T1 as the upper instrumented vertebra might have reduced this risk.

The reported prevalence of proximal junctional kyphosis ranges from 10% to 27% in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis and may be higher in other complex deformities. This case underscores the importance of careful determination of fusion levels and consideration of sagittal alignment when treating severe kyphoscoliosis in PIEZO2-deficient arthrogryposis.

## Conclusion

Posterior spinal fusion can provide effective correction for severe spinal deformity in patients with PIEZO2-deficient arthrogryposis. However, meticulous planning of fusion levels is critical to minimize the risk of proximal junctional kyphosis and subsequent neurological complications.
