# Spinal fusion as a viable treatment option for scoliosis management in Pompe disease: a postoperative 3-year follow-up

## Abstract

### Purpose
This study presents 3-year postoperative outcomes of posterior spinal correction and fusion of a patient diagnosed with late-onset Pompe disease (PD) for his progressive scoliosis.

### Methods
The patient was diagnosed with PD during infancy. Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) was initiated at the age of 13. The first office visit for his spinal deformity was at the age of 15, and spinal curvatures of 40°, 34°, and 6° were seen in T6–L3, T1–T6, and L3–S, respectively. Reduced pulmonary function, limited gait function, and atrophied limbs were documented. Initial brace treatment could not control curve progression; therefore, posterior spinal correction and fusion were performed at the age of 17.

### Results
Immediate preoperative curves of 55°, 42°, and 23° were corrected to 18°, 26°, and 7° in T6–L2, T1–T6, and L2–S, respectively. Spinal fusion was performed from T3 to L4. The patient exhibited excessively low pulmonary function preoperatively with a VC, FVC, and %VC of 1.45 L, 1.36 L, and 35%, respectively. This was managed with only moderate reductions despite reduced pulmonary function from PD throughout the operative period and at 3 years. At the final follow-up, VC, FVC, and %VC were 1.33 L, 1.12 L, and 28.5%, respectively.

### Conclusion
Posterior spinal correction and fusion adequately controlled spinal curvatures for 3 years after surgery. Additionally, pulmonary function was managed throughout the follow-up period. Despite ERT, skeletal muscle and pulmonary function can still be severely affected by PD. Spinal correction and fusion is a useful method for the management of spinal curvature and pulmonary function in patients with PD.

## Introduction

Alpha-glucosidase deficiency syndrome, commonly known as Pompe disease (PD), is caused by a mutation in the glucosidase, alpha; acid (GAA) gene. It is a genetic autosomal recessive disorder resulting in the deficiency or dysfunctional lysosomal alpha-glucosidase enzyme. A deficiency of this enzyme leads to an accretion of internal glycogen in the lysosomes of muscular tissue, and further induces autophagic build-up within the cell [1]. The onset of PD can occur anytime after birth and, once triggered, it was considered fatal until the usage of enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) in recent years [2]. The estimated prevalence of PD in the world is between 1 in 33,000 and 1 in 300,000 [3].

In recent years, the role of abnormal activation of autophagic pathways has been brought to attention in patients with PD, which damages normal tissue structure and replaces healthy muscle fibres with autophagic debris [4, 5]. Clinically, severe muscle weakness and atrophy [6], and increased prevalence of spinal deformity were reported in patients with PD. For example, Roberts et al. [3] found spinal deformities in 33% of patients with PD. Spinal deformities in patients with PD are especially problematic since their cardiopulmonary systems are already compromised by their weakened muscles. The aforementioned study by Roberts et al. [3] also compared the pulmonary function of scoliotic and non-scoliotic patients with PD and found significantly reduced pulmonary function in the scoliosis group [3].

Pompe disease is considered a neuromuscular disorder and shares many common grounds with other diseases that cause neuromuscular scoliosis. Neuromuscular scoliosis is known to continue its progression beyond the age of maturity and has known implications in the loss of sitting and standing posture as well as implications to pulmonary and cardiac function [3]. Mild spinal deformities are commonly treated by bracing [7], and more severe scoliosis cases require surgical interventions [7]. However, operative treatments are not always possible in severe cases. For example, Haaker et al. [8] reported surgical intervention in patients with PD for scoliosis and other orthopedic morbidities, describing four patients indicated for surgery, yet only half were able to receive the treatment due to poor pulmonary function, respiratory disorder, and neurological issues [8]. Of the two that received surgical treatment, only one was available for regular follow-up, whereas data for the other patient were available 7 months post-operation. Posterior fusion via a screw and wire construct was employed in both patients: the major curve was corrected from 60° to 23° at L1–L5 in the patient without follow-up, and from 90° to 35° at T6–L5 in the patient whose 2-year follow-up was possible. At 2-year follow-up, scoliosis was managed with a major curve of 37°.

In this study, outcomes were followed for 3 years after spine surgery. The purpose of this study is to present 3-year postoperative outcomes of posterior spinal correction and fusion of a patient diagnosed with late-onset PD for his progressive scoliosis.

## Clinical overview

A boy was born at 38 weeks’ gestation, with a body weight of 3124 g. His first unassisted gait was at 1 year and 4 months, and motor development delay was recognized at the age of 1. Through muscle biopsy and blood tests, the patient was diagnosed as PD at the age of 1. There was no family history of PD. The patient received his first ERT at age 13 and continued the therapy biweekly until present. The late initiation of ERT was due to the unavailability of alglucosidase alpha in Japan until 2006. The patient did not exhibit any signs of infusion-associated reaction.

He first visited our institution for his spinal deformity at age 15. On physical examination, the patient showed scoliosis along with classical signs of PD such as winged scapula, drastically atrophied limbs and torso, muscle weakness, and breathing difficulties. No neurological or cardiological abnormalities were observed. Blood tests demonstrated elevated levels of creatine kinase (CK), a biomarker for PD. At the first visit, his major curve (MC) scoliosis was 40° (T6–L3), 34° in the upper curve (UC, T1–T6), and $6^{\circ}$ at the lower curve (LC, L3–S).

The patient started a brace treatment program; however, steady progression of scoliosis was seen for 2 years and 6 months until surgery was planned. The patient reported persistent low back pain and hip pain from sitting. In addition, the patient exhibited reduced ability to keep sitting posture for an extended duration of time, where he would start to decompensate to the side of scoliotic concavity. The patient also complained of pain at the apical area of the thoracic curve and the lower side of the opposing lumbar, which are symptoms representative of fatigue pain arising from the muscles around the apical area in scoliosis. At the same visit, breathing difficulty was noted and the patient started a nighttime biphasic positive airway pressure (BIPAP) regimen. Blood gas was not available, as it was not measured due to lack of cyanosis and dyspnea during rest; breathing difficulties were assumed to be a result of reduced vital capacity due to a restrictive lung condition.

Although the patient was able to walk unassisted for short distances, he had difficulty keeping balance during gait and frequently fell down due to progressive muscular atrophy. Therefore, his primary means of transfer was using a wheelchair. At the age of 17 years and 8 months, the patient was 158.5 cm in height and 31 kg in weight; his scoliosis had progressed to 55° in the MC (T6–L2), 42° in the UC (T1–T6), and 23° in the LC (L2–S). Due to inability to keep constant sitting posture, pain in the low back and apical area during sitting, and the progressive nature of neuromuscular scoliosis, surgical treatment was suggested to him and his family. Upon agreement for surgical intervention, a total of 8 units of blood were preoperatively drawn in preparation for autotransfusion.

Posterior spinal fusion and correction was performed from T2 to L4 using pedicle screws segmentally with local bone graft and allograft. Spinal curvatures were corrected in the MC, UC, and LC angles to 18°, 26°, and 7°, respectively. The total surgical time was 5 h and 43 min, with an estimated blood loss (EBL) of 5732 mL with cell saver employed. The patient required 10 units of blood transfusion in addition to autologous blood transfusion and platelet concentrate (PC) transfusions of 20 units. Dural tear and excessive EBL were two intraoperative complications recognized during this surgery. Induction of general anesthesia was uneventful.

The patient received biweekly ERT and one ERT infusion during the stay in our institution. The patient was on BIPAP throughout most of the stay; however, breathing became stabilized and only required BIPAP during the night at the end. The patient was able to regain ambulation at the time of discharge. The patient exhibited biomarkers of PD including elevated CK, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) [9].

Postoperatively, the patient did not have any complications from scoliosis surgery and curve corrections were maintained throughout follow-up. At follow-up, wheelchair use for 3–4 h a day was reported due to increasing muscle weakness in lower extremities. No low back pain was reported for 2 years after surgery. The patient was able to keep sitting posture for extended duration without decompensation and no longer felt pain in the apical area that existed preoperatively. At the 3-year postoperative visit, progressively worsening muscle strength was observed: 2/5 in knee extension and flexion, 2/5 ankle dorsiflexion, 3/5 in ankle plantar flexion, and 3/5 in biceps and triceps flexion. Nighttime use of BIPAP remained and sleep disturbance was reported. Pulmonary function test (PFT) data obtained at the 1-year postoperative visit showed reduced values compared with those obtained at the 3-month postoperative follow-up; pulmonary physical exercise was conducted and taught to the patient.

Despite correction of spinal deformity, PD symptoms progressed slowly with increased weakness in the extremities. It is important to note that despite a large fusion area, the patient showed growth in height to 184 cm; nevertheless, he maintained a low body weight of 33 kg due to PD.

The patient’s preoperative PFT values consisted of a VC of 1.45 L, forced vital capacity (FVC) of 1.36 L, and percent vital capacity (%VC) of 35%. At the 3 months’ postoperative follow-up, VC, FVC, and %VC were 1.31 L, 1.20 L, and 32.3%, respectively. At the postoperative 1-year visit, VC, FVC, and %VC were 0.82 L, 0.80 L, and 18.1%. At the final visit, PFT results at postoperative 3 years for VC, FVC, and %VC were 1.33 L, 1.12 L, and 28.5%, respectively.

Predicted VC for the preoperative %VC values had been corrected for height using the equation by Kono et al. in order to account for height difference from scoliosis [10].

The patient’s qualitative clinical outcome was measured using the SRS-30 questionnaire collected preoperatively and postoperatively over 3-year follow-up. Preoperative mean scores for each domain (function/activity, pain, self image/appearance, mental health) were 3.6, 4.4, 2.5, and 4.4, respectively. Mean scores for the aforementioned four domains, followed by the satisfaction with management domain at the patient’s postoperative 4-year follow-up were 3.5, 2.0, 2.9, 3.2, and 2.3, respectively.

## Discussion

Pompe disease is a rare neuromuscular disorder that results in progressive muscular atrophy that may manifest throughout the body in terms of cardiac, skeletal, and pulmonary systems. Once a fatal disorder, PD can now be managed through ERT. Despite the availability of treatment, patients do suffer from other complications that persist, such as scoliosis and progressive muscular atrophy. Previous accounts of surgical treatment of scoliosis in patients with PD are rare since ERT only became available in recent years.

A large discrepancy is seen in the changes in height and weight when comparing preoperative values with immediate postoperative and postoperative 3-year values, and this may be attributed to progressive muscular atrophy due to PD. Around the time of operation and at discharge, the patient was able to walk unassisted, albeit unbalanced and frequently tripping. Yet at 2- and 3-year follow-up, the patient was unable to keep standing posture for less than a minute and required constant wheelchair use for locomotion. The same can be said for pulmonary function: pulmonary function parameters (VC, %VC, tidal volume [TV], FVC, forced expiratory volume in 1.0 second [FEV1.0]) did not recover to or beyond preoperative values even at postoperative 3 years.

The efficacy of the conducted surgery should be considered based upon several factors. First and foremost is the potential of further progression of scoliosis to a point that may start to affect pulmonary function as well as activities of daily living (ADL). Neuromuscular scoliosis, unlike many forms of idiopathic scoliosis, commonly continues to progress beyond the age of maturity. This patient showed a progressive course of scoliosis curvature beyond 50° in the 2 years prior to operation, and therefore was indicated for surgery to avoid further progression. In patients with skeletal muscle disease who have weak pulmonary function to begin with, any further physical changes such as scoliosis that may affect the dynamics of the thoracic cavity can have detrimental effects on breathing mechanics.

Secondly, the patient showed inability to keep adequate sitting posture for extended duration of time, with obvious decompensation to the side of scoliotic concavity. Through this surgery, these symptoms were treated by providing a stable trunk that allows for postural stability despite easily fatigued muscles and progressive atrophy. Furthermore, since the patient did not have the ability to use full range of motion (ROM) in the limbs even preoperatively, the limitation imposed by fusion did not have a detrimental effect on ADL. Finally, it is important to recognize that this condition is similar to other neuromuscular types of scoliosis and short apical fusion is not an option as the pathology affects the whole spine. As with other neuromuscular scoliosis, muscle balance deteriorates and trunk control is impeded. If only the thoracic spine were fused, correction to sitting posture could not be achieved. The strategy of this surgery is not only to alleviate worsening pulmonary function, but to maintain spinal balance to allow sitting position without support.

Additionally, when considering the preoperative and postoperative SRS-30 scores, a noticeable reduction in the pain domain and a modest increase in the self image/appearance domain were recognized at the patient’s postoperative 3-year follow-up. On the other hand, the patient exhibited a slight decrease in the function/activity domain and a minor decrease in the mental health domain. Taking both into account, it may be interpreted that the surgery was successful in reducing pain along with improving self-image, albeit increasingly disabling symptoms of PD further limit ADL along with potential negative psychological effects arising from living with disabilities. Psychological management along with further ADL support are issues that must be addressed when considering treatment of patients with PD and other chronic neuromuscular conditions.

This study is one of the first few studies to report surgical outcome of a patient with late-onset PD for scoliosis treatment with detailed pulmonary and scoliotic curvature measurements in prognosis over a 3-year period. Surgical outcome at the time of operation was good, despite the large amount of blood lost during surgery, and the patient showed modest recovery in pulmonary functions close to but not beyond preoperative values. The excessive blood loss may be a result of tissue weakness in various structures with abnormal autophagy seen in PD contributing to its manifestation, albeit no experimental validation is available that correlates tissue structure in patients with PD and blood loss during surgery [4, 5].

This surgery allowed the patient to keep stable sitting posture and trunk support as well as preventing further progression in scoliosis. The patient did show decreased reflex and further skeletal muscle atrophy in the limbs, most likely attributed to PD, and this is a shortcoming of the current ERT regimen. As seen in this patient, while ERT has decreased mortality risk in PD and patients live longer than pre-ERT accounts, complications previously discussed only sparsely in literature are now being observed, and clinicians have been given a chance to treat symptoms of PD beyond the primary objective of PD treatment.

## Conclusion

Surgical intervention for the treatment of scoliosis in Pompe disease remains a challenging field with varying degrees of muscular atrophy and pulmonary function. This study exhibited a well-maintained clinical outcome in terms of scoliosis and pulmonary function at postoperative 3 years with achievement of adequate sitting posture and reduction of pain, although limitations of ADL gradually aggravated due to muscle atrophy in a patient with PD. Treatment options require careful consideration of pulmonary and cardiac function, ADL, and potential course of natural history if scoliosis is not corrected in the setting of a neuromuscular condition.
