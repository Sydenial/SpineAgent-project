# Familial pseudotail, scoliosis and synpolydactyly syndrome

## Abstract

**Purpose**: This case series describes a novel condition characterized by familial pseudotail associated with scoliosis, and synpolydactyly that has not been previously reported in literature.  
**Methods**: The authors present three siblings and one cousin from the same family living in the northern region of the Arabian Peninsula. All cases presented with pseudotail, scoliosis, and complex synpolydactyly. The authors demonstrated complete clinical and radiological descriptions in addition to detailed performed surgeries.  
**Results**: The histopathological result of the resected pseudotail specimens revealed bony lesions covered with thick fibrous tissue and evidence of mature adipocytes within trabecular spaces.  
**Conclusions**: The described cases represent a novel condition that has not been previously reported in the literature. Familial pseudotail scoliosis synpolydactyly syndrome is a newly recognized form of familial pseudotail.  

**Keywords**: Familial, Pseudotail, Scoliosis, Synpolydactyly, Syndrome

## Introduction

The etiology of congenital sacral abnormalities is still not well understood; however, maternal diabetes has been considered a primary cause, with 16% of the cases born to diabetic mothers. On the other hand, other authors have suggested that exposure to chemicals like acetone could be the cause. Some have reported that a familial genetic mutation is responsible for these abnormalities.

Caudal appendages are uncommon congenital malformations, presenting as either a true tail or a pseudotail. The true tail is usually a rare remnant of the embryonic tail containing adipose tissue, striated muscle, blood vessels, and nerves, covered by normal skin.

Human tail embryology is poorly understood, but it is generally believed to result from a disturbance of caudal regression during the sixth week of embryonic life.

In this case series, the authors describe a novel condition characterized by familial pseudotail, scoliosis, and synpolydactyly, previously unreported in the literature.

## Materials and Methods

### Case 1

RA is a 12-year-old female from the northern region of the Arabian Peninsula who was born with bilateral synpolydactyly in both hands and syndactyly in both feet. She had a full-term, normal vaginal pregnancy from consanguineous parents with no medical history of disease. At age four, she underwent several surgical procedures to correct her hand synpolydactyly. A year later, her parents noticed she had a deformed back. A scoliosis screening series showed a curve of around 20 degrees. By age 12, her scoliosis curve progressed to 65 degrees, at which time she was referred for treatment.

Her chief complaints were scoliosis, synpolydactyly of both hands (type SD 2), syndactyly of both feet, and a hard mass at the end of her tailbone causing pain when sitting. Family history revealed that she has two younger siblings with the same condition: a 10-year-old sister and a 4-year-old brother.

Physical examination revealed a left thoracolumbar curve, moderate hump, and a right proximal thoracic compensatory curve. Neurological examination was normal. A skin dimple with a thick, pigmented, overlaying subcutaneous bony prominence was felt at the lower end of the tailbone. The pseudotail showed signs of chronic pressure at its tip, without pressure sores or hair tufts.

X-rays revealed a left thoracolumbar curve with a Cobb's angle of 90 degrees and a right proximal compensatory thoracic curve with an angle of 40 degrees. A CT scan of the lumbosacral spine showed a transverse sacrum and a retroverted coccyx. MRI of the pseudotail showed a dark signal with no nerve tissue.

The patient was referred for further evaluations, which confirmed the absence of other clinical abnormalities. Genetic testing and chromosomal analysis were negative for any known genetic disorders.

A scoliosis correction surgery with excision of the pseudotail was performed, followed by successful post-operative follow-ups for one year.

### Case 2

ARA is a 10-year-old female, the younger sister of RA. She presented with synpolydactyly of both hands and feet, mild scoliosis, and pseudotail. Her perinatal history was similar to that of her older sister. A pediatric dysmorphologist and a geneticist did not find any known syndrome in her case.

Physical examination revealed mild right thoracic scoliosis and a hard bony prominence at the lower end of the tailbone. X-rays showed a mild right thoracic curve with a Cobb's angle of 12 degrees. CT of the lumbosacral spine showed a retroverted coccyx. MRI showed no spinal cord abnormalities.

Due to mild scoliosis, no surgical correction was offered. Pseudotail excision was recommended to relieve sitting pain, but her parents opted to defer the procedure until scoliosis correction was needed.

### Case 3

ANA is a 4-year-old male, the younger brother of RA and ARA, presenting with the same triad of pseudotail, scoliosis, and synpolydactyly. Upon examination, there were no neurological deficits. X-rays confirmed left curve scoliosis with a Cobb's angle of 40 degrees. CT and MRI of the lumbosacral spine showed pseudotail and tethered cord.

Surgery was recommended to release the tethered cord and excise the pseudotail. The patient underwent surgery and was followed up for scoliosis progression. 

### Case 4

ABA is a 12-year-old male cousin of the other three cases. He presented with pseudotail, scoliosis, and synpolydactyly. His back examination revealed scoliosis and a hard bony mass at the tailbone. X-rays showed moderate right curve scoliosis with a Cobb's angle of 38 degrees. CT scan revealed a retroverted coccyx and sacral abnormalities.

Surgical excision of the pseudotail was recommended for cosmetic reasons. A posterior approach was used to expose the pseudotail, and the retroverted coccyx was excised.

## Results

Both parents of the described cases (RA, ARA, ANA, and ABA) were evaluated clinically and radiologically, showing no evidence of scoliosis, syndactyly, or pseudotail. All cases underwent reconstructive surgeries for synpolydactyly. Three of the cases underwent pseudotail excision with satisfactory outcomes, while one (ARA) opted to delay surgery.

## Discussion

The pseudotail is a protrusion of the lumbosacral area, commonly composed of bone, cartilage, and other spinal elements. The condition has been associated with various spinal abnormalities, but the association of pseudotail with scoliosis and synpolydactyly has not been previously reported.

The pathogenesis of the pseudotail is not fully understood, and the treatment standard is still under study. Surgical excision is performed to relieve pain and improve cosmetic appearance. Long-term follow-up is necessary to assess for potential complications such as sacral regeneration.

The described cases represent a novel condition of familial pseudotail scoliosis synpolydactyly syndrome (FPSSS), a previously unrecognized syndrome.
