# Costotransverse Screw Fixation in a Severe Cervicothoracic Deformity Due to Type 1 Neurofibromatosis: Case Report

## Abstract

**Background Context.** Spinal implant placement may be challenging in cases of severe cervicothoracic spinal deformities and anatomical anomalies, such as in type 1 neurofibromatosis. Intralaminar screw fixation of the thoracic spine has been described in a few cases in which pedicles were hypoplastic. The costovertebral joints have never been used before as an anchorage point for screws.

**Purpose.** To describe a new thoracic fixation technique to be used in severe deformities whenever the posterior arch (laminae and pedicles) is not available due to anatomic abnormalities.

**Study Design.** Case report.

**Methods.** An 18-year-old female with progressive tetraparesis caused by increasing deformity of the cervicothoracic spine underwent evaluation and surgical treatment. The procedure and techniques were described. Clinical features, radiological findings, and outcomes were assessed. Complications and local recurrences were also recorded.

**Results.** Costotransverse joint screwing was successfully used in one case of severe cervicothoracic spine deformity with major hypoplasia of the pedicles. The posterior arch of one thoracic vertebra became mobile soon after periosteal stripping, probably due to iatrogenic fracture of the only existent pedicle. The four-cortical trajectory of the screws resulted in good bone purchase, allowing completion of the procedure. No local or general complications were recorded during two years of follow-up.

**Conclusion.** The procedure was used as a salvage technique during a difficult surgery in which a local complication forced a change of strategy. Although the implant remained stable long enough to achieve fusion, it involves placing a screw through a joint that remains slightly mobile, which could possibly result in long-term screw loosening if fusion is not achieved. This technique should be considered only when all other options have been explored and excluded for anatomical reasons.

**Key Words.** Costotransverse screws; thoracic spine; neurofibromatosis; cervical kyphosis.

## Introduction

Surgery of severe kyphotic deformities of the cervical and thoracic spine in type 1 neurofibromatosis remains challenging even for experienced surgeons. Despite advances in surgical planning and instrumentation, fixation strategies may be limited by poor bone quality and significant anatomical variations. During a posterior approach, both bone quality and anatomy can make stable fixation difficult to achieve.

Intralaminar screw fixation has recently been extended to the thoracic spine with encouraging results [2–3]. Nevertheless, in cases of severely dystrophic deformities, pedicles may be very thin or even interrupted on one side. In such situations, intralaminar fixation of posterior implants may be unreliable because the connection between the posterior arch and the vertebral body is weak. For the same reason, the use of laminar hooks does not significantly improve stability.

To our knowledge, screw implantation through the costotransverse joint has never been described before, although the surgical approach to the entry point is available during standard posterior exposure of the thoracic spine. This single-case experience illustrates the feasibility of this technique in the presence of complex anatomy and standard instrumentation limitations.

## Case Report

An 18-year-old girl with a long-standing diagnosis of type 1 neurofibromatosis developed rapidly progressive weakness in all four limbs. Within one month, she became unable to walk without a stick. Standard radiographic examination showed a 100° cervical kyphosis with a fulcrum at C5, which was posteriorly dislocated into the spinal canal. A similar severe lordosis was present in the upper thoracic spine, and the two curves compensated each other, resulting in a clinically acceptable head position relative to the trunk.

Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated severe spinal cord compression at the apex of the kyphosis. Flexion–extension radiographs confirmed stiffness of the curves. In addition to the complexity of the deformity, thoracic pedicle hypoplasia was observed, with unilateral connections between the posterior arch and vertebral body at T3 and T5.

The patient was initially treated with a halo-jacket to gradually correct the kyphosis, but after one month the result was extremely poor. A three-stage surgical procedure was therefore planned: anterior release and decompression with C5–C6 corpectomy, posterior correction and fusion from C2 to T7, and anterior reconstruction and fusion using a titanium mesh cage and cervical plate.

The main concerns during surgical planning were achieving adequate decompression without overcorrecting the kyphosis, which was already compensated by thoracic lordosis, and obtaining reliable fixation in the thoracic spine if standard techniques proved ineffective. After detailed analysis of the CT images, it was hypothesized that screw fixation through the four cortices of the costotransverse joint could provide sufficient bone purchase to maintain stability during the time required for fusion. This technique was planned as a backup option.

Neuromonitoring was used throughout surgery. After anterior decompression, the curve became partially correctable, and anterior fusion was completed during the same stage. The cervical plate was contoured to match the kyphotic anatomy. During the posterior approach, the posterior arch of T3 was found to be loose, likely due to iatrogenic pedicle fracture during periosteal stripping. As a result, six screws were placed into the costotransverse joints, with lengths ranging from 18 to 24 mm. Bone purchase was considered adequate in all cases.

Postoperative CT imaging confirmed satisfactory decompression of the spinal canal. The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient required standard analgesia for five days, mainly for cervical wound pain. Chest radiographs showed no pleural effusion or pneumothorax. She was discharged after ten days and transferred to a rehabilitation facility, wearing a soft collar for two weeks.

Three months after rehabilitation, the patient had almost completely recovered neurologic function. Clinical and radiological results remained stable at one-year follow-up, with acceptable sagittal alignment and effective decompression. At final follow-up, the ASIA motor score improved from 64 to 83 out of 100. On the SF-36 scale, the physical component summary score improved from 28.6 to 43.2, and the mental component summary score improved from 29.1 to 54.2.

At 28 months, CT imaging showed solid fusion of the anterior graft through the cage. However, early signs of posterior screw mobilization were noted, likely due to insufficient posterior grafting, which had been placed only in the central area of the instrumentation.

## Surgical Technique

Preoperative planning with CT imaging is mandatory when dealing with severe spinal deformities [6–7]. CT scans, complemented by three-dimensional reconstructions, help identify the entry point and drilling trajectory, as the rib head—which accepts the distal portion of the screw thread—is not visible from a posterior approach.

The transverse processes are fully exposed during the posterior approach, as in standard scoliosis surgery. The entry point is located at the midpoint of the transverse process, equidistant from its superior and inferior borders. To minimize the risk of lung injury, drilling is performed progressively until the last layer of bone is crossed, using a pedicle probe to identify entry into the pleural space. Tapping is performed in the proximal two-thirds of the trajectory, followed by screw insertion.

Anatomic studies have reported screw lengths ranging from approximately 14 to 20 mm for this type of fixation in normal cadaveric spines [8]. In the present case, screw lengths ranged from 14 to 20 mm, with an average effective bone purchase of approximately 16 mm. Due to the medial position of the screw heads in the thoracic spine, rod connection to the cervical instrumentation was facilitated. Final fixation was asymmetrical, as placement of the left T4 screw was not feasible due to proximity to the adjacent screw; fixation was therefore extended one level below on that side.

## Discussion

Instrumentation placement in the thoracic spine is particularly challenging in severe deformities with significant anatomical abnormalities and compromised bone quality. When pedicle screw placement is not possible, surgeons may resort to hooks or laminar wires, although these options often provide inferior stability.

Intralaminar screw fixation has been proposed as an alternative and represents a valuable addition to the spine surgeon’s armamentarium. However, in neurofibromatosis-related dystrophic deformities, the connection between the posterior arch and vertebral body may be severely compromised, rendering fixation through the posterior arch unreliable.

Costotransverse screw placement represents a potential salvage technique in such situations. This method provides four-cortical bone purchase, offering reasonable resistance to pull-out compared with pedicle screws. Biomechanical studies have shown moderate axial resistance in cadaveric models [1]. Nevertheless, the costotransverse joint retains a small degree of physiological motion, and placing a screw across a joint that does not routinely fuse may predispose to mechanical failure. Respiratory motion induces repetitive stress at this interface, potentially increasing the risk of loosening compared with pedicle or laminar fixation.

In the present case, thoracic screws remained in place for 28 months, a period sufficient to achieve solid anterior and posterior fusion. The later appearance of screw mobilization highlights the importance of adequate posterior grafting and long-term follow-up.

In conclusion, costotransverse screw fixation should be considered a salvage technique when standard fixation methods are impracticable due to severe anatomical anomalies or previous laminectomy. Thorough preoperative planning and careful surgical execution are essential before employing this technique.
