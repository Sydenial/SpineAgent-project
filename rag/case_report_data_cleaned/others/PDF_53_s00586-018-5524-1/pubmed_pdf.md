# Cervicothoracic Spine Duplication: A 10-Year Follow-Up of a Neurologically Intact Boy

## Abstract

We report a case of cervicothoracic spine duplication in a neurologically intact male. According to our knowledge, it is the first case in the literature of cervicothoracic spine duplication. The patient, an 11-year-old male, presented with short stature and was diagnosed with spinal deformity. Imaging revealed anterior bony structure duplication and a syrinx cavity at the duplication level. The patient was followed up for 10 years. In the literature, spine duplication is classified as a severe form of split cord malformation (SCM) due to bone duplication and associated spinal cord malformation. This case presents a distinct form of SCM, with a non-duplicated dural tube and cervicothoracic duplication level without neurological deficits. Treatment of SCM typically involves the removal of fibrous/osseous splitting processes. Neurologically intact spine duplication can be followed up without surgical intervention.

**Keywords**: Cervicothoracic spine duplication, Split cord malformation, Syrinx.

## Purpose

Spinal column duplication is a rare abnormality of the vertebral column. The oldest report in the literature dates back to 1980 [1]. Following McKay and Nason's case report, many published papers have described both symptomatic and asymptomatic thoracolumbar and lumbar spine duplications [2–7].

Spine duplication (SD) is classified as a severe type of split cord malformation (SCM) due to the accompaniment of bony duplication with split spinal cord malformation. Most previously reported cases involved two hemicords in separate dural tubes with bone duplication, classified as type 1 split cord malformation (SCM) [2–7].

This report presents the first case of cervicothoracic spine duplication in the English literature. The patient, diagnosed at age 3, has been followed up for 10 years with no neurological deficits. He has a single enlarged dural tube with a syrinx cavity at the duplication level. Based on previous cases without neurological deficits, cervicothoracic duplication can be followed up conservatively.

## Clinical Presentation

After obtaining informed consent from the patient's family and approval from the institutional review board (44-314, January 19, 2016), the patient's hospital chart was reviewed.

We report the case of a 13-year-old male diagnosed with cervicothoracic spine duplication at age 3. He presented with short stature compared to his peers and was referred to our spine center with suspicion of a spinal abnormality.

The patient was delivered full-term via normal vaginal delivery. His parents were healthy, with no genetic disorders or consanguineous marriage. His sister was healthy with no spinal abnormalities. The patient had a height of 127 cm and weighed 37 kg, which was below average for his age. He was at stage 3 according to the Tanner scale. No facial dysmorphism was present, and his intelligence was normal. Physical examination revealed no abnormalities in the musculoskeletal system except for spinal deformity. The coronal plane examination showed no shoulder asymmetry, thoracic hump, or pelvic obliquity. The sagittal plane examination revealed thoracic hypokyphosis (C5–T12 Kyphosis angle: 22º) with nearly normal lumbar lordosis (Lumbar Lordosis Angle: 51º). The processus spinousus was difficult to palpate in the cervicothoracic region but could be easily felt in the lumbar region. Neuromotor evaluation showed intact light touch, pin prick, proprioception, and vibrational sensations. Reflexes were symmetric, without hyperreflexia or clonus. Romberg, Hoffman, and Babinski signs were negative, and rectal sphincter tone was normal. Bilateral upper and lower extremity muscles were grade 5 on manual muscle testing, with no weakness or muscle atrophy. No pigmentation or hypertrichosis was noted. The patient underwent a detailed evaluation of his gastrointestinal and genitourinary systems, both of which were normal. A pulmonary function test revealed mild obstructive pulmonary disease [Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 second (FEV1): 0.62 L/s; Forced Vital Capacity (FVC): 0.85 L/s; FEV1/FVC: 79.8%], without clinical significance. The total SRS 22R score was 4.15 (excluding the management domain) and 4.4 in the pain domain. He is attending standard education and participating in daily sports activities successfully, as with his peers.

The patient underwent EOS, CT, and MRI of the whole spine, including the craniocervical junction. Sagittal spinal variables were measured with digital software (Surgimap Spine, Nemaris Inc., New York, NY, USA). EOS imaging revealed near-normal sagittal alignment, except for hypokyphosis at the duplication level. CT showed marked duplication of the spine between C5 and T9, with incomplete dysmorphic vertebral elements, and fusion abnormalities. A 2 cm bone defect was present at the duplication level, with the anterior epidural area in close proximity to the posterior mediastinum. The spinal cord was a single structure, with an enlarged dural tube and syrinx cavity. There was a cystic formation filled with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in the bone defect, which communicated with the intramedullary syrinx cavity. The spinal cord ended at T12, and no abnormalities were noted in the craniocervical junction.

## Discussion

Split spinal cord malformations (SCM) are congenital anomalies where the spinal cord is longitudinally divided by a rigid or fibrous septum. Pang classified SCMs based on the number of dural tubes and the type of splitting within the spinal cord. Type 1 SCM consists of two hemicords, each with its own dural tube, separated by a rigid median septum. Type 2 SCM consists of two hemicords within a single dural tube, separated by a non-rigid, fibrous median septum [8].

According to Pang's theory, SCMs form in the third week of gestation due to the failure of the primitive neurenteric canal to close. This failure results in a communication between the amnion and yolk sac, leading to the formation of the split canal. The split may involve just the cord and dura or extend to the vertebral bodies [8].

Spine duplication with significant separation of bony elements is a rare malformation. Limited cases have been reported in the literature, with most cases classified as type 1 SCM, but some authors consider these cases unclassified due to the extensive bone duplication. This case represents the longest follow-up of a cervicothoracic spine duplication.

## Conclusion

This case represents the first documented instance of cervicothoracic spine duplication with a 10-year follow-up. Unlike other cases, this patient has an enlarged dural tube and a single spinal cord without any split anomaly. The treatment of SD typically involves removal of fibrous/osseous splitting processes. As in previous cases with no neurological deficits, cervicothoracic duplication without cord abnormalities can be followed up conservatively.
