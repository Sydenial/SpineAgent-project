# Flexible Dropped Head Deformity Following Laminectomy for Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy: A Case Series and Review of Literature

## Abstract

Background context: Flexible dropped head deformity (FDHD) following laminectomy for cervical spondylotic myelopathy is a debilitating entity. Patients need to support their head manually to look forward in standing or sitting position. FDHD is different from rigid dropped head deformity in patients with post-laminectomy kyphosis or ankylosing spondylitis and has only once been described after surgery to the cervical spine.

Purpose: To report a rare, but severe complication of cervical posterior decompressive surgery, to describe its possible etiology, and to review the literature on the management of FDHD in order to provide recommendations for its treatment.

Study design: A retrospective clinical case series.

Patient sample: This sample comprises four patients with FDHD.

Methods: We retrospectively reviewed the charts with clinical follow-up data of all patients that underwent posteriorly directed spinal interventions and concomitantly developed FDHD and were admitted between January 1998 and July 2015.

Results: Cervical decompressive laminectomy is regularly performed in our institution. Four patients with FDHD were identified (3 males and 1 female). The overall prevalence was less than 1% (= 4 of 460 total estimated CSM surgeries). FDHD developed within weeks or months after surgery. One patient was treated conservatively, whereas the other three received surgery to reconstruct stability of the cervical spine. Two of the surgically corrected patients eventually required revision surgery as a result of failure of the hardware and because of skin erosion.

Conclusions: FDHD is a rare, but serious complication of posterior decompressive surgery. Its etiology appears multifactorial. Its management needs to be directed towards early surgical intervention.

Key words: cervical spine; kyphosis; dropped head syndrome (DHS); laminectomy; laminoplasty.

Abbreviations: dropped head syndrome (DHS), flexible dropped head deformity (FDHD), cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM).

## Introduction

Dropped head syndrome (DHS) is a debilitating condition that is characterized by severe weakness of the extensor muscles of the cervical spine. This weakness causes the inability to keep the neck in a neutral position and often results in a characteristic chin-on-chest deformity in standing or sitting position. DHS has been associated with various conditions, but is usually caused by severe systemic neuromuscular disorders, such as motor neuron disease, myasthenia gravis and inflammatory myopathy [1]. Progressive forward tilt of the neck and kyphosis are also well-known to occur as complication of posteriorly directed cervical spinal interventions, such as laminectomy and laminoplasty [2]. However, these patients have a fixed head position as a result of structural changes in the cervical spine.

Flexible dropped head deformity (FDHD) is totally different. The neck deformity is correctable with passive neck extension and is not necessarily accompanied by structural changes, although weakness of the neck extensor muscles may eventually contribute to cervical segmental instability and accelerate degenerative transformation. Patients with FDHD often need the use of both hands to support their head and keep horizontal gaze in the upright position. It therefore results in serious physical and social invalidity. In contrast to rigid cervical kyphosis, FDHD has only once been described after a standard laminectomy for cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) [3]. In this case-series, we present four patients with FDHD following (multilevel) laminectomy for CSM with special emphasis on its etiology and treatment.

## Patients and Methods

After institutional review committee approval, the senior author's database was used to identify all patients with FDHD, who previously underwent posterior decompressive surgery for CSM in our primary (Radboud University Medical Centre) and secondary (Canisius Wilhelmina Hospital) neurosurgical center. We retrospectively reviewed the charts with all clinical follow-up of the patients admitted between January 1998 and July 2015. Radiographs and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were routinely performed preoperatively. Postoperative radiographs were made in all patients and follow-up outpatient visits with radiological evaluation of cervical alignment were routinely arranged after 3, 12 and 24 months. All patients with FDHD received neurological work-up with laboratory investigations and electromyography (EMG) to exclude the presence of neuromuscular disease or other extrinsic causes for deformity.

## Results

Four patients with FDHD following conventional non-instrumented (multilevel) laminectomy for CSM were identified at our institution. All had the same characteristics, which we will summarize, after which we will discuss one case in more detail. In all instances, the symptoms and signs developed gradually, but soon after the laminectomy (weeks–months). All patients were unable to hold their head upright without manual assistance, and they had to actively support their head bimanually. This contributed to social isolation, since activities such as dining with friends could not be performed properly.

After a longer period of conservative management, consisting of a hard collar and physiotherapy in order to strengthen muscles, all patients were offered fusion to correct the deformity and support stability. One patient refused surgical correction. He was treated conservatively, consisting of a treatment with both hard and soft neck collars, and multidisciplinary pain management.

A solely posterior surgical intervention was performed in only one case. In the other two patients, a 360-degrees reconstruction was performed. In the patient with the posterior approach, the construct failed, which was managed by a 360-degrees approach with anterior cervical discectomies and fusion with cages followed by re-exploration of the posterior construct and replacement of the broken implant. Another surgically treated patient experienced disabling extensor muscle atrophy with ulceration of the skin. Therefore, he underwent surgery with transposition of a musculocutaneous latissimus dorsi flap, reduction of cervical spinous processes and partial extirpation of posterior pedicle-screws. At final follow-up (24–72 months postoperatively) the surgically treated patients were satisfied, because they did not experience the consequences of a dropped head, and were able to have normal daily activities, despite some residual symptoms of CSM.

### Case Presentation

#### Case 1

A 45-year-old female patient presented with symptoms and signs of CSM. An uneventful C3–C6 non-instrumented laminectomy was performed with removal of the upper part of C7, leaving the muscle attachments to the spinous process intact. After several months, outpatient follow-up revealed that she had increasing difficulties keeping her head in upright position. She needed the use of both hands to extend the neck and leaned back in her chair to compensate for her cervicothoracic kyphosis. Examination revealed a characteristic flexible dropped head, but she was able to actively extend her neck for a short period of time (< 1 minute).

MRI showed C2–C3 and C7 myelomalacia with atrophy of the spinal cord, but no evidence of progression of myelopathy or structural kyphotic changes. At EMG, evidence of neuromuscular disease could not be detected. The patient preferred conservative treatment, consisting of a hard (Philadelphia) neck collar and multidisciplinary pain-treatment including cervical peripheral nerve blocks.

During the following months, muscle strength, fine motor skills and coordination further decreased. Eventually, she could only walk for 50 meters and needed the use of a cane for long walks. The dropped head and loss of horizontal gaze made her feel socially isolated, as she was not capable of dining and having conversations with friends and family. She finally agreed with a 360-degrees fusion. An anterior discectomy with fusion of C2–C7 and posterior fusion of C2–Th3 was performed and the stability restored. Postoperatively, her pain decreased and strength in both arms and legs improved. After one-year follow-up, she is still satisfied and can participate in social activities.

## Discussion

The prevalence of FDHD after cervical laminectomy is unknown. In a recent evaluation of complications after cervical laminectomy none of the patients suffered from FDHD [4]. In a review of DHS, cervical laminectomy was not mentioned as a cause of a flexible dropped head [1,5]. Therefore, we think this case series is unique. In the Netherlands, most patients are referred to the surgeon who operated them initially, and we were therefore really confident that the number of patients with FDHD was representative for our practice.

Earlier, we calculated that in a three year period (2009–2012) 80 patients underwent a laminectomy for CSM in our institution [6]. Since the rate of dorsal procedures for CSM did not change, nor did the technique, we estimated that between 1998 and 2015 approximately 460 cervical laminectomies were done solely for CSM. The overall prevalence for this entity in the total cohort in this time period was 0.87%. Therefore, this complication seems rare, and, though not unknown to the spine surgeon, has only once been described in a patient who underwent a cervical laminectomy for CSM [3].

The exact pathophysiology remains unclear. A flexible dropped head might be a presentation of various neuromuscular diagnoses. In the report by Petheram et al., a relation with CSM or more specifically post-laminectomy was not made [7]. In our patient that was treated conservatively, a search for a neuromuscular diagnosis did not reveal anything specific, except nonspecific, non-inflammatory, myopathic changes at muscle biopsy.

There have been multiple reports that relate FDHD to CSM and describe patients that fully recover after posterior decompressive surgery [8–10]. It has been suggested that cervical spondylosis may have caused preferential denervation and weakness of the posterior cervical muscles by compromising the microcirculation of the anterior horn cells of the spinal cord. When FDHD develops, patients actively try to extend their cervical spine in order to preserve horizontal gaze and compensate for their kyphotic deformity. This might result in narrowing of the anterior-posterior diameter of the spinal canal, which negatively influences both myelopathy and head drop [8].

In our cases, not the myelopathy, but the laminectomy itself seemed to be the primary cause of FDHD. For instance, there were no signs of FDHD before the initial procedure and radiological post-procedural follow-up showed significant reduction of spinal cord narrowing without evidence of progression of myelopathy. However, in two of our four cases, evidence of post-laminectomy draping of the spinal cord across the posterior aspect of the vertebral bodies of the cervical spine can be seen. This “draping” of the spinal cord may have compromised the microvascular supply of the cord as a result of flattening of small feeder vessels [11]. Moreover, cervical flexion is known to increase longitudinal cord tension due to the tethering effect of the dentate ligaments and cervical roots. The combination of ischemia and cord tension might have caused direct neuronal injury with concomitant muscle weakness, and eventually cervical deformity and development and progression of FDHD in these patients.

It could be hypothesized that as a result of the laminectomy, the posterior tension band was severely damaged, thereby inducing kyphosis. The muscle attachments to C2 were left intact in all patients, because C2 was not involved, though two of the four patients initially underwent a complete C7 laminectomy, thereby disrupting extensor muscle support. As a result of the combination of weakened strength due to surgery, but also due to neuronal injury as described above, the gravitational force of the head will move forward. The lever arm will elongate and therefore more strength was needed to keep the head upright and in balance. In case of FDHD, the balance between lever arm, center of gravitational center and muscle strength is lost. This hypothesis can be the base for formulating a treatment algorithm.

The necessity to offer a solution was, in our opinion, obvious. FDHD not only caused severe physical, but also social invalidity. Difficulties existed when performing daily activities and self-care tasks, such as getting dressed and having dinner and conversations with family and friends. Finding a solution for FDHD certainly can improve the quality of life. The first option could be training the muscles to increase strength and restore the balance. Support by an external brace and pain management might be helpful. In the series by Petheram et al. of DHS not related to previous surgery, three out of six cases noticed mild improvement, whereas the others did not improve. The final case underwent surgery with a dissatisfying result [7]. If the conservative strategy fails, surgery could be an option.

In our opinion, especially due to the longer lever arm, a 360-degrees fusion should be performed in order to diminish movement within the anterior column by removing softer discs and replacing them by stiffer materials (cages). This will create less stress on a posterior construct with a long lever arm, since the construct will include the cervicothoracic junction [1]. In our patient treated by a posterior approach alone, the construct failed. This could be explained by the mechanism of persisting laxity of the anterior column. Finally, all of our surgically treated patients were satisfied because they could participate in all kinds of activities.

## Conclusion

Patients with FDHD are different from those with similar deformities because of the flexibility, whereas progressive post-laminectomy kyphosis or kyphosis related to ankylosing spondylitis is rigid. The etiology of FDHD still remains obscure, but seems to involve a multifactorial process consisting of structural myelopathic changes to the cervical spine and a vicious circle of biomechanical factors, muscle damage, fatigue and pain. If left untreated, FDHD can cause severe physical and social invalidity. After a conservative treatment, management could be directed towards early surgical intervention, consisting of a 360-degrees fusion with extension through the transitional cervicothoracic zone in order to acquire durable correction of sagittal cervical alignment.
