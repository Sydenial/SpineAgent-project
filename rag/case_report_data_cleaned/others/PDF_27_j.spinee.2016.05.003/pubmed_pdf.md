# Granulomatosis Polyangiitis (Wegener's Granulomatosis) Causing Atlantoaxial Instability: A Case Report

## Abstract

Granulomatosis with polyangiitis is a vasculitis that most commonly affects the upper airway, lower airway, and kidneys. Cervical spine involvement has not been previously reported. We describe a case of a 45-year-old woman who developed atlantoaxial instability as a result of granulomatosis with polyangiitis. The patient initially presented with chronic sinusitis, pharyngitis, and a large ulcerative oropharyngeal lesion. Over time, she developed progressive neck pain and radiographic evidence of atlantoaxial subluxation. Surgical stabilization with atlantoaxial fusion resulted in resolution of her neck and shoulder pain. She was treated medically with cyclophosphamide for control of the underlying disease. Granulomatosis with polyangiitis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of non-traumatic cervical spine instability.

## Introduction

Cervical spine degeneration and instability are common manifestations in many rheumatologic disorders, such as rheumatoid arthritis. In rheumatoid arthritis, the most common pathology involving the cervical spine is C1–C2 instability, which often requires surgical treatment. Patients with rheumatoid arthritis are especially prone to injuries at this level because there is no intervertebral disc between C1 and C2 [1].

Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA), formerly known as Wegener's granulomatosis, is a systemic vasculitis that classically presents with involvement of the kidneys, upper airway, and lower airway [2]. Cervical spine involvement has not been previously reported. We describe a case of GPA threatening the cervical spine and resulting in atlantoaxial instability requiring surgical fusion.

## Case Presentation

A 45-year-old Caucasian woman with a two-year history of chronic sinusitis and pharyngitis presented with severe odynophagia. Over several months, she developed headaches, bilateral otalgia, and nasal pain. She was initially treated with multiple courses of antibiotics for presumed bacterial pharyngitis. At an outside academic medical center, a large ulcerative lesion was identified in the oropharynx, along with smaller ulcers in the nasopharynx and a nasal septal perforation. Multiple biopsies demonstrated acute and chronic inflammation, necrosis, and bacterial colonization, without evidence of lymphoma, epithelial malignancy, vasculitis, or granuloma. Serologic testing, including C-ANCA, was unremarkable. Endoscopic gastrointestinal evaluation was normal. Wound cultures grew normal oral flora and Candida tropicalis, and she was treated with antibiotics and antifungals without improvement. The working diagnosis was a multifactorial ulcer, and she was treated with methotrexate, prednisone, and azathioprine with transient improvement before symptom recurrence.

After failure of these treatments, the patient was referred for further evaluation. She reported severe throat pain, sinus congestion, nonproductive cough, and right ear pain. Repeat laboratory evaluation was again nondiagnostic. Outside pathology slides were re-reviewed and showed no evidence of lymphoma or malignancy.

Around the time of admission, she developed new-onset, poorly localized neck and facial pain. Cervical spine radiographs showed mild degenerative disc disease at C4–C5 and C5–C6. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated edema within the anterior arch of C1 and the body of C2, enhancement of the atlantoaxial joint, and ulceration of the posterior pharyngeal wall extending to the prevertebral fascia.

On physical examination, the patient was awake, alert, and oriented, with mild distress due to pain. Flexible endoscopy showed necrosis extending to the prevertebral fascia in the right nasopharynx. Neck range of motion and upper extremity strength were normal, and neurologic examination was nonfocal.

The differential diagnosis included infection, lymphoma, autoimmune disease, and vasculitis. Repeated bacterial and fungal cultures were negative. Pseudogout was considered unlikely based on imaging findings. A focused biopsy performed at the treating institution ruled out lymphoma on histopathologic and immunohistochemical examination. Given the chronic sinusitis, lack of response to prior therapies, and exclusion of alternative diagnoses, granulomatosis with polyangiitis was suspected. The patient was treated with intravenous methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide, resulting in improvement of her neck and posterior pharyngeal pain.

Four weeks later, her neck pain recurred, now radiating into the upper trapezial region and shoulders. She had no signs of myelopathy or radiculopathy. Repeat imaging showed worsening erosion of the odontoid process and anterior ring of C1, with progressive atlantoaxial subluxation. Flexion–extension radiographs demonstrated increased atlantoaxial interval. Neurologic examination remained normal. Due to progressive atlantoaxial instability, a posterior C1–C2 instrumented fusion was performed, resulting in resolution of her neck, upper trapezial, and shoulder pain.

Over the subsequent eight months, her throat pain and odynophagia worsened, and monthly intravenous cyclophosphamide was resumed. She continued cyclophosphamide for one year and has been followed for two years since initiation of therapy. Her throat pain improved markedly, oral intake resumed, and weight stabilized. Follow-up imaging showed a stable lesion consistent with a healed ulceration. Since her atlantoaxial fusion, she has remained neurologically stable without recurrence of neck, trapezial, or shoulder pain.

## Discussion

This report describes the first known case of granulomatosis with polyangiitis presenting with cervical spine involvement and atlantoaxial instability. The patient initially developed a pharyngeal ulcer causing odynophagia and throat pain. As the disease progressed, the lesion eroded into the cervical spine, resulting in progressive neck pain, radiation to the shoulders, and radiographic evidence of atlantoaxial subluxation. Surgical stabilization with atlantoaxial fusion successfully addressed the mechanical instability, while cyclophosphamide controlled the underlying inflammatory disease process.

Granulomatosis with polyangiitis most commonly affects the upper and lower respiratory tracts and the kidneys [2]. Prior case reports have described thoracic and lumbar spine involvement, including prevertebral soft tissue masses that resolved with immunosuppressive therapy. These findings have been attributed to granulomatous tissue invading adjacent structures, with resolution following appropriate medical treatment. Cervical spine involvement, however, has not previously been documented.

This case highlights the importance of including granulomatosis with polyangiitis in the differential diagnosis of non-traumatic cervical spine instability. Features suggestive of GPA include chronic sinusitis, destructive upper airway lesions, pulmonary nodules, and ulcerative lesions of the nasopharynx. Diagnosis is typically confirmed by histopathologic examination demonstrating granulomatous inflammation. Long-term management requires close rheumatologic follow-up and treatment with glucocorticoids and cyclophosphamide, which has been shown to result in significant improvement or remission in the majority of affected patients [4].
