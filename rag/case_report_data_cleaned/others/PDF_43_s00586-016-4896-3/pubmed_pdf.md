# Primary Intramedullary Hydatid Cyst: A Case Report and Literature Review

## Abstract
Intramedullary hydatid cyst is extremely rare. We present a case of pathologically confirmed primary intramedullary hydatid cyst in an otherwise healthy patient. A 17-year-old boy presented with lumbar pain, weakness, and numbness in both lower limbs, and urinary difficulty for 2 years. The patient had no other signs of systemic hydatid cyst disease. An intramedullary cystic lesion was identified with magnetic resonance imaging and was shown to be a hydatid cyst by histopathologic examination after surgical removal. Although extremely rare, primary intramedullary hydatid cyst pathology might be the cause of lumbar pain, weakness, and numbness in both lower limbs for those living in endemic areas. Surgical removal is feasible and effective for intramedullary hydatid cyst.

## Introduction
Hydatid disease is caused by the parasitic tapeworm *Echinococcus*, including the larval stage of *Echinococcus granulosus*, and less commonly *Echinococcus multilocularis* [1]. *Echinococcus* can infect many hosts, including sheep, cattle, goats, and humans. Humans may be contaminated through direct contact with the definitive host or by ingesting food infected with parasite eggs [2]. It is endemic in North Africa, Spain, Greece, Turkey, Portugal, the Middle East, Australia, New Zealand, South America, Baltic areas, and the Philippines [3].

The most common sites of infection are the liver (75%), lung (15%), brain (2–4%), and genitourinary tract (2–3%) [1]. Spinal involvement is rare, with an incidence of about 1% of all cases of hydatid disease [4]. Spinal hydatid cyst disease presents with cauda equina symptoms or symptoms of spinal cord compression [5]. It is classified into five types: (1) primary intramedullary hydatid cyst; (2) intradural extramedullary hydatid cyst; (3) extradural intraspinal hydatid cyst; (4) hydatid disease of the vertebrae; and (5) paravertebral hydatid disease [6].

Intramedullary involvement has been reported in only two cases [7,8]. To the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first reported case of a primary intramedullary hydatid cyst in the thoracic region.

## Case Report

### Presentation and Examination
A 17-year-old boy was admitted to our hospital in February 2016. He presented with lumbar pain, weakness, and numbness in both lower limbs, and urinary difficulty for 2 years. Ten days before admission, he experienced increasing difficulty with walking, and his feet lost the sensation of cold and heat. Neurological examination revealed bilateral lower extremity weakness, spastic paraparesis, and hypoesthesia below the T10 level. In addition, he had a marked chest tightness and abdominal tension.

He grew up in a rural area and had frequent contact with farm animals, such as horses, cows, and sheep.

### Neuroimaging
Magnetic resonance imaging showed a cystic lesion with thin walls and cerebrospinal fluid–like signal intensity. The lesion was hypointense on T1-weighted images and hyperintense on T2-weighted images. The T7–T9 vertebral bodies and paraspinal muscles appeared intact. Cranial, cervical, lumbosacral, lung, and abdominal computed tomography showed no evidence of systemic hydatid cyst disease.

### Operation and Histopathological Examination
Based on MRI findings, an enterogenous cyst was initially diagnosed. Due to the patient’s rapid neurological deterioration, emergency surgery was performed via a posterior approach. T7–T9 laminectomy was carried out. After opening the dura, the spinal cord appeared intact. Median myelotomy revealed a whitish, pearl-like, semitranslucent cystic lesion. The cyst was excised completely, and dexamethasone irrigation was used to prevent an anaphylactic reaction. The dura was sutured watertight, and the wound was closed in layers.

Histopathological examination showed a cyst wall stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Granulomas and inflammatory granulation tissue composed of hyperplastic fibers and vessels, epithelioid cells, and multinucleated giant cells were observed. Hydatid cyst structures and *Echinococcus* were identified. Chronic inflammatory cells in the interstitium supported the presence of a marked inflammatory reaction.

### Postoperative Course
The patient developed paraplegia 2 days after surgery but gradually improved. At the 9-month follow-up, he was able to ambulate independently. Radiological evaluation showed no evidence of recurrence.

## Discussion
If the parasite is not lodged in the liver or lungs, it may localize almost anywhere in the body, including the peritoneum, spleen, kidney, heart, brain, spine, bony skeleton, and muscles [9]. To date, only two cases of intramedullary hydatid cyst have been reported.

Ley A Jr described a 28-year-old man with urinary incontinence, severe leg pain, and paraparesis. He had a prior history of pulmonary hydatid disease. An intramedullary cystic mass was eventually identified and removed, but the patient remained paraplegic after 8 months of follow-up [7].

Senol MG reported a 55-year-old woman with an intramedullary hydatid cyst in the cervical spine and multiple cysts in the liver, spleen, lungs, and thoracic vertebrae. Serological tests confirmed hydatid disease. Surgery for the intramedullary cyst was avoided due to high risk, and her symptoms improved after long-term albendazole treatment during 6 years of follow-up [8].

In our case, misdiagnosis occurred preoperatively because of the rarity of this condition. MRI is highly valuable for preoperative diagnosis and early detection of recurrence. Histopathologically, hydatid cysts consist of three layers: an outer adventitial fibrous layer with eosinophils, an intermediate laminated cuticular layer, and an inner germinal layer containing nucleated epithelium [10]. Cysts can be unilocular or multilocular, with unilocular cysts being more common [11].

Differential diagnoses of intramedullary cystic lesions include syringomyelia, myelomalacia, hematoma, arachnoid cyst, neurenteric cyst, epidermoid cyst, cystic hemangioblastoma, and cystic astrocytoma [12]. Early and accurate diagnosis is essential. Well-defined, smooth, thin-walled, homogeneous cysts with cerebrospinal fluid–like signal intensity should raise suspicion of hydatid disease, particularly in patients from endemic regions.

With advances in surgical techniques, complete removal of intramedullary cysts without rupture is feasible. Cyst rupture may lead to recurrence and severe hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylactic shock [9,13]. The reported recurrence rate ranges from 30% to 40% [5]. The cyst becomes fertile within 6–9 months, which is a critical period for recurrence assessment [14].

## Conclusion
Although extremely rare, primary intramedullary hydatid cyst should be considered as a potential cause of lumbar pain, weakness, and numbness in both lower limbs in patients from endemic areas. Surgical removal is feasible and effective for treating intramedullary hydatid cysts.
