# Spinal Rosai-Dorfman Disease: Case Report and Literature Review

## Abstract

**Objective:** Sinus histiocytosis with massive lymphadenopathy or Rosai-Dorfman disease (RDD) is a rare benign disease of dubious etiology that arises predominantly in lymph nodes with generalized fever and malaise. Isolated intraspinal involvement has its unique characteristics. The purpose of this study is to present the largest series of cases in the spinal Rosai-Dorfman disease literature to increase familiarity with its clinicopathologic features, diagnosis, and treatment of RDD from the spine.  
**Methods:** We present the case of a 34-year-old man who presented with paraplegia secondary to an isolated thoracic vertebral lesion. On physical exam, the patient displayed progressive weakness and tendon hyperreflexia of the lower limbs. After a total resection, symptoms were obviously relieved, and the patient remained asymptomatic with no signs of recurrence after a 5-month follow-up. We also retrospectively analyzed 60 cases of spinal RDD published in English since 1969. Clinical data, histopathology, and radiological features were analyzed.  
**Results:** Spinal RDD should no longer be considered rare and may occupy an increasingly prominent place in the list of differential diagnoses for intraspinal lesions. Only elaborate histopathology was diagnostic for RDD. Most of the patients were surgically treated, and marked improvements were observed in their clinical conditions.  
**Conclusions:** RDD with spinal involvement is uncommon and challenging in making a certain diagnosis. Histopathologic characteristics and immunohistochemical findings are considered key points for the diagnosis of this disease. The optimal treatment remains controversial, and more efforts should be focused on investigating the etiology and adjuvant therapy for relapsing cases or subresected lesions.

**Keywords:** Rosai-Dorfman disease, Sinus histiocytosis with massive lymphadenopathy, Spinal involvement.

## Introduction

Sinus histiocytosis with massive lymphadenopathy (SHML), also known as Rosai-Dorfman disease (RDD), was first reported by Rosai and Dorfman in 1969 [1]. It is a rare idiopathic lymphoproliferative disorder of uncertain etiology that is generally characterized by bilateral, painless, massive, peripheral cervical lymphadenopathy. Patients with RDD may also have other constitutional symptoms, such as fever, neutrophilia, increased serum erythrocyte sedimentation rate, leukocytosis, lymphopenia, polyclonal hyperglobulinemia, and anemia. Extranodal involvement can be found in 43% of cases sustained with RDD, and the primary extranodal sites include skin, orbit, upper respiratory tract, and bones [2]; however, central nervous system (CNS) involvement was reported in less than 5% of extranodal RDD cases [3], and spinal RDD was only described in 20–25% of CNS RDD patients [4, 5]. We report a case of spinal RDD and review the relevant identified manuscripts. In conclusion, spinal RDD is unfamiliar to clinical doctors, so a systematic review is necessary to summarize its clinical features.

## Case Report

A 34-year-old man presented with 4 weeks of girdle-like pain in the chest and 2 weeks of progressive bilateral lower extremity paresthesia. He denied fever and weight loss. On physical exam, he had hypesthesia below T10 and weakness of the lower limbs (left 3/5 and right 4/5) with normal muscle tone. No lymph nodes were palpated in the cervical, supraclavicular, or axillary regions.

Upon hospitalization, he developed bladder and bowel dysfunction with a deteriorating neurologic examination. Muscle strength of the left lower limbs was grade 2/5 with active knee and ankle reflex, and the right lower limbs was grade 3/5 with active knee and ankle reflex. Laboratory data were normal except for a mildly increased erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 17 mm/h (ref: 0–15 mm/h).

As his neurological symptoms progressed, myelopathy was suspected. Thoracic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a dorsal epidural mass with clear borders extending from the T9–T10 levels, which caused spinal cord compression. The lesion was iso-intense to the spinal cord on T1-weighted (T1WI) sequences and heterogeneously hypointense on T2-weighted (T2WI) sequences with significant enhancement after gadolinium application.

To decompress the spinal canal and obtain tissue for pathological diagnosis, laminectomy from T8 to T10 was performed. A firm, well-encapsulated mass, measuring 2.0 cm x 1.5 cm x 1.0 cm, was found in the epidural space. Complete resection was achieved. The frozen section showed diffuse infiltration of lymphocytes, consistent with a lymphoid and hematological system tumor. Post-operatively, both bilateral lower extremity paresthesia and bladder and bowel dysfunction were significantly relieved.

Histopathologic examination revealed histiocytosis with an infiltrate of lymphocytes and plasma cells. These histiocytes demonstrated emperipolesis. Immunohistochemistry confirmed positivity for CD68 and S-100. These findings were consistent with a diagnosis of Rosai-Dorfman disease. Throughout a 5-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no signs of recurrence.

## Discussion

### Rosai-Dorfman Disease

RDD was first described in 1965 by Destombes in the French literature as a lipid storage disorder potentially developing after inflammation (adénites avec surcharge lipidique). It was later recognized by Rosai and Dorfman in 1969 as a unique histiolymphoproliferative disease of the lymph nodes. Initially, it was associated with bilateral, painless cervical lymphadenopathy, hence the name "sinus histiocytosis with massive lymphadenopathy" (SHML). However, cases have shown that RDD can involve multiple organ systems and may be fatal [7]. Extranodal involvement was recognized in 43% of cases. Common extranodal sites include skin, upper respiratory tract, orbits, testes, and bones [2].

### Rosai-Dorfman Disease in the Spine

Less than 5% of RDD cases involve the central nervous system (CNS), with spinal involvement noted in 11.4% of these cases [8]. Spinal RDD can cause spinal compression, leading to neurological symptoms, yet limited reports on spinal RDD exist. Hence, spinal surgeons must be aware of this disease as a potential cause of spinal cord compression.

### Etiology

The exact etiology of RDD remains unclear. Infectious agents such as human herpes virus-6 (HHV-6) and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) have been suggested, although there is no conclusive evidence linking them to the disease [48, 56]. Abnormal immune responses and occasional familial clusters further complicate the understanding of RDD's pathogenesis.

### Symptom

RDD typically presents with bilateral, painless cervical lymphadenopathy, though patients with spinal involvement may present with neurological symptoms, such as paraparesis or paraplegia. Our case featured girdle-like pain in the chest and progressive bilateral lower extremity paresthesia, indicative of thoracic vertebral spinal cord compression.

### Treatment and Prognosis

RDD is typically a benign disease. Spontaneous resolution is seen in approximately 90% of cases without CNS involvement, while surgical resection is the preferred treatment for spinal RDD cases. Complete resection offers the best outcomes, although in cases where resection is incomplete, adjuvant therapies such as corticosteroids or radiotherapy may be considered. The prognosis is generally favorable, but cases with multiple organ systems involved may have poorer outcomes.

## Conclusion

Spinal RDD is a rare and often misdiagnosed cause of spinal compression. Its clinical presentation can mimic other dural-based masses, such as meningiomas. Histopathological examination, including the presence of emperipolesis and positivity for S-100, is critical for diagnosis. Surgical resection remains the best treatment, though the disease's etiology and optimal treatment for relapsing cases require further research.
