# Delayed Penetration of the Thoracic Aorta by Pedicle Screws

## Case Report

### Abstract

**Study Design.** A case report.  

**Objective.** Presentation of a patient diagnosed with perforation of the aorta by pedicle screws at levels T6 and T9 two years after spinal fusion, who was advised no intervention. Review of other reported cases that did not undergo excision of the penetrating screws.  

**Summary of Background Data.** More than 30 cases of aortic penetration by pedicle screws have been described. Gradual penetration of the screws into the aorta rarely causes symptoms other than backache. However, only two cases were treated conservatively.  

**Methods.** A 65-year-old female patient underwent spinal decompression and T5–T10 posterior fusion following a spinal abscess. Two years and eight months postoperatively, she underwent a computed tomography scan for suspicion of spinal hardware infection, which showed T6 and T10 screws penetrating the thoracic aorta.  

**Results.** Due to high morbidity, the patient was not offered an operation for screw excision.  

**Conclusion.** This case report adds to the only two previous reports of patients who did not undergo revision of pedicle screws penetrating the thoracic aorta.  

**Key Words.** Aorta perforation, pedicle screw, posterior stabilization, thoracic spine.  
**Level of Evidence.** 5  

## Introduction

Despite a high appreciable fraction of mispositioned pedicle screws (15%–20%) [1,2] and the proximity of the aorta to the thoracic spine, the reported rate of vascular complications following spinal surgery is very low. Acute or subacute bleeding due to hardware penetration of major vessels appears to be an extremely rare complication [3]. However, when this occurs, immediate intervention and repair is clearly life-saving.  

Chronic penetration of the aorta, however, presents differently, mostly as a backache [4–6]. Patients remain stable and screw removal is often performed electively. No intervention, however, may be considered by some patients. More than 30 studies have reported on one or more patients who underwent revision of a pedicle screw penetrating the aorta [5–14]. Of these, only two patients were treated nonoperatively [4]. We report a case of nonoperative follow-up of a patient with two pedicle screws penetrating the thoracic aorta.

## Case Presentation

A 65-year-old woman with hypertension and morbid obesity underwent decompression of vertebral osteomyelitis complicated by an abscess at spinal levels T7–T8, with posterior spinal fusion at levels T5–T10. Screws were placed in the pedicles of T5–T6 and T9–T10. She suffered from residual paraplegia, remained confined to a wheelchair, and required a permanent urethral catheter. She later underwent a left above-knee amputation for an infected left knee arthroplasty.  

About three years after spinal decompression surgery, the patient was hospitalized for a right above-knee amputation. Despite a normal postoperative course and absence of any signs of wound infection, levels of C-reactive protein remained elevated. As part of the workup, a chest X-ray was examined and showed lateral displacement of the tips of the left T6 and T9 pedicle screws. A computed tomography scan was performed to rule out hardware infection or failure, but neither was present. There was, however, new kyphosis at T7, and the left T6 and T9 pedicle screws were seen penetrating the thoracic aorta.  

The vascular surgery unit in our medical center advised against operative intervention. The patient remained well and asymptomatic during a three-year follow-up period.

## Discussion

The currently reported incidence of vascular injuries in spinal surgery, about 0.01%, is likely underreported [2]. The degree of contact or encroachment of the aorta by a screw is present in approximately 0.29% of cases [2]. Local pressure imposed by a screw tip on the pulsating vessel wall causes ulceration and gradual penetration of the screw into the lumen. In the majority of reviewed cases, diagnosis of aortic injury was made six months to six years postoperatively [6,16]. Local inflammation and scar formation processes probably prevented blood leakage and dissection. The chronicity of this process may explain the universally benign presentation of this complication.  

The two screws demonstrated in our case represent two consecutive stages of the process: one screw entered the aorta but only partially penetrated the vessel wall, while the second was completely intraluminal. Similar to other reported cases of screw penetration of the aorta [5–14], our patient was recommended conservative follow-up. Soultanis et al. [4] presented a similar scenario involving two patients diagnosed with this complication. One was an 82-year-old female with progressive kyphosis five years after T7–L4 fusion who declined both endovascular screw removal and correction of kyphosis. She continued follow-up for two years without any adverse outcomes reported. Another was a 26-year-old patient with T10–L4 fusion for idiopathic scoliosis who presented five years postoperatively and declined revision for this “silent” complication; however, no long-term follow-up information was available.  

The presence of an intraluminal screw may be unsettling for both the surgeon and the patient. Nevertheless, careful consideration of risks versus benefits of revision is recommended, especially in elderly patients. Endovascular screw removal and stenting may result in long-term graft migration, whereas open thoracotomy with cross-clamping may lead to paraplegia and pulmonary failure [4,16]. Other reported complications include seroma formation, intraoperative bleeding, and transient multiple organ dysfunction. Conservative treatment may require chronic anticoagulation or antiaggregation therapy, although no specific guidance is available because most intravascular foreign bodies are treated surgically [19].  

In our situation, multiple comorbidities precluded surgery, and the patient had already received double antiaggregation therapy. Influenced by previously reported cases [4] and an understanding of the chronic nature of this complication, we concluded that the intraoperative risk would be unacceptably high.

## Key Points

- This report presents a patient whose thoracic aorta was penetrated by two pedicle screws.  
- As a postoperative complication, pedicle screw penetration into the aorta may be underreported.  
- Nonintervention may be a viable treatment modality, depending on patient preference and perioperative risk.
