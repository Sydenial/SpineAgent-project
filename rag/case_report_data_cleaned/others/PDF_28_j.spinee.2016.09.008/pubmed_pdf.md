# An Unusual Cause of Cervical Kyphosis

## Abstract

**Background Context:** Acute fixed cervical kyphosis is a rare presentation and may be associated with conversion disorder, psychogenic dystonia, or as a side effect of typical antipsychotic drugs. Haloperidol (Haldol) has been associated with acute dystonic reactions, and in some cases rigid deformities may ensue. We report a case of fixed cervical kyphosis following the use of Haldol.

**Purpose:** To present a case of a potential acute dystonic reaction temporally associated with Haldol ingestion leading to fixed cervical kyphosis.

**Study Design:** Case report.

**Methods:** A patient diagnosed with bipolar disorder presented to the emergency department multiple times with severe neck pain and stiffness. The neck appeared fixed in flexion, with extensive osteophyte formation developing over a three-month period.

**Results:** The patient’s condition was resolved using a posterior–anterior–posterior surgical approach, correcting the cervical curvature from 88° to 5°.

**Conclusion:** Acute dystonic reactions have the potential to apply sufficient pressure on bone to cause rapid osteophyte formation, resulting in fixed cervical kyphosis.

**Keywords:** dystonic reaction; haloperidol; fixed cervical kyphosis; osteophyte formation; severe neck pain; chin-to-chest deformity.

## Introduction

Acute dystonic spasms are a well-known side effect of neuroleptic drugs such as haloperidol. Haloperidol is a typical high-potency antipsychotic and has been reported to induce dystonia in 16% to 94% of patients [1,2]. Usually, the effects of acute dystonia can be reversed by cessation of haloperidol, administration of intravenous anticholinergic agents, or dopamine agonists.

In this case report, we describe a unique presentation in which a painful dystonic reaction was associated with the development of a fixed cervical kyphosis and rapid formation of anterior osteophytes. Surgical intervention was required to correct the deformity.

## Case Report

A 31-year-old female with a diagnosis of bipolar disorder and a documented history of intravenous substance abuse presented to the emergency department with a stiff and painful neck. She had recently been started on haloperidol during an inpatient psychiatric admission. Within 72 hours of initiation of antipsychotic therapy, she experienced progressive neck stiffness and abnormal forward flexion of the neck.

Haloperidol was immediately discontinued, and she was started on benztropine (Cogentin), an anticholinergic agent. Despite this, her symptoms persisted, prompting transfer to the emergency department 28 days after initiation of haloperidol. She denied headache, numbness, or weakness. There were no signs of radiculopathy or spinal cord involvement, although she reported bilateral radiation of neck pain to the upper extremities. Her neck was fixed in flexion and was not passively correctable. No cervical spine radiographs were obtained at that time. She was treated symptomatically with methadone and clonazepam for pain control. Neurology consultation suggested acute dystonia as a complication of haloperidol treatment.

Three months later, the patient was referred to our outpatient clinic for persistent neck pain. Imaging of the cervical spine revealed marked kyphosis with osteophyte formation along the anterior margins of the vertebral bodies. The deformity was not passively correctable. Two attempts at manual correction in the clinic were unsuccessful and caused severe pain. Halo traction was discussed but declined by the patient due to intolerance during prior correction attempts.

Surgical intervention was therefore deemed necessary. A posterior–anterior–posterior fusion from C3 to C7 was performed. The posterior approach was undertaken first to allow facet osteotomies at three levels, facilitating correction during the anterior stage. Despite general anesthesia, the spine remained surprisingly stiff. Following completion of the facet osteotomies, partial correction was achieved, allowing progression to the anterior approach. Multiple anterior discectomies were performed, resulting in significant correction of the deformity.

Postoperatively, the patient demonstrated complete reversal of the fixed neck flexion. Preoperatively, the Cobb angle measured from the superior endplate of C3 to the inferior endplate of C7 was 88°. Following surgery, cervical alignment improved to a lordotic angle of 5°. Neck Disability Index (NDI) scores showed statistically significant improvement postoperatively. At 14-month follow-up, the patient declined repeat radiographs but was clinically evaluated and found to have stable sagittal balance.

## Discussion

Haloperidol use in younger patients has long been associated with acute dystonic reactions, including tonic contractions of the neck musculature. Boyer et al. reported that 15 of 16 patients between the ages of 19 and 32 developed dystonia when treated with haloperidol alone [2]. Haloperidol acts as a dopamine D2 receptor antagonist, particularly affecting the nigrostriatal pathway. Blockade of these receptors leads to increased cholinergic output from the striatum, resulting in the tonic muscle contractions observed in dystonia [6].

Pharmacokinetic aspects of haloperidol metabolism may further explain the occurrence of acute dystonia. One of its metabolites, haloperidol pyridinium, is structurally similar to 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium (MPP+), a neurotoxic metabolite associated with parkinsonian syndromes. In animal models, this compound produces effects similar to MPP+, suggesting a potential mechanism for the neurological side effects of haloperidol [8].

Osteophyte formation following fixed cervical kyphosis has not previously been reported as a consequence of acute dystonic contractions. Osteophyte formation is more commonly associated with chronic degenerative disc disease. Based on Wolff’s law, increased mechanical stress on bone can result in adaptive remodeling. In kyphotic regions of the cervical spine, abnormal load distribution over even short periods may increase stress on vertebral bodies, activating osteocytes and promoting osteophyte formation [10,11].

Other differential diagnoses considered included dropped head syndrome, myopathic head drop, cervical dystonia, and psychogenic dystonia [12–14]. Dropped head syndrome and myopathic etiologies were excluded due to the presence of acute neck pain and fixed cervical kyphosis. Cervical dystonia and psychogenic dystonia could not be entirely excluded; however, the rapid development of osteophytes suggests sustained mechanical stress rather than inconsistent or distractible symptoms. Psychogenic dystonia is often associated with symptom variability and selective functional impairment, which were not observed in this patient.

In cases such as this, where rigid deformity and severe pain are present, conservative measures including physical therapy may be ineffective or contraindicated. Surgical management proved to be the most effective means of restoring upright head posture and functional cervical alignment in our patient.

## Conclusion

This case demonstrates that acute dystonic reactions associated with haloperidol can, in rare instances, lead to fixed cervical kyphosis with rapid osteophyte formation. When conservative management fails and deformity becomes rigid, surgical correction may be necessary and can result in significant clinical and functional improvement.
