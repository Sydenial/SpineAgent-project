# Serous degeneration of bone marrow mimics spinal tumor

## Abstract

**Objective**  
To present a rare case of serous degeneration of bone marrow which resembles primary spinal tumor or bony metastasis to the spine.

**Summary of background data**  
Serous degeneration of bone marrow or gelatinous marrow transformation is a rare disease characterized by focal marrow hypoplasia, fat atrophy, and accumulation of extracellular mucopolysaccharides abundant in hyaluronic acid. Few literature reports have been reviewed and only a small number of clinical cases have been presented.

**Methods**  
Two cases of serous marrow transformation were reported.

**Results**  
In the first case, a 29-year-old man suffered from severe left buttock pain. Bone metastasis was suspected on radiological examinations. Percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy was performed along with bone biopsy. In the second case, a 49-year-old man presented with lower back pain radiating to both lower legs. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a water-like signal lesion in the sacrum. Serous marrow transformation was confirmed pathologically in both cases.

**Conclusion**  
To the best of our knowledge, cases of serous degeneration of bone marrow resembling malignancy have rarely been reported in the literature. In this report, two cases demonstrate that serous transformation of bone marrow can mimic spinal tumors.

**Keywords**  
Serous degeneration · Serous atrophy · Gelatinous marrow transformation · Magnetic resonance imaging

## Introduction

Serous degeneration, also known as gelatinous marrow transformation (GMT) [1], is a rare disease characterized by fat cell atrophy, focal marrow hypoplasia, and accumulation of extracellular gelatinous material identified as hyaluronic acid mucopolysaccharides [2,3]. Serous degeneration of bone marrow is not specific to a single disease entity, but rather represents a clinical consequence of generalized severe illnesses. Although the causes of this transformation remain unclear, serous degeneration has been reported in several chronic diseases, including prolonged starvation, anorexia nervosa [4], malabsorption, alcoholism, malignancy, and infectious diseases such as tuberculosis and acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) [5]. Clinically, only a few reports describe serous degeneration occurring in the spine.

## Case Report

### Case 1

A 29-year-old male with a history of gout was referred to the orthopedic department for evaluation of severe left buttock pain radiating to the posterior thigh for one month. He denied fever, weight loss, trauma, or prior infection. Physical examination revealed low back pain at the lumbar spine level, intermittent soreness of the left hip and lateral thigh, and numbness of the left heel. Laboratory examinations showed no significant abnormalities, including a negative test for human immunodeficiency virus.

Radiographs demonstrated narrowing of multiple intervertebral disc spaces. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a herniated disc at the L5–S1 level and multiple space-occupying lesions at the L1, L2, L3, and L4 vertebral bodies with water-like signal intensity, characterized by low signal on T1-weighted images and high signal on T2-weighted and short tau inversion recovery (STIR) images. A tentative diagnosis of herniated intervertebral disc with suspected multiple myeloma or multiple hemangiomas was made.

The patient underwent percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy at L5–S1, and a biopsy of the L5 bone lesion was performed. Microscopic examination showed serous degeneration of bone marrow with marked reduction of hematopoietic and fat cells, and hypocellularity relative to the patient’s age. A diagnosis of serous degeneration of bone marrow was confirmed. The patient was followed regularly for two years after surgery.

### Case 2

A 49-year-old male, a heavy smoker and drinker with a medical history of hypertension and hepatitis B, presented with lower back pain radiating to both lower legs for two months. Physical examination revealed local tenderness over the lower back, and the straight leg raising test was positive. He was treated conservatively with analgesics and rest for two months without symptomatic improvement.

MRI revealed an amorphous lesion with heterogeneous high signal on T2-weighted images and low signal on T1-weighted images located in the upper sacrum, accompanied by cortical destruction at the posterior border of the S1 vertebral body. Bone scintigraphy demonstrated increased uptake at the S1/S2 level, raising suspicion for malignant bone metastasis, primary bone tumor, or osteomyelitis.

A bone biopsy was subsequently performed. Histological examination revealed fragmented trabecular bone without evidence of malignancy on hematoxylin and eosin staining. Focal hemorrhage with fibrinous degeneration was also observed. These degenerative changes in the loose marrow confirmed the absence of malignancy. A diagnosis of serous degeneration of bone marrow was made. After discharge, the patient remained symptom-free for one year.

## Discussion

This report describes rare cases of serous degeneration of bone marrow mimicking spinal tumors. In both cases, secondary malignant neoplasm of the bone marrow was initially suspected based on radiological findings. However, pathological examination revealed no malignancy, instead demonstrating characteristic features of serous degeneration.

Serous degeneration of bone marrow represents a pattern of injury that develops under various pathological conditions [3]. It is most often associated with cachexia secondary to chronic diseases such as tuberculosis, malignancy [6,7], leukemia following chemotherapy, acute febrile illnesses, systemic lupus erythematosus, chronic renal failure, severe hypothyroidism, and chronic nutritional deficiencies including starvation and anorexia nervosa [4]. Notably, none of these conditions were present in the reported cases, suggesting an idiopathic etiology.

Although previous studies have reported a slight peak in GMT occurrence among individuals aged 20 to 29 years [3], serous degeneration is rarely observed in the spine, particularly in young patients. The 29-year-old patient in this report represents one of the youngest documented cases of spinal serous degeneration, highlighting the diagnostic challenge in the absence of pathological confirmation.

Serous degeneration typically begins in the peripheral skeleton and may eventually involve the axial skeleton [8]. On MRI, these pathological changes appear as fat-poor marrow with water-like signal intensity, characterized by low signal on T1-weighted images and high signal on T2-weighted and STIR sequences [9–11]. The absence of contrast enhancement after gadolinium administration may help differentiate serous degeneration from malignant or infectious processes.

Pathologically, the hallmark of serous degeneration is stromal edema with microvesicular changes and hypocellularity due to loss of normal hematopoietic elements. Immunohistochemical analysis in these cases showed negative staining for cytokeratin (AE1/AE3) and no significant increase in CD138 expression, further excluding malignancy.

Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values have been shown to correlate inversely with cellularity in certain studies, suggesting a potential role in distinguishing serous degeneration from highly cellular malignant tumors. However, current evidence remains insufficient to support routine use of this technique for definitive diagnosis.

## Conclusion

These two cases demonstrate that serous transformation of bone marrow in the lumbar spine can closely mimic malignant or infectious spinal diseases. A comprehensive understanding of this rare entity, combined with careful interpretation of MRI findings and pathological confirmation, is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary aggressive treatment.
