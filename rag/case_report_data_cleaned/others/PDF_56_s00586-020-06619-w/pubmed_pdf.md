# Bezold–Jarisch reflex causing bradycardia and hypotension in a case of severe dystrophic cervical kyphotic deformity: a case report and review of literature

## Abstract

**Purpose**  
A 17-year-old adolescent with neurofibromatosis and severe cervicothoracic deformity was identified to have thoracic inlet compression leading to bradycardia and hypotension only during prone positioning, and we discuss its successful management.

**Methods**  
Preoperative halo-gravity traction reduced the deformity from 126° to 91°. During prone positioning, sudden onset bradycardia and hypotension with asystole occurred, and surgery was called off after two additional failed attempts of prone positioning.

**Results**  
A retrospective analysis of CT and MRI showed severe narrowing of the thoracic inlet. In this patient, the right thoracic inlet was severely narrow, and prone positioning caused a further dynamic compromise stimulating the right vagal nerve. The right vagus supplies the sinoatrial node, which is the natural pacemaker of the heart, and its stimulation causes sympathetic inhibition. Bezold–Jarisch reflex is a cardio-inhibitory reflex occurring due to vagal stimulation resulting in sudden bradycardia, asystole, and hypotension. To facilitate prone positioning, the medial end of the clavicles, along with limited manubrium excision, was performed relieving the vagal compression. C2–T4 instrumented decompression followed by anterior reconstruction and cervical plating was performed. The postoperative period was uneventful, and the final deformity was 45°.

**Conclusion**  
Bezold–Jarisch reflex as a result of a narrow thoracic inlet caused by cervical kyphosis and compensatory hyperlordosis is a rare and potentially life-threatening complication.

## Case Report

A 17-year-old adolescent boy with neurofibromatosis and a gradually progressive cervical kyphotic deformity for ten years presented with sudden progression of deformity associated with recent-onset clumsiness of hands, loss of dexterity, gait instability, and difficulty in performing day-to-day activities over the past six months. On examination, he had multiple café-au-lait spots over the trunk with a severe rigid cervical kyphotic deformity and compensatory thoracic hyperlordosis. Neurologically, weakness of left C6 and C7 roots (MRC grade 4−/5) and exaggerated deep tendon reflexes were noted.

Plain radiographs revealed a severe dystrophic cervical kyphotic deformity of 126°. CT showed C5–C6 and C6–C7 facet joint subluxation with buckling collapse of the cervical spine, with the apex at the C5–C6 level. MRI demonstrated stretching of the cord with a thinned-out caliber over the acutely deformed cervical spine. No intraspinal mass lesions were detected.

The surgical plan was a single-stage 540-degree approach correction comprising anterior release (corpectomy), posterior column shortening, and instrumentation followed by anterior reconstruction. Preoperative ambulant halo-gravity traction reduced the deformity to 91°, achieving a 35° correction.

After C5, C6, and C7 corpectomy, during prone positioning, there was a sudden onset of bradycardia and hypotension followed by asystole, which disappeared immediately on turning the patient back to the supine position. With no obvious explanation, surgery was called off after two additional failed attempts of prone positioning.

A retrospective analysis of CT and MRI revealed severe narrowing of the thoracic inlet, leading to the diagnosis of dynamic thoracic inlet compromise identified 48 hours following the initial surgery.

## Discussion

Neurofibromatosis is associated with severe, acute, and rigid dystrophic deformities of the spine and can have serious anesthetic complications [1]. The thoracic inlet is an opening at the apex of the thoracic cavity bounded by the T1 vertebra posteriorly, the manubrium anteriorly, and the first rib laterally. The clavicle forms the anterior border of the thoracic inlet. Vital structures passing through it include the trachea, esophagus, thoracic ducts, apices of the lungs, nerves (phrenic, vagus, recurrent laryngeal nerve, and sympathetic trunks), and vessels (carotid and subclavian arteries, jugular and brachiocephalic veins) [2].

Narrow thoracic inlets can cause a variety of clinical issues depending on the structure compromised. In this case, the right vagal nerve was compressed as it passes on the right side of the trachea and lies behind the sternoclavicular joint. Prone positioning further compromised this already narrowed space, dynamically stimulating the vagus nerve.

Bezold–Jarisch reflex is a cardio-inhibitory reflex occurring due to vagal stimulation and results in sudden bradycardia, asystole, and hypotension [5]. The right vagus nerve supplies the sinoatrial node, and its stimulation causes sympathetic inhibition with parasympathetic overactivity. Experimental studies have demonstrated that stimulation of the right vagus nerve can induce bradycardia, slowing of atrioventricular conduction, and reduction in atrial contractility, with maximal effects occurring at the level of the thoracic inlet [7].

Dynamic compromise of the thoracic inlet during prone positioning has been described in other deformity cases, with varying clinical manifestations depending on the compressed structure, including arterial compression and profound hypotension [8–11]. In some cases, alternative positioning strategies have been used; however, these were not viable options in the present case [12].

After a multidisciplinary team discussion involving anesthetists, otolaryngologists, spine surgeons, and cardiothoracic surgeons, it was decided to attempt thoracic inlet decompression if prone positioning again proved impossible. As anticipated, the same phenomenon recurred on reattempting prone positioning. Resection of the medial ends of the clavicles with limited manubrium excision relieved the vagal compression and allowed safe prone positioning.

Subsequently, C2–T4 instrumented decompression followed by anterior reconstruction and cervical plating was performed. The postoperative period was uneventful, and the final Cobb angle was corrected to 45°. The patient recovered completely from his preexisting neurological deficits and completed 29 months of follow-up without additional complications.

This case highlights the importance of preoperative assessment of the thoracic inlet in patients with severe cervicothoracic and chest wall deformities, as dynamic compromise during prone positioning can lead to life-threatening cardiovascular events.

## Conclusion

Bezold–Jarisch reflex resulting from a narrow thoracic inlet caused by rigid cervical kyphosis and compensatory hyperlordosis of the upper thoracic spine is an extremely rare but serious complication. Preoperative recognition of thoracic inlet narrowing may help surgeons and anesthetists anticipate and manage this condition. Thoracic inlet decompression is an effective method to address this unique and potentially fatal complication.
