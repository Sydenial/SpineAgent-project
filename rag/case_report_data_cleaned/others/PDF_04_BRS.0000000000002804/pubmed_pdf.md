# Klippel-Feil syndrome with auxiliary anterior cervical meningomyelocele and thoracic syringomyelia: A case report

## Abstract

**Study Design**: Case report.

Klippel-Feil syndrome (KFS) with auxiliary anterior cervical meningomyelocele and syringomyelia is a rare entity. To the best of our knowledge, only four cases have been reported. This makes this case unique.

**Summary of Background Data**: Klippel-Feil syndrome with auxiliary anterior cervical meningomyelocele is an exceedingly rare condition. A 22-year-old male patient presented with a two-year history of left-hand paresthesia and progressive weakness. Diagnostic evaluation showed KFS with auxiliary anterior cervical meningomyelocele and thoracic syringomyelia.

**Methods**: A 22-year-old male patient was presented to the neurology outpatient department with two years of history of left-hand paresthesia and progressive weakness. The diagnostic evaluation showed Klippel-Feil syndrome with auxiliary anterior cervical meningomyelocele and thoracic syringomyelia.

**Results**: The patient was sent to the neurosurgery department for intervention. After discussing the possible risks and complications of the intervention, he opted for conservative therapy and declined surgery.

**Conclusions**: The paucity of data is the key reason for the lack of recommended protocols for the management of such patients, but available literature recommends neurosurgical intervention in symptomatic patients.

**Key Words**: paresthesia, muscle weakness, Klippel-Feil syndrome, meningomyelocele, syringomyelia, MRI Spine, CTA neck, congenital anomalies, spinal cord, anterior horn cell disease.

**Level of Evidence**: 5

## Introduction

Klippel-Feil syndrome (KFS) is a rare congenital anomaly characterized by the fusion of one or more cervical vertebrae. It is defined by a triad of short neck, restricted neck movements, and low hairline, which is seen in only around 50% of patients with KFS. Many extravertebral manifestations, including cardiopulmonary, urogenital, and gastrointestinal system abnormalities, have been reported in association with KFS. Associated neurological anomalies include hydrocephalus, syringomyelia, hearing loss, thin corpus callosum, atlanto-occipital fusion, split cervical spinal cord, meningomyelocele, and pyramidal tract decussation failure. KFS with auxiliary anterior cervical meningomyelocele is extremely rare, and only four cases have been reported. We present the fifth case that presented with progressive left-hand paresthesia and wasting.

## Case Report

A 22-year-old right-handed man presented to the outpatient clinic with a two-year history of progressive clumsiness, weakness, and numbness in the left hand. There was no history of neck pain or radiculopathy, and the right hand was asymptomatic. There was no history of urinary urgency or increased frequency. The patient's past medical history was unremarkable. On examination, significant wasting of the intrinsic muscles of the left hand was noted. The weakness of the C8 to T1 myotomes (finger flexion and abduction) was more pronounced (MRC grade 1/5) compared to the C6 and C7 myotomes (wrist extension, elbow extension, wrist flexion, and finger extension) whose strength was MRC grade 3/5. Triceps and brachioradialis reflexes were absent. Pain and temperature sensation were reduced in the C6-T1 dermatomal distribution in the affected hand. Although asymptomatic, there was mild functional weakness and depressed reflexes in the right hand. The deep tendon reflexes were exaggerated in the lower limbs. There was no restriction of neck flexion and extension, and the neck hairline was normal. No obvious spine deformity was noted, and the neurological and general physical examinations were normal. Plain X-ray of the cervical spine showed fused vertebral bodies from C6 to T1. MRI of the entire spinal cord demonstrated a large bony defect in the anterior parts of C5 to T1, through which meninges and the cervicodorsal cord were protruding, forming a sac measuring 3.2 x 5 x 6.1 cm. The sac extended into the posterior mediastinum, predominantly on the left side. The dural sac was widened at this level, with two hemi-cords and a dilated central canal. Moreover, there was a syrinx extending from T9 to T12. CT scans and 3D CT reconstruction of the cervical spine revealed the fusion of the last three contiguous cervical and the first thoracic vertebrae along with an anterior defect at the same level. Additionally, mild focal scoliosis with concavity toward the left side was observed. CT angiogram of the neck was normal. His nerve conduction studies and EMG were consistent with focal anterior horn cell disorder affecting C6-T1 on the left side. Further investigations, including lumbosacral spine X-ray, echocardiography, and abdominal, pelvic, and scrotal ultrasound, were normal. Based on these imaging features, we diagnosed Klippel-Feil syndrome with anterior cervical meningomyelocele. Neurosurgical consultation was sought, but the patient declined surgery, opting for a conservative approach and referral to physiotherapy services.

## Discussion

Klippel-Feil syndrome (KFS) is a perplexing congenital condition characterized by the fusion of one or more cervical vertebrae. Various associated spinal and extraspinal manifestations have been reported, including neurological, skeletal, cardiopulmonary, urogenital, and gastrointestinal anomalies. Meningomyelocele (MMC) is the most common malformation in the spectrum of neural-tube defects. The MMC can occur at any level of the spine but most frequently affects the lower lumbar and/or sacral spine. Anterior cervical MMC associated with KFS is an extremely rare condition. Upon reviewing the English literature, we found only four reported cases of anterior cervical MMC with KFS. All four patients were female. The clinical histories, examination findings, and imaging features of these cases are summarized in Table 1. Among these four cases, only one patient underwent surgical intervention, and only one had associated syringomyelia.

The classic triad of KFS includes neck webbing, low hairline, and limited neck mobility, which is found in about 50% of patients. The usual presenting complaint is reduced cervical spine mobility, but facial asymmetry and hearing loss are also frequent presenting features. Various multisystemic abnormalities are associated with KFS, including neurological findings like developmental anomalies, nonspecific headaches, neck pain, hydrocephalus, myelopathy, radiculopathy, synkinesia (due to pyramidal tract decussation failure), and split spinal cord. Musculoskeletal anomalies, such as scoliosis and Sprengel deformity (high scapula), are also common. Other associated abnormalities include renal agenesis, absent vagina, uterus and ovaries, undescended testes, ventricular septal defects, aortic arch aneurysms, and aberrant vertebral arteries. Diagnostic workup includes plain X-ray of the cervical spine and chest, MRI of the cervical spine and brain, and CTA of the neck. Echocardiography, abdominal and pelvic ultrasound, and imaging of the thoracic and lumbar spine are also recommended to detect any possible associated anomalies. Treatment of KFS depends on the severity of symptoms, and symptomatic patients often require neurosurgical intervention.

In conclusion, patients with KFS, with or without associated anterior MMC, may present to neurological clinics, and careful diagnosis and management are crucial.
