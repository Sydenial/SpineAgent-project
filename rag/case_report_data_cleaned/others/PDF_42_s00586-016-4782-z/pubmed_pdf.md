# Resection of Giant Invasive Sacral Schwannoma Using Image-Based Customized Osteotomy Tools

## Abstract

**Background:** Giant invasive sacral schwannomas are rare tumors. Surgical excision is the standard treatment and total resection is performed if feasible. Advances in three-dimensional (3D) imaging technology have facilitated treatment designs of complex surgical procedures.  
**Objective:** Our aim was to evaluate virtual surgical planning, computer-aided design (CAD), and manufacturing with 3D printing technology of the customized osteotomy guiding device in giant invasive sacral schwannoma resection.  
**Methods:** A digital 3D model of the sacrum, including the giant invasive sacral schwannoma, was rendered from patient computer tomography (CT) images. The surgeon chose excision margins of the tumor. Based on the virtual surgical planning, the customized guiding tool for osteotomy was designed and manufactured using CAD and 3D printing.  
**Results:** We used the guiding block to successfully excise a giant sacral schwannoma using only a posterior approach to achieve gross total resection. No augmented spinal instrumentation was used to prevent iatrogenic spinal instability. Clinical symptoms resolved dramatically after the operation. No spinal instability occurred during follow-up.  
**Conclusion:** With the assistance of an image-based customized osteotomy guiding device, we achieved both goals of tumor resection and bone preservation in giant sacral schwannoma resection. With thorough surgical planning, this technology can be applied to complex surgical procedures easily and reliably.

**Keywords:** Giant sacral schwannoma, Surgical excision, Computer-aided design, Guiding tool, CT imaging

## Introduction

Primary sacral tumors are rare, and sacral schwannomas are even rarer. Only a few dozen cases are reported in the literature, most of which are case reports or series [1, 2]. The schwannoma is an encapsulated, slow-growing benign neoplasm, arising from a myelinated nerve sheath, the Schwann cell [1]. Although these nerve sheath tumors rarely arise within bone, they sometimes invade adjacent vertebral bodies and the extraspinal space. Sridhar et al. defined giant invasive spinal schwannomas as masses that invade more than two vertebral levels, invade vertebral bodies, and by extending posteriorly, reach the myofascial regions [3].  

Clinically, giant invasive sacral schwannomas are challenging due to their invasive nature, complex anatomy, and difficulty in achieving safe margins. Currently, there is no established consensus regarding the best treatment strategies. The ideal treatment is complete removal of the tumor without neurovascular complications or spinal instability secondary to bone removal. This would relieve neurovascular compression and alleviate symptoms [2, 4].  

Based on advances in three-dimensional (3D) imaging, virtual surgical planning (VSP), and computer-aided design (CAD) with 3D printing technology for the production of customized guiding devices, surgeons and bioengineers can collaborate to resolve the most complex surgical cases [5–7]. This study describes an image-based customized surgical guiding tool that supports surgeons working with giant invasive sacral schwannoma.

## Materials and Methods

The workflow for the design of the guiding tool is shown in Figure 1. The DICOM CT data set was retrieved and decoded into general images for further image processing. The Marching Cube method was applied to reconstruct the sacrum model. The surgeon then marked the schwannoma region using our self-developed software. The osteotomy guide tool includes the holder, standing part, and resection C-ring for the schwannoma. The standing region was drawn on the dorsal surface of the median sacral crest of the 3D model. The standing part was grown from the marked region. A finger holder connects the standing part and the resection C-ring to form the osteotomy guiding device for further 3D printing.

## Clinical Presentation

A 23-year-old female patient presented with pain in the sacrococcygeal region and progressive voiding disturbance for months before the consultation. Imaging studies revealed a giant invasive sacral tumor extending from S1 to S3. The tumor not only changed the shape and composition of the bony structure but also posed a difficult anatomical location for surgical resection. The 3D-reformatted CT scan is shown in Figure 2. She received a histological diagnosis via a computerized tomography-guided percutaneous needle biopsy. The pathology report confirmed the diagnosis of schwannoma. After thorough discussion, surgical resection was chosen as treatment.

## Design Principles of the Osteotomy Guiding Device

A 0.625 mm CT scan of the sacrum was obtained preoperatively. Based on the CT data, including the tumor region, we reconstructed the 3D image model using our self-developed software for VSP. Image processing and the Marching Cube method were used for 3D image reconstruction [8]. The surgeon then determined the planned margins of resection in the software, which displayed the 3D model of the sacrum (Figure 2). Since a posterior surgical approach was decided, we created the base of the osteotomy guiding tools to stably fit on the dorsal surface of the median sacral crest. We then connected the base with a C-shaped structure that marked and disclosed the resection area (Figure 3). After thorough assessment and planning, the sacral model and customized guiding block were fabricated by a 3D printing machine using acrylonitrile-butadiene-styrene (ABS) plastic material.

## Resection Margin Planning

Planning the resection margin of the tumor is a critical step in the design workflow for the guiding tools. The physicians worked hand-in-hand with engineering colleagues to exchange ideas and computer-generated graphics. The engineer designed the connection pieces along the marked lines of the resection area defined by the surgeon. The specific needs of the patient and particular anatomical hazards were closely monitored. Comprehensive 3D surgical planning could be obtained through a user-friendly interface combining the CT images with the sacral model in a 3D virtual environment. The digital data for the virtual surgical guide were rendered into an actual physical template using a stereolithography modeling technique.

## Results

A posterior 'Y' incision was performed with the patient in the prone position (Figure 4). This approach provided good exposure of the sacrum, the dorsal sections of the iliac wings, and the surrounding soft tissues, while allowing exposure of the lumbar spine if necessary. As preoperative planning in 3D-reformatted CT and the phantom bone model, this bone-model complex served as a dimension and shape reference to guide subsequent resection tasks (Figure 5a). The osteotomy margin of the tumor was localized using the customized guiding block. To achieve accurate intraoperative localization, the standing platform of the customized guiding block was designed to rest closely on the median sacral crest on the contralateral side (Figure 5b). Surface bony landmarks are critical in stabilizing the guiding device. The tumor was excised using a posterior approach, while the anterior aspect was removed piece by piece, ensuring that the nerve and dura were preserved. The S1-S3 nerve roots were intact after resecting the tumor (Figure 5c). No augmented spinal instrumentation was used to prevent iatrogenic spinal instability. Clinical symptoms resolved dramatically after the operation. No spinal instability occurred during follow-up for 2 years. In this pilot project, we achieved both goals of tumor resection and bone preservation in giant sacral schwannoma resection.

## Discussion

The use of VSP in CAD software has gained popularity in clinical practice. The advent of 3D printing technology has further enhanced the quality and predictability of surgical procedures [9–11]. A variety of measurements, analysis, editing, and Boolean operations now provide surgeons with a clear picture of human anatomy and a vivid preview of postsurgical results. Based on the 3D models generated from CT scans, precise simulation of the resection area of a giant sacral schwannoma can be scheduled preoperatively. With the supplement of modern technology, surgeons can perform surgeries with more confidence and accuracy.  

Traditionally, surgeons preoperatively planned the resection margin according to 2D projective images or 2.5D stacked images and chose resection areas by direct vision intraoperatively. However, they often needed to confirm the safe resection margin depending on intraoperative frozen sections [12]. It is difficult to make these decisions intraoperatively. With this novel customized osteotomy guiding block, combined with intraoperative frozen sections, we achieved more accurate intraoperative localization of the resection margin of the tumor.

3D printing technology is widely used as an aid in the medical field, offering clear anatomical details with high-quality simulations of surgical procedures through the fabrication and adaptation of biomaterials [13]. Combining the interdisciplinary knowledge of surgeons and engineers, a custom-designed surgical guidance device, an osteotomy guiding block for sacral schwannoma, was constructed with 3D printing technology. This technology has advantages over traditional surgical techniques, offering improved accuracy compared with the surgeon's manual selection of margins at the surgical site.

In our case, individual templates were customized based on 3D reconstructions of the bone structures extracted from CT image data using self-developed software. A 3D printing machine was used to produce the shape of reference areas on the bone surface. The base of the surgical guide was "grown" on marked, non-destructible sacral surfaces. The holder was then added to the base of the surgical guide. To achieve accurate and stable intraoperative localization of the resection margin of the tumor, the platform of the customized guiding block was designed to rest closely on the median sacral crest. The holder crosses over the spinous process, allowing the surgeon to hold it in place just above the vertebra. Our design required flexibility for varying environments (both small and stable) and complied with the above-described principles. The customized template can be sterilized and used intraoperatively. This technique reduced the time required for the operation and increased the accuracy of the resection area.

Although this report shows the advantages of the guiding template, there are some limitations. Primarily, this preliminary study is only a case report with a relatively short follow-up period. Second, the potential benefits of the surgery-guided technique in improving surgical accuracy could not be proven by a control study without using the guiding template on the same patient. Nevertheless, further applications are expected to rely on the cooperation of surgeons and engineers.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, this preliminary study demonstrates the use of VSP and 3D printing technology in the fabrication of an image-based customized surgical guiding device. This novel technique, stereolithographic model-guided osteotomy, will be the mainstay of the authors’ approach to complex spinal tumor surgery. We believe it has the potential to be a powerful tool for the most complex surgical cases.
