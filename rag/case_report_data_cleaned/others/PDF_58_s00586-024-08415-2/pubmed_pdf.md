# Growth rate of a giant Tarlov (perineural) cyst with intrapelvic extension

## Abstract

**Background and importance:** Giant Tarlov cysts (GTCs) are perineural cysts and their presacral intrapelvic extension are extremely rare entities. We present a case of GTC with intrapelvic extension who has preoperative Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) follow-ups of 12 years, and we demonstrate the annual growth rate and the time-size correlation of a GTC. 

**Methods:** Case report.

**Clinical presentation:** A 37-year-old woman was admitted with left gluteal pain radiating to left foot, left leg numbness, progressed over 12 years. On MRI, starting from the L5–S1 level, a giant Tarlov cyst with an atypical configuration is observed. The patient had a known acral Tarlov cyst, first discovered on MRI obtained 12 years before the surgery. She had 6 consecutive MRI follow-ups in 12 years preoperatively. The cyst's diameters have been measured, and the growth rate was estimated. The annual growth rates were 7.671% for RGR_SD and 6.237% for RGR_SC.

**Conclusion:** When the time-size correlation is observed, it becomes evident that the GTC's growing speed increases over the years because of minimal resistance in the intrapelvic cavity. Surgery may be considered to prevent rapid growth in the intrapelvic cavity and to reduce possible complications of the giant cyst.

**Keywords:** Giant Tarlov cyst, Growth rate, Perineural cyst, Pelvic cyst, Presacral mass, Sacral meningocele.

## Background and importance

Tarlov cysts (TCs) were first described by Tarlov. Spinal Meningeal cysts (MCs) are classified into three categories: Type I MCs do not have spinal nerve root fibers, while Type II MCs have them. Type III MCs are intradural. TCs were named Type II meningeal cysts by Nabors and colleagues in 1988. The TC can expand due to cerebrospinal fluid pressure, which can cause stretching or compression of the sacral nerve roots. This can ultimately lead to progressive painful radiculopathy, sacral nerve root dysfunction, or localized pain. Bladder and sexual dysfunction, as well as symptoms related to the perineal area or the gastrointestinal system, may also be observed.

The etiology remains unclear and may be congenital or acquired, secondary to trauma, infection, inflammation, or iatrogenic causes.

Giant Tarlov cysts (GTCs) are exceptionally rare, although TCs are seen at a rate of 4.6%, and only 1% of these cases present with symptoms in adults. In GTCs, common symptoms include back pain and radicular findings. They grow and extend into the pelvis. There is a fistula path associated with the cyst's content and the subarachnoid space, which continues with the perineural structures that surround the nerve roots. Intrapelvic cystic extensions may exceed 10 cm in diameter, and these patients may experience symptoms related to abdominal complaints such as abdominal pain and ureteral compression. When the cyst exceeds the boundaries of the sacral bone, its growth may accelerate.

We did not find any data about the growth rate of a GTC in the English literature. Here, we present a case of GTC with intrapelvic extension who has preoperative Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) follow-ups of twelve years, and we demonstrate the annual growth rate and the time-size correlation of a GTC.

## Clinical presentation

A 37-year-old woman was admitted with left gluteal pain radiating to left foot, left leg numbness, progressed over 12 years. Her left leg numbness and severe pain is constant and aggravated when doing up shoelaces. On neurological exam, her left S1 innervated muscles were slightly weak, left Achilles reflex was absent contrary to the hyperreflexia on right Achilles. Her straight leg raising test was negative bilaterally. On MRI, starting from the L5-S1 level, a giant Tarlov cyst with an atypical configuration, filling and expanding the sacrum, especially in the left half, extending to the presacral area on the left with an extraspinal component, thin-walled in multiloculated character, isointense with CSF in all sequences, is observed. The patient had a known sacral TC, first discovered on an MRI obtained 12 years before. She had 6 MRI follow-ups in 12 years. MRIs were from 12, 9, 6, 3 years, and 6 and 2 months before surgery. It is clear that the cyst has grown in years and reached an intrapelvic giant mass.

## Treatment plan and case follow-up

During the operation, only the posterior approach was performed in the prone Trendelenburg position. After opening the dorsolumbar fascia, left S1–S4 laminectomy was performed. The neck of the inferior cyst was opened by dissecting it from the nerve roots, and the defect in the left medial wall of the dura, which had active CSF leakage and filled the inferior cyst, was repaired with muscle grafts. Later, the superior cyst was opened at the S1-S2 level. Anterior dissection of the cyst allowed visualization of the cyst neck associated with the intrapelvic region. Then, the S1 nerve root sheath was opened, and flow was allowed into the cyst around the rootlets. CSF flow was restored with muscle grafts. Pain and numbness complaints completely disappeared in the postoperative period. On neurological examination, left S1 innervated muscle weakness disappeared and no deficit was detected. She could walk long distances without complaint. On MRI, the intrapelvic cyst completely disappeared. It has been followed for 24 months without any complaints.

## Radiological follow-ups and growth rate measurements

### Method

We evaluated 12 years of 6 MRI follow-ups. The Sagittal Diagonal (SD) and Sagittal Craniocaudal (SC) maximum lengths at each MRI date were used to calculate the size of the cyst at the given date. Maximum length of the SD axis was measured at a 45° angle and SC axis was measured at a 90° angle on sagittal T2WI sections.

The annual relative growth rate was then calculated separately for SD and SC lengths. The Relative Growth Rate (RGR) of the cyst was calculated using the formula:

$$\mathrm{R G R}=\left(\ln W_{2}-\ln W_{1}\right)/\left(t_{2}-t_{1}\right)$$

where $W_{1}$ and $W_{2}$ are the maximum lengths at consecutive MRI dates and $t_{2}-t_{1}$ is the time between consecutive MRIs. This formula is the classical method for calculating plant growth in biology and was adapted to this case. MATLAB was utilized for calculations and creating graphical representations, ensuring in-depth analysis. Microsoft Excel was used for table creation, ensuring clear and concise presentation.

### Result

Table 1 provides a detailed breakdown of the annual Relative Growth Rate (RGR) values for each MRI period, presenting the annual RGRs for both the SC (RGR_SC) and Sagittal Diagonal (RGR_SD) axes. All calculations and evaluations demonstrated that the cyst grows in both directions over time, with the line representing the diagonal length ascending more rapidly than the line for craniocaudal length.

The first values for RGR_SC and RGR_SD are 0, due to the absence of preceding data for length comparison. The overall annual growth rates, 7.671% for RGR_SD and 6.237% for RGR_SC, represent the annual growth observed between the first and last MRIs.

## Discussion

The study spanned 12 years and utilized MRI data to evaluate the growth of a giant Tarlov cyst. Measurements were taken along the SD and SC axes at different angles. The volume calculations were not made due to the cyst's irregular shape and insufficient data. The results showed that the cyst grew in both directions, with the SD axis exhibiting a faster growth rate. In the SC axis, the growth rate fluctuated yet remained consistently positive, indicating continuous growth at varying speeds.

When the cyst overcomes the high resistance of the sacrum bone and reaches the low-pressure cavity in the pelvis, the growth rate increases rapidly. GTCs usually grow quickly because they are reported to have a free flow of CSF between the cyst body and subarachnoid space. We suggest that the speed of cyst growth increases over time due to minimal resistance in the intrapelvic cavity.

## Conclusion

GTCs extending into the pelvis are rare compared to common TCs located in the bony sacrum. The symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, follow-up, and management processes of GTCs differ from intrasacral TCs. We showed for the first time that a GTC grows in both directions over time, with annual growth rates of 7.671% for RGR_SD and 6.237% for RGR_SC. Over 12 years, the cyst grows 2.1 times in the craniocaudal and 2.5 times in the diagonal axis. Early surgery may be considered to prevent rapid growth in the intrapelvic cavity and to reduce possible complications of the giant cyst.
