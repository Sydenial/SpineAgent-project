# Tenosynovial Giant Cell Tumor, Diffuse Type / Pigmented Villonodular Synovitis in a Pars Defect: A Case Report

## Abstract

Tenosynovial giant cell tumor, diffuse type / pigmented villonodular synovitis (PVNS) rarely occurs in the lumbar spine, and no previous English-language reports have described PVNS arising in a pars defect associated with lumbar spondylolysis. We report a case of PVNS occurring in a pars defect in a 14-year-old female with lumbar spondylolysis. The patient presented with a 5-month history of low back pain. Plain radiography showed spondylolysis at L5, and computed tomography revealed a $1 \times 2$ cm slightly eroding tumorous mass at the left L5 pars. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated intermediate intensity and gadolinium enhancement on T1-weighted images and high intensity on T2-weighted and T2 STAR-weighted images. A computed tomography–guided needle biopsy confirmed PVNS, followed by gross total resection. Postoperative imaging showed no residual lesion, and the clinical course was uneventful. This case represents the first reported instance of PVNS occurring in a pars defect in lumbar spondylolysis.

## Introduction

Tenosynovial giant cell tumor, diffuse type / pigmented villonodular synovitis (PVNS) is a slowly progressing proliferative lesion of the synovial membrane with an uncertain etiology. It most commonly affects the knee and hip joints. Spondylolysis is a defect of the pars interarticularis, typically resulting from stress fracture. Pars defects are known to communicate with both cranial and caudal facet joints and may be accompanied by inflammation, leading to low back pain through communicating synovitis from adjacent facet joints [1].

Although PVNS rarely occurs in the spine, several cases involving facet joints have been reported [2]. However, PVNS arising within a pars defect associated with lumbar spondylolysis has not been previously described. This report presents a unique case of PVNS occurring in a pars defect in a patient with lumbar spondylolysis.

## Case Report

A healthy 14-year-old female presented with a 5-month history of low back pain. She was an active basketball player and reported no history of trauma. Physical examination revealed tenderness around the L5 spinous process and bilateral L4–L5 paravertebral muscles. No neurological deficits, including sensory disturbance or motor weakness, were observed.

Plain radiographs demonstrated bilateral spondylolysis at L5. Computed tomography revealed a $1 \times 2$ cm tumorous mass located at the left L5 pars defect, with slight erosion of the L5 lamina. Magnetic resonance imaging, including T1-weighted, T2-weighted, T2 STAR-weighted, short-tau inversion recovery, and gadolinium-enhanced fat-saturated T1-weighted sequences, was performed using a 1.5 T system. The lesion showed intermediate signal intensity and gadolinium enhancement on T1-weighted images and high signal intensity on T2-weighted and T2 STAR-weighted images. Sagittal imaging confirmed that the mass originated from the pars defect.

Computed tomography–guided needle biopsy was performed, and histopathological analysis revealed proliferative mononuclear histiocyte-like cells with scattered multinucleated giant cells, foamy cells, and slight hemosiderin deposition, consistent with PVNS.

Gross total resection was planned, including marginal resection of the lamina and ligamentum flavum. A midline lumbar incision was made, and the biopsy tract was excised. The left L4–L5 lamina was exposed while preserving the posterior elements on the right side. The tumor was found to communicate with adjacent facet joints and adhered to the ligamentum flavum. Total gross resection required partial laminectomy at left L4 and L5 and partial facetectomy at left L4–L5 and L5–S, with careful protection of the L5 nerve root. The resected mass measured $2.5 \times 1.5 \times 1.5$ cm and was soft, elastic, and light yellow-reddish in appearance. Both gross and postoperative pathological findings confirmed PVNS.

The postoperative course was uneventful. Follow-up computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated no residual lesion and no apparent spinal instability.

## Discussion

PVNS most commonly affects the knee (approximately 80%) and hip (approximately 15%) joints and rarely involves the spine [3,4]. The first case of spinal PVNS was reported in the cervical spine in 1980 [5]. Due to its rarity, accurate diagnosis of spinal PVNS requires careful evaluation. Clinical presentations vary and include low back pain, radiculopathy, myelopathy, or incidental findings on imaging.

Imaging plays a critical role in diagnosis. PVNS frequently demonstrates bone erosion on radiographs and computed tomography. Typical magnetic resonance imaging findings include low signal intensity on both T1- and T2-weighted images, with particularly low intensity on T2 STAR-weighted images due to hemosiderin deposition [2]. Gadolinium-enhanced fat-saturated T1-weighted images often show contrast enhancement. In the present case, imaging findings suggested a soft tissue tumor but were not specific for PVNS, necessitating computed tomography–guided biopsy. Spinal PVNS has been reported to exhibit less hemosiderin deposition than peripheral joint lesions [7,8], which was also observed in this case.

PVNS is a benign but locally aggressive tumor, and complete excision is essential to minimize recurrence risk [10–12]. In this case, gross total resection with marginal bone resection was achieved based on a preoperative histological diagnosis. The tumor’s origin could not be definitively determined, but given the known communication between pars defects and adjacent facet joints in lumbar spondylolysis [1,16], it was presumed to arise from a facet joint synovium.

The pathogenesis of PVNS remains controversial, with both inflammatory and neoplastic mechanisms proposed. Overexpression of colony-stimulating factor 1 by neoplastic cells has been implicated in the accumulation of non-neoplastic inflammatory cells forming the tumor mass [15,17]. In the present case, chronic inflammation associated with spondylolysis may have contributed to tumor development, suggesting a combined reactive and neoplastic process.

## Conclusion

This case represents the first reported occurrence of pigmented villonodular synovitis arising in a pars defect associated with lumbar spondylolysis. Complete surgical resection following definitive preoperative diagnosis resulted in an excellent clinical outcome. Careful long-term follow-up is warranted due to the locally aggressive nature of PVNS.

## Key Points

- This is the first reported case of pigmented villonodular synovitis occurring in a pars defect in lumbar spondylolysis.  
- PVNS is a benign but locally aggressive tumor requiring complete surgical excision.  
- Preoperative computed tomography–guided needle biopsy was essential for definitive diagnosis.  
- PVNS in a pars defect may originate from adjacent facet joints due to anatomical communication.
