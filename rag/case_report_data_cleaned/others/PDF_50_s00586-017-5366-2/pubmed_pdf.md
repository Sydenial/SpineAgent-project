# Slow correction of severe spastic hyperlordosis in an adult by means of magnetically expandable rods  

## Abstract 

Background: We describe a case of severe and progressive lumbar hyperlordosis in a 28-year-old female university student with cerebral palsy. Her main complaints were abdominal wall pain and increasing inability to sit in her custom wheelchair. 

Methods: After an initial radical release and partial correction, a slow correction procedure was performed using percutaneously expandable magnetic rods (MAGEC) as a key component for satisfactory correction of this severe and rigid curve. 

Results: Three years after the completion of surgery, the patient has stable clinical and radiological results, as well as solid posterior fusion on CT. 

Conclusion: This is the first case published in which percutaneous magnetic distraction was successfully used in an adult patient.

Keywords: Cerebral palsy, Hyperlordosis, Spasticity, Magnetically controlled growing rods, Slow deformity correction, Viscoelastic.

## Case presentation

A 28-year-old female university student with cerebral palsy presented with slowly progressive abdominal wall pain and increasing difficulty sitting in her customized wheelchair. Pain and a subjective curve increase had been noted over the previous 2–3 years. Various attempts with bracing and botulinum toxin injections had been unsuccessful in halting the progression of the deformity.

Clinically, there was severe hyperlordosis, flexion-adduction deformities of both hips, and a visual pulsation of the skin below the umbilicus, which was the region of the abdominal wall pain. Radiographs, CT, and MRI showed a lordosis angle of 160° without major coronal deformity. Angio-CT and duplex ultrasound demonstrated that the aortic bifurcation, the confluence of the iliac veins, as well as the stomach and possibly the duodenum, were stretched out in front of the spine and pressing against the abdominal wall.

## Surgical approach

Initially, a primary anterior approach was considered but was ruled out due to the high risk of vascular and abdominal complications. A posterior approach was chosen, but we anticipated that a single-stage procedure would not achieve sufficient correction. We decided on a radical release followed by slow correction using percutaneously expandable magnetic rods.

In the first surgery, a radical posterior soft tissue release was performed, consisting of a stepwise release of the erector spinae muscles from the iliac crest, partial incisions of the lumbar fascia at multiple levels, and partial transections of the erector spinae muscles. A pelvic base was constructed with bilateral S1 and iliac screws, along with a thoracic base using bilateral T12 and T9 pedicle screws. A temporary rod was anchored between a lumbosacral and thoracic crossbar for slow and stepwise distraction. Despite the release and mobilization efforts, we were unable to achieve a significant correction in a single-stage procedure.

We then constructed a hinged construct with two 5.5 mm standard MAGEC rods with motors positioned subcutaneously and connected to the crossbars by laminar hooks.

### Fig. 4 Angio-CT
Images from an Angio-CT to help decide the best surgical strategy. The sagittal plane view shows the aorta and abdominal organs stretched over the apex of the deformity.

### Fig. 5 Post-surgical images
After the surgery, a temporary rod was used for slow distraction of the spine, and the abdominal wall was tented. A further radiograph showed some improvement with a lordosis angle of 85°.

Over the following 3 months, the rods were expanded on six occasions, leading to a gradual improvement in the lordosis angle, which was ultimately finalized at 66°. The MAGEC rods were removed during a posterior instrumented fusion from T9 to the pelvis.

## Outcome and follow-up

The clinical and radiological results remained stable for 3 years with a solid posterior fusion. The patient experienced complete resolution of abdominal pain and regained full sitting capacity, along with limited standing and walking capacity with aids. Her functional score improved from Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) level IV preoperatively to an intermediate level III–IV.
