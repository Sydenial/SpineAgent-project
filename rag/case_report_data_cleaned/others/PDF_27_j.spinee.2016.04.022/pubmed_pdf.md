# Developmental abnormalities of the craniocervical junction resulting in Collet-Sicard syndrome

## Abstract
BACKGROUND CONTEXT: Collet-Sicard syndrome describes the paralysis of cranial nerves IX-XII and is the most frequently reported neurologic complication associated with Jefferson fractures. Association with other anomalies of the atlas has rarely been reported. PURPOSE: To report an unusual case of Collet-Sicard syndrome resulting from developmental abnormalities of the craniocervical junction. STUDY DESIGN/SETTING: Case report of a single patient. METHODS: Chart and radiographic data were reviewed and summarized. RESULT: Developmental abnormalities of the craniocervical junction were identified; on computed tomography, the distance between the atlas transverse process and the left styloid process of the skull was 3 mm. CONCLUSION: In suspected Collet-Sicard syndrome, developmental abnormalities of the craniocervical junction should be considered in the differential diagnosis.

Keywords: Assimilation of the atlas; Collet-Sicard syndrome; Cranial neuropathy; Craniocervical junction; Developmental disorders; Hypoplastic dens

## Introduction
Collet-Sicard syndrome describes the paralysis of cranial nerves IX-XII [1]. It is usually caused by a posterior lacerocondylar space lesion, where these nerves are closely related [2]. Earlier reports describe Collet-Sicard syndrome caused by skull base tumors of primary and metastatic origin (breast, prostate, lung, renal cell, cervix) [3-6], multiple myeloma [7], dissection and coiling of the internal carotid artery [8,9], vasculitic conditions such as polyarteritis nodosa [10], jugular vein thrombosis [2], closed head injury [11], skull base fractures [12], and penetrating trauma [13]. In addition, Collet-Sicard syndrome is the most frequently reported neurologic complication associated with Jefferson (C1) fractures [1,14,15]. As the lateral mass of the atlas is displaced laterally away from the spinal cord toward the styloid process and the stylohyoid ligament, the lateral mass can impinge on cranial nerves IX-XII [1,14,15].

Damage to cranial nerve IX (glossopharyngeal) causes loss of taste in the posterior tongue, loss of the ipsilateral gag reflex, and poor pharyngeal elevation with swallowing and speaking, resulting in dysphagia and dysarthria [1,16]. Impairment of cranial nerve X (vagus) causes dysphagia, hoarse speech, ipsilateral paralysis of the soft palate, and loss of laryngeal and pharyngeal sensation [1,16]. The soft palate droops on the ipsilateral side and deviates to the contralateral side during phonation [17]. Direct laryngoscopy can show ipsilateral paralysis of the vocal cords [16]. Damage to cranial nerve XI (accessory) causes paralysis of the sternomastoid and the upper fibers of the trapezius [1,17]. Impairment of cranial nerve XII (hypoglossal) results in paresis and atrophy of half of the tongue; on protrusion, the tongue deviates to the affected side [16,17].

However, Collet-Sicard syndrome in association with other anomalies of the atlas has rarely been reported. We report a patient whose initial presentation of developmental abnormalities of the craniocervical junction was most consistent with Collet-Sicard syndrome.

## Case Report
A 70-year-old man with a history of limited neck motion, hypertension, and left facial palsy presented with 2 weeks of difficulty articulating words and 10 days of dysphagia to solids. He had limitation of rotary neck motion after trauma several decades earlier. There was no recent trauma.

Examination showed a left lower motor facial nerve palsy, a decreased gag reflex on the left, the soft palate pulling to the right, and weakness of the left trapezius and sternocleidomastoid. His tongue deviated to the left (Fig. 1). There was no Horner syndrome. Cervical spine rotation was restricted bilaterally without neck pain. Direct laryngoscopy revealed left vocal cord paralysis. The overall clinical picture was consistent with dysfunction of cranial nerve VII (a previous Bell palsy) and IX-XII (Collet-Sicard syndrome).

Cervical spine radiographs (Fig. 2), computed tomography (CT) (Fig. 3), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Fig. 4) showed congenital occiput-C1-C3 fusion, hypoplastic dens, and scoliosis. The left transverse processes of the atlas and axis were hypertrophic (Fig. 2, left; Fig. 3, left). On CT, the distance between the styloid process of the skull and the atlas transverse process was 3 mm on the left and 13 mm on the right (Fig. 3, right). Brain diffusion-weighted MRI was normal. Brain and neck magnetic resonance angiography (Fig. 5) showed absent right vertebral artery flow and an elongated, stenotic basilar artery. Rheumatoid factor was 10.7 IU/mL (normal range, 0-18 IU/mL).

Videofluoroscopic modified barium swallow study demonstrated delayed swallow, residual barium in the vallecula and piriform sinuses, and aspiration. He was prescribed a dysphagia diet and received swallowing therapy including oral exercises, postural facilitation, and transcutaneous neuromuscular electrical stimulation. He was discharged 8 days after admission and declined follow-up, seeking a second opinion elsewhere.

## Discussion
Occipitalization (assimilation) of the atlas refers to congenital partial or complete fusion of the atlas to the occiput [18,19]. Unilateral occipitalization can cause torticollis in young children [19,20]. Other anomalies may coexist, such as congenital fusion of the second and third cervical vertebrae [18-20]. Atlantoaxial instability may result from aplasia or hypoplasia of the odontoid process or with assimilation of the atlas [18-20]. The C1-C2 articulation is the most mobile and inherently unstable part of the vertebral column [19]. The odontoid acts as a bony buttress that prevents hyperextension [19]. The remainder of normal motion depends on the integrity of capsular and ligamentous structures [19]. When the transverse atlantal ligament is ruptured, a ventral shift of the atlas over the axis can result in brainstem injury by impingement against an intact odontoid [19]. If the odontoid is absent, the risk of brainstem injury is lower [19]. Chronic atlantoaxial dislocation may provoke granulation tissue formation that can constrict the cervicomedullary junction, causing neurologic deficit [19,20]. Symptoms from vertebral artery compression may occur [19,20].

Management of atlas assimilation is complex and individualized [21]. Nonoperative methods such as immobilization in a cervical orthosis should be attempted first in patients with minor symptoms [21]. When signs and symptoms are caused by anterior impingement, occipital-cervical fusion is indicated [21]. When caused by posterior impingement, posterior decompression and occipital-cervical stabilization are indicated [21]. Cranial neuropathy in our patient was not due to vertebral artery compromise nor to brainstem compression from a backward-projecting odontoid or retro-odontoid pannus.

Collet-Sicard syndrome refers to unilateral lesions of cranial nerves IX-XII [1,14]. Cranial nerves IX-XI exit the skull through the jugular foramen; cranial nerve XII exits through the hypoglossal foramen. After exiting the skull, these nerves pass through the space between the styloid process and the transverse process of the atlas [1,14]. There is normally 8-10 mm of space between them [15]. In our patient, the distance from the styloid process to the atlas transverse process was only 3 mm on the left (Fig. 3, right). As the left atlas transverse process was hypertrophic and displaced laterally toward the styloid process, we propose it impinged on cranial nerves IX-XII.

Treatment of Collet-Sicard syndrome depends on the cause. Three reported cases were due to Jefferson fractures [1,14,15]. Two patients treated with halo traction improved: one had near resolution after 2 weeks of traction, 10 weeks in a halo vest, and 2 weeks in a cervical collar [1]; another could drink sufficiently at 8 weeks for feeding tube removal, with voice normalization by 13 months though high notes remained difficult [15]. A third patient treated with a cervical collar had persistent mild dysphonia at 18 months [14]. In our patient, Collet-Sicard syndrome was due to developmental abnormalities of the craniocervical junction rather than a Jefferson fracture. When neurologic symptoms arise with atlas assimilation, occipital-cervical fusion or decompression is suggested [21]. It is unknown whether these procedures relieve Collet-Sicard syndrome resulting from atlas assimilation.

It is difficult to explain the late development of Collet-Sicard syndrome in our patient. Although atlas assimilation is congenital, most patients do not develop neurologic symptoms until the third or fourth decade [21]. Symptoms usually progress slowly, but may develop dramatically [21]. Degenerative changes of aging might restrict motion of the lower cervical spine, increasing demand on the capsular and ligamentous structures of the atlantoaxial articulation. Ligamentous laxity with aging might gradually increase atlantoaxial instability, producing cranial nerve compromise [21]. In addition, younger patients' cranial nerves may be more resistant to compression, which later proves harmful in older patients.

Swallowing difficulty increases morbidity and mortality in Collet-Sicard syndrome. The water swallowing test is a simple bedside screen for dysphagia but has limitations, including lack of reliability, subjectivity, potential to miss silent aspiration, and inability to define mechanism [22]. The videofluoroscopic modified barium swallow study is a standard assessment [22]. Patients require food of modified consistency; those unable to maintain hydration or nutrition orally may need nasogastric feeding [22]. Dysphagia treatment includes compensatory strategies to keep oral intake safe during recovery and rehabilitation strategies to change swallowing physiology and accelerate recovery [22]. Because dysphagia may resolve over weeks to months following cranial nerve injury, reassessment within a few weeks is appropriate [1,14,15]. If a repeat videofluoroscopic study shows no aspiration, the nasogastric tube can be removed and a regular diet resumed [22].

## Conclusions
In suspected Collet-Sicard syndrome, developmental abnormalities of the craniocervical junction should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Patients with known developmental abnormalities of the craniocervical junction who have been treated conservatively may develop Collet-Sicard syndrome during follow-up. Surgical decompression of neural structures might be considered in these patients.
