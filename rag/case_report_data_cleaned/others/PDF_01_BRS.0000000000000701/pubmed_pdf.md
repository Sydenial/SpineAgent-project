# Rheumatoid Arthritis–Induced Lateral Atlantoaxial Subluxation With Multiple Vertebrobasilar Infarctions

## Abstract

**Study Design.** Case report.  

**Objective.** To highlight the probability that lateral atlantoaxial subluxation (AAS) exists in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and induces vertebrobasilar infarctions that are more foregrounded than compressive myelopathy.  

**Summary of Background Data.** Although lateral subluxation is a well-known subtype of AAS, a case of cerebral ischemia associated with lateral AAS has not been reported before.  

**Methods.** A 52-year-old male with a 6-year history of RA had a sudden onset of visual field defect and mild right cerebellar ataxia. Head magnetic resonance imaging revealed acute multiple infarctions in the vertebrobasilar area, and magnetic resonance angiography revealed stenosis of the left vertebral artery (VA). Lateral radiograph of the cervical spine in the neutral position revealed atlanto-occipital assimilation and anterior AAS. T2-weighted sagittal images on cervical magnetic resonance imaging revealed high signal intensity in the spinal cord at C1–C2. Cerebral angiography revealed right VA occlusion and severe stenosis of the left V3 segment of the VA. Three-dimensional computed tomography angiography of the craniovertebral junction revealed lateral AAS due to severe erosive changes of the facet joints, with stenosis of the left V3 portion by a bony component. During conservative therapy, the patient experienced left oculomotor nerve palsy due to a second stroke.  

**Results.** Two months later, the patient underwent occipitocervical posterior fusion with an iliac bone graft. His postoperative course was uneventful, and the left VA stenosis disappeared. At the 45-month follow-up, he had no further infarctions. Bony fusion was radiologically confirmed, and three-dimensional computed tomography angiography revealed good patency of the affected left VA.  

**Conclusion.** In patients with RA, the potential risk of AAS should be recognized. Lateral AAS in particular may induce cerebral ischemia by positional VA occlusion in advanced stages of the disease.  

**Key Words.** Rheumatoid arthritis; multiple cerebral infarction; vertebral artery; atlantoaxial subluxation; positional vertebral artery occlusion; three-dimensional computed tomography angiography; lateral subluxation; posterior fusion.

## Introduction

Atlantoaxial subluxation (AAS) with severe occipitalgia or high cervical myelopathy is the most common type of instability of upper cervical involvement in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [1]. A subtype of this disorder is lateral subluxation, which usually occurs with rotational deformity and is observed in approximately 20% of cases [2]. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are no reported cases of cerebrovascular disease induced by lateral AAS in patients with RA. Here, we present a unique case of multiple vertebrobasilar infarctions associated with RA-induced lateral AAS that were more prominent than compressive myelopathy.

## Case Report

A 52-year-old male presented with sudden visual disturbance. He had a 6-year history of RA with severe osteolytic changes in the knee and hand joints with disability, classified as Steinbrocker stage 3 or 4, long-term steroid use, and a history of cerebral infarctions with residual right hemiparesis. Neurological examination revealed left hemianopsia and mild right cerebellar ataxia. His head position resembled a “cock robin” deformity, with intense dizziness and nausea during side-to-side head movement. Deep tendon reflexes were normal, but glove-and-stocking–type numbness was noted.

Head magnetic resonance imaging revealed multiple acute infarctions in the right cerebellar hemisphere and occipital lobe, and magnetic resonance angiography demonstrated stenosis of the extracranial portion of the left vertebral artery (VA). Lateral radiographs of the cervical spine in the neutral position showed occipitalization of the atlas and an atlantodental interval of 10 mm. Further cervical spine magnetic resonance imaging revealed intramedullary hyperintensity on T2-weighted images at the atlantoaxial level. Cerebral angiography confirmed occlusion of the right VA and severe stenosis of the third segment (V3) of the left VA. Coronal reconstruction images from three-dimensional computed tomography angiography of the craniovertebral junction demonstrated lateral atlantoaxial subluxation and osseous compression of the left VA due to severe erosive changes of the atlantoaxial facet joints.

Heparinization and external fixation with a hard neck collar were initiated during diagnostic evaluation; however, the patient developed a new pontine infarction with left oculomotor nerve palsy without an obvious trigger event. Two months later, occipitocervical posterior fusion with an iliac bone graft was performed. To prevent further mechanical injury to the VA, the stenotic portion of the artery was not exposed during surgery, and blood flow of the left VA was monitored with a Doppler flowmeter. Heparinization was restarted the day after surgery, followed by long-term anticoagulant therapy with warfarin.

The postoperative course was uneventful, with no recurrent cerebrovascular events during the 45-month follow-up period. Bony fusion was radiologically confirmed, and follow-up three-dimensional computed tomography angiography showed complete disappearance of the left VA stenosis.

## Discussion

In patients with RA, the upper cervical spine is the most common site of damage, often causing severe neck pain and myelopathy. Atlantoaxial subluxation is the most common type of cervical instability, whereas posterior and lateral subluxations are less frequent. Although fatal thromboembolic events related to AAS have been reported, cases of vertebrobasilar insufficiency due to lateral AAS remain extremely rare. Abnormal rotation of the atlantoaxial joint often accompanies lateral subluxation; however, the concept that lateral AAS may directly injure the vertebral artery has not been widely recognized. Assessment can be difficult because optimal frontal visualization of the atlantoaxial facet joints is occasionally limited [3]. Therefore, the possible coexistence of lateral AAS and vertebrobasilar insufficiency in patients with RA should be carefully considered.

The mechanism of cerebral embolism in this case is particularly noteworthy. The underlying pathology was positional vertebral artery occlusion, classically characterized by transient hemodynamic cerebral ischemia. In contrast, recent studies have reported embolic ischemia caused by artery-to-artery embolism following arterial intimal injury [10–13]. In the present case, chronic progression of RA likely induced atlantoaxial arthritis and instability. Mechanical compression of the vertebral artery due to lateral subluxation may have caused intimal injury, leading to embolic source formation and subsequent cerebral artery-to-artery embolism. Nevertheless, no apparent embolus or intimal injury was detected by diagnostic imaging.

Myelopathy is a major indication for surgical intervention in patients with RA and AAS; however, stroke events have not been sufficiently emphasized in this population. Because severe and irreversible physical impairment may result from cerebral stroke, the possibility of vertebrobasilar insufficiency should be considered in patients with RA-associated AAS, regardless of the presence or absence of myelopathy.

## Key Points

- Lateral atlantoaxial subluxation presenting with cerebral stroke is a very rare clinical condition.  
- Three-dimensional computed tomography angiography clearly demonstrated osseous compression of the left vertebral artery at the axial level.  
- The potential risk of atlantoaxial subluxation should be recognized in patients with rheumatoid arthritis, as the lateral form of this disorder may induce cerebral ischemia.
