# Spinal Epidural Hematoma Due to Tyre-Blast Injury: A Case Report

## Abstract

**Study Design.** A retrospective case report.  
**Objective.** To report a spinal epidural hematoma (SEH) due to shock wave injury.  
**Summary of Background Data.** SEH is an infrequent condition, and most cases are spontaneous. Traumatic SEH without bone lesions is rare. Exploding truck tyres represent a distinct category of blast injuries, in which shock waves are the main injury mechanism.  
**Methods.** A 33-year-old man presented with weakness and numbness of the upper extremities. Computed tomography revealed an epidural high-signal density without osseous lesions from C2 to C5. Magnetic resonance imaging showed a T2-weighted hyperintense lesion from C2 to C5 with anterior and posterior spinal cord compression, consistent with SEH.  
**Results.** The patient underwent emergency decompressive surgery and was discharged with complete neurologic recovery.  
**Conclusion.** SEH should be considered in blast injuries, as it carries a significant risk of morbidity.

**Key Words:** blast injury, cervical trauma, shock wave, spinal epidural hematoma, tyre-blast injury.  
**Level of Evidence:** 5

## Introduction

Spinal epidural hematoma (SEH) is an infrequent condition but represents an important cause of spinal cord or cauda equina compression [1–4]. According to its pathogenesis, SEH can be classified as idiopathic spontaneous or secondary [5,6]. Most SEHs are spontaneous [7], whereas post-traumatic SEH is relatively uncommon [8]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is considered the diagnostic modality of choice [3].

Exploding truck tyres constitute a distinct category of blast injuries. Injuries from large tyre blasts resemble those from landmine explosions but lack thermal or chemical effects [9,10]. Shock waves are the primary mechanism responsible for these injuries [11]. To our knowledge, no prior report has described an acute SEH caused solely by shock wave injury. We present a unique case of traumatic SEH without associated bone lesions following a tyre-blast injury.

## Case Report

A 33-year-old man was brought to the emergency department with complaints of weakness and numbness in the upper extremities. Physical examination revealed superficial abrasions on the upper body. Neurological examination showed moderate paresthesia with muscle strength graded 3/5 bilaterally in the upper extremities. Deep tendon reflexes were absent in the upper extremities, and hypoesthesia was present at the C4 dermatome.

Computed tomography (CT) of the cervical spine demonstrated an epidural high-signal density without osseous lesions extending from C2 to C5. MRI revealed a T2-weighted hyperintense epidural lesion from C2 to C5 with anterior and posterior compression of the spinal cord, confirming the diagnosis of SEH. Laboratory studies, including hematologic, biochemical, and coagulation profiles, were within normal limits.

The patient underwent emergency decompressive laminectomy at C3 and C4, where maximal compression was observed. The facet joints were preserved. An epidural hematoma measuring approximately 5 × 1 × 0.5 cm was identified and completely evacuated. No active bleeding, spinal instability, or vascular abnormalities were noted. Postoperative CT and MRI confirmed successful evacuation of the hematoma.

Four weeks after admission, the patient was discharged with complete neurologic recovery and was advised to undergo rehabilitation therapy and follow-up radiographic and MRI examinations to monitor for potential instability or kyphosis.

## Discussion

Large tyre blasts generate high energy and may cause severe injuries, with reported mortality rates ranging from 19% to 29% [9,10]. Blast injuries can result from primary, secondary, or tertiary mechanisms [12]. Primary injuries are caused by the initial pressure (shock) wave, predominantly affecting hollow organs and leading to lung injury, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and bowel or eardrum perforation [11]. In the present case, the primary effect manifested as hemorrhage within the spinal canal at the epidural space.

Secondary injuries result from high-velocity projectiles generated by the explosion, whereas tertiary injuries occur when the individual is thrown against surrounding objects. In this patient, there were no clinical or radiological signs of secondary or tertiary cervical injuries. Although SEH can develop following hyperflexion or hyperextension of the cervical spine, such cases are usually associated with fractures, ligamentous injuries, or facet joint disruption, none of which were observed in this case.

Primary blast injury can produce severe barotrauma leading to tissue damage. While various blast-related injuries have been reported in the literature, including pulmonary contusions, brain edema, cranial epidural hematoma, pneumothorax, hemothorax, and extremity fractures [10,13], no previous report has described SEH caused by shock waves alone. Common predisposing factors for SEH include coagulopathy, neoplasm, vascular malformation, and pregnancy; however, none were present in this patient. This appears to be the first reported case of SEH due to shock wave injury in the English literature.

## Conclusion

Spinal epidural hematoma should be considered in patients exposed to blast injuries, even in the absence of fractures or ligamentous damage. Early diagnosis with CT and MRI is essential, and prompt surgical decompression can result in excellent neurological recovery. This case highlights shock wave injury as a rare but important mechanism of traumatic SEH.
