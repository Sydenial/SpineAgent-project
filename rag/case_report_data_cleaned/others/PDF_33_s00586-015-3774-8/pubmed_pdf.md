# What are MRI findings of Spine Benign Metastasizing Leiomyoma? Case report with literature review

## Abstract

Benign Metastasizing Leiomyoma (BML) is a rare disease that results from metastasis of uterine leiomyoma to distant sites with benign pathologic features. Spine BML is very rare, and the information about its features and pathophysiology is seldom known. We present a case of a 42-year-old woman who experienced right buttock and leg pain with paresthesia. She had a surgical history of uterine myomectomy. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lumbar spine revealed a well-circumscribed mass lesion in the posterior compartment of the L4 vertebral body, extending into the ventral epidural space and both foramina. The mass showed hypointensity on T1- and T2-weighted images and strong homogeneous enhancement on gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted images. Tumor removal was performed, and the permanent biopsy revealed the mass as leiomyoma. We reviewed nine previous spine BML reports along with our case and analyzed the clinical and MRI findings of spine BML to identify common denominators. We found that predisposing clinical factors included premenopausal women, a history of uterine myoma, myomectomy/hysterectomy, and lung BML. MRI findings common to spine BML included posterior vertebral body invasion with bony destruction, neural foramen invasion, and canal encroachment. Low T1 and T2 signal intensities with strong homogeneous enhancement were consistent features. Although spine BML is rare, it does exist, and spine surgeons should be suspicious of spine BML when confronted with typical clinical history and MRI findings.

## Introduction

Benign metastasizing leiomyoma (BML) is a rare disease caused by the metastasis of uterine leiomyoma to distant sites with benign pathological features. BML is most commonly reported in the lungs but has also been observed in other locations such as the skin, trachea, bladder, esophagus, liver, adrenal gland, skull base, and spine. It is usually associated with a history of hysterectomy or myomectomy. Despite various reported locations of BML, there is no organized literature specific to spine BML, making it difficult to diagnose. We present a case of spine BML and compare its clinical and radiologic characteristics with previous reports.

## Case Report

A 42-year-old woman presented with right buttock and leg pain with paresthesia, which had started 2 years earlier and worsened one week ago. She had no neurologic deficit or motor impairment. MRI of the lumbar spine showed a well-circumscribed mass lesion in the posterior compartment of the L4 vertebral body, extending into the ventral epidural space. The mass showed hypointensity on both T1- and T2-weighted images and strong homogeneous enhancement on gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted images. An osteolytic lesion was observed on the posterior aspect of the L4 vertebral body, with expansion into the central spinal canal and both neural foramina. The patient had a history of uterine myomectomy 14 years prior. To rule out the possibility of a primary or metastatic tumor, a whole-body Positron Emission Tomography (PET) scan was performed, which showed no abnormal uptake in any organ, including the spinal lesion. The patient underwent surgery for diagnosis and treatment. During surgery, a whitish, adhesive mass was found ventral to the thecal sac, expanding into both neural foramina and the posterior vertebral body. A frozen biopsy confirmed it was a benign spindle cell tumor. Subtotal tumor removal and pedicle screw fixation with lateral fusion were performed on L4/5. Postoperatively, the patient's symptoms improved. The permanent pathology report confirmed the diagnosis of benign metastasizing leiomyoma (BML). Chest computed tomography (CT) and gynecologic sonography were performed to further evaluate the BML. The chest CT showed no abnormalities, while the gynecologic sonography detected three small uterine myomas. Retrospectively, it was noted that the patient had a history of recurrent myomas, though they were small and had shown no significant growth since previous evaluations.

## Discussion

Spine BML is difficult to diagnose due to its rarity. There is limited information on the clinical, pathophysiologic, and imaging features of spine BML. We reviewed the literature and found only 9 previous case reports of spine BML, making this the 10th report of the condition. The distribution of spine BML cases included cervical, thoracic, lumbar, and sacral involvement. Among these cases, a history of lung BML was present in five patients, while two patients showed no evidence of lung BML. Most patients had a history of myomectomy or hysterectomy, and the time interval from the myomectomy/hysterectomy to the discovery of spine BML ranged from 3 to 20 years. This is consistent with the timing observed in lung BML cases.

MRI findings in spine BML typically show a well-circumscribed mass with low signal intensity on both T1 and T2-weighted images. In post-gadolinium T1-weighted images, the mass shows strong homogeneous enhancement. All lesions involve the posterior element of the vertebral body, with bony destruction, spinal canal encroachment, and neural foramen or pedicle invasion observed in all cases with available descriptions. These findings represent a common pattern for spine BML, which had not been previously characterized in detail.

## Conclusion

This study consolidates the clinical and imaging findings of spine BML. Spine surgeons should be aware of the typical clinical history and MRI findings of spine BML, which include (1) a well-circumscribed mass located on the posterior vertebral body with bony destruction, neural foramen invasion, and canal encroachment; (2) low T1 and T2 signal intensities with strong homogeneous enhancement. Further clinical and imaging data collection, as well as pathophysiological studies, are needed to better understand spine BML.
