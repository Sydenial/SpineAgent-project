# Intradural Cavernous Lymphangioma of the Thoracic Spine: Case Report, Technical Considerations, and Review of the Literature

## Abstract

### Background Context

Cavernous lymphangioma is a rare, slow-growing tumor that can cause neurologic compromise when it involves the central nervous system. Involvement of the spinal column is rare but may affect the osseous structures or the epidural space of the spinal column.

### Purpose

We report the first case of an intradural, extramedullary cavernous lymphangioma involving the thoracic spinal cord.

## Introduction

Lymphangiomas are benign, slow-growing tissue tumors with a predilection for the head and neck, but they may involve any organ. Lymphangiomas are classified into congenital or acquired forms and histologically divided into cavernous, capillary, and cystic types. Tumor involvement of the spinal column is exceedingly rare and presents either as a primary osseous tumor or as a soft tissue tumor in the epidural space. When lymphangiomas involve the epidural space, it usually occurs as an isolated mass or an extension from a primary mediastinal lesion. Treatment for symptomatic lymphangiomas of the spine requires complete surgical resection. Recurrence is common when residual disease is left behind.

## Methods

An 83-year-old female presented with progressive gait ataxia, bilateral lower extremity weakness, and a band-like sensation in the middle and lower thoracic dermatomes. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thoracic spinal cord revealed hyperintensity on T2 and enhancement of an intradural cystic lesion at the T5-8 levels, with significant compression of the spinal cord.

## Results

Complete surgical resection was difficult due to the adherence of the tumor to the pial surface and microvasculature of the thoracic spinal cord. Recurrence of the mass was ultimately treated with cystic fluid diversion into the peritoneum. At her 28-month follow-up visit, the patient was able to ambulate with minimal assistance. A comparative literature review is presented. There are no reports in the literature of intradural thoracic spinal cord involvement.

## Discussion

Lymphangiomas are characterized by abnormal dilated lymphatic channels and cystic spaces lined with endothelium of varying sizes containing lymphocytes. These benign hamartomatous tumors are most commonly found within the first two years of life and often involve the head and neck. Lymphangiomas are thought to be true congenital malformations of large ectatic and sequestered lymphatic channels that fail to connect with venous blood vessels or normal lymph tissue. Histologically, lymphangiomas are classified into one of three variants: cystic, cavernous, or capillary lymphangiomas. Cavernous lymphangiomas are composed of thin walls and opaque milky fluid with a lumen consistent with findings in this reported case.

Lymphangioma involvement of the spinal column is very rare. When lymphangioma tumors involve the spinal column, they occur either as a primary soft tissue tumor with epidural involvement, a primary osseous tumor, or a combination of both. The first reported case of primary lymphangioma involving the vertebral body of the spinal column was in 1956 by Falkmer and Tilling. In 2002, Mendez et al. documented nine cases of primary lymphangioma involving the bone, only five of which involved the spinal column. Involvement of the osseous structures of the spinal column may result in devastating outcomes, as massive osteolysis occurs, resulting in severe destruction and instability of the spinal cord, especially at the craniocervical junction.

The first reported case of a primary soft tissue lymphangioma involving the epidural space was described by Saito et al. in 1996, presenting as a dumbbell tumor. Several case reports followed, describing epidural spinal cord involvement, where the tumor originated from neural tissue with subsequent extra-spinal involvement. Epidural spinal cord involvement may also occur due to the invasion of a mediastinal mass into the intervertebral foramen, subsequently involving the nerve root and epidural space. However, only one prior case of an intradural lymphangioma tumor involving the spinal cord was reported.

### Radiographic Diagnosis and Differential Diagnosis

Radiographic diagnosis of a lymphangioma tumor of the spinal column remains challenging due to its nonspecific characteristics. MRI has superiority over computed tomography (CT) because it can define the boundary, size, and loculations if present. Our imaging findings are consistent with the few case reports of cavernous lymphangioma involving the spinal column: low to isointense on T1, high intensity on T2, and homogeneous cyst enhancement with intravenous gadolinium on MRI examination. However, whether cavernous lymphangiomas involve the intradural or epidural space remains difficult to definitively identify on preoperative imaging alone. Differential diagnosis for an intradural thoracic cystic mass is extensive and includes metastatic tumor, schwannoma, meningioma, neurofibroma, sarcoma, intraspinal arachnoid cyst, lipoma, embryonal tumor, hemangioma, meningothelioma, and other cystic structures.

### Surgical Considerations and Treatment Options

Cavernous lymphangioma presents with neurologic decline due to its mass effect. Complete excision is the primary goal for successful treatment. However, as noted in this report, total resection may not be possible. Aggressive attempts at developing tissue planes or dividing shared vascularity between the tumor and pial structures can result in ischemic cord events. Recurrence of lymphangioma is due to incomplete resection rather than malignant transformation. Prior resections, trauma, infection, and hemorrhage of a primary soft tissue lymphangioma may precipitate an "aggravated tumor," resulting in worsening mass effect and subsequent neurologic decline. This is a well-documented phenomenon in other organs of the body where lymphangioma occurs more commonly.

As reported in this case, the dural capsule of the tumor was adherent to the microvasculature and pia of the spinal cord, thus impeding complete resection. Although residual tumor remained, removal of the mass effect allowed neurologic symptoms to temporarily improve. Since surgical debulking of the tumor provided symptomatic management, a permanent method of decompression was attempted by placing a cysto-peritoneal shunt. Although the pathophysiology of intradural lymphangiomas is not well understood, clinical stability after shunting suggests that fluid diversion may aid in symptomatic treatment. Cystoperitoneal shunt placement is an established treatment for developmental and traumatic symptomatic syringomyelia, with reasonable clinical outcomes reported in the literature.

## Conclusion

We describe the first case of an intradural, extramedullary cavernous lymphangioma of the thoracic spine with intradural involvement. Caution is advised when attempting complete resection due to the adherence of the tumor capsule to the microvasculature and pia. Consideration should be given to a cystic fluid diversion procedure if complete surgical removal is not possible.
