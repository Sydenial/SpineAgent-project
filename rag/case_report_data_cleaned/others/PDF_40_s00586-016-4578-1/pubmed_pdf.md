# Idiopathic intracranial hypertension occurred after spinal surgery: report of two rare cases and systematic review of the literature

## Abstract

### Background
Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) is a relatively rare syndrome of increased intracranial pressure of unknown etiology. It is characterized by cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) opening pressure more than 250 mmH₂O, with normal cranial imaging and CSF content. IIH occurring after spinal surgery is extremely rare.

### Methods
We present two IIH cases occurring after spinal surgery and conduct a systematic review of articles reporting IIH after spinal surgery.

### Results
The first patient underwent a posterior decompression and fixation for cervical fractures. IIH symptoms appeared 3 days postoperatively and gradually resolved with appropriate medication. The second patient underwent posterior spinal fusion with segmental instrumentation for congenital scoliosis. IIH symptoms appeared 5 days postoperatively, and the patient died due to irreversible intracranial hypertension despite intensive care and treatment. The literature review revealed five cases of IIH occurring after spinal surgery.

### Conclusions
IIH occurring after spinal surgery is relatively rare; the diagnosis is based on the exclusion of other diseases. IIH should be considered in patients who have undergone spinal surgery as it could develop into irreversible intracranial hypertension.

## Introduction

Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH), also known as primary pseudotumor cerebri, is a syndrome of unknown etiology that causes increased intracranial pressure. It is a condition of increased intracranial pressure without a space-occupying lesion, hydrocephalus, or intracranial hemorrhage. The classic symptoms of IIH include headache, pulsatile tinnitus, transient visual obscurations, blurred vision, diplopia, and papilloedema. The diagnostic test typically shows normal neuroimaging and cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) content.

In 1893, IIH was first described by Quincke as "serous meningitis" in patients with increased intracranial pressure without a brain tumor. Over the last 120 years, this disease has acquired many different names, such as pseudotumor cerebri, benign intracranial hypertension, and primary intracranial hypertension. The term "idiopathic intracranial hypertension" was first proposed by Corbett et al., though the exact etiology and pathophysiology remain unclear. 

Risk factors for IIH include obesity, recent weight gain, polycystic ovarian syndrome, and thin children.

The diagnostic criteria for IIH were initially outlined by Dandy et al. in 1937 and later formulated by Smith and updated by Friedman et al. According to Friedman et al., the criteria for diagnosing IIH include:
1. Papilledema
2. Normal neurologic examination except for cranial nerve abnormalities
3. Normal neuroimaging
4. Increased lumbar puncture opening pressure (≥250 mmH₂O in adults, ≥280 mmH₂O in children)
5. Normal CSF composition

The incidence of IIH in developed countries is about 0.9/100,000 persons and 3.5/100,000 in 15- to 44-year-old females. The incidence is increasing with the worldwide epidemic of obesity. IIH during pregnancy and childhood has been well-documented, but few studies report IIH after spinal surgery.

In this study, we present two cases of IIH occurring after spinal surgery and conduct a systematic review to determine the overall incidence, diagnosis, management, and prognosis of IIH after spinal surgery.

## Case Report

### Case 1: IIH after spinal trauma surgery

A 58-year-old female patient presented with progressively worsening headaches, frequent vomiting, and blurred vision in both eyes on day 3 after spinal trauma surgery. One week earlier, she developed C4 and C5 fractures with spinal cord injury (ASIA-C) after a 50 kg wood crushed her neck. The CT scan showed fractures of the C4 inferior articular process and the C5 right pedicle and lamina. The MRI showed spinal cord swelling and high signal intensity in T2-weighted images at C4–6 levels. She underwent a posterior decompressive bilateral laminectomy and instrumented arthrodesis from C4 to C6. 

The patient developed IIH symptoms 3 days postoperatively. On examination, the patient's vital signs were normal, but her BMI was 31.22 kg/m², and she had 2/5 strength in left biceps and lower extremity muscles. Ophthalmologic examination revealed bilateral papilledema.

A lumbar puncture showed an opening pressure of 290 mmH₂O, which decreased to 210 mmH₂O after removal of 20 ml of CSF. Acetazolamide (500 mg twice daily) was prescribed.

Two weeks after surgery, the patient’s condition improved from ASIA Grade C to Grade D. After 3 months, the patient's BMI decreased to 29.32 kg/m², and the IIH symptoms completely resolved. The ophthalmologic funduscopic examination showed no papilledema.

### Case 2: IIH after spinal deformity surgery

A 20-year-old girl presented with severe headache, pulsatile tinnitus, nausea, projectile vomiting, and blurred vision on day 5 after spinal deformity surgery. She underwent posterior spinal fusion with segmental instrumentation from T2 to L3 for congenital scoliosis. Postoperatively, she was started on methylprednisolone due to quadriceps weakness. 

Physical examination was normal, but ophthalmologic examination revealed severe papilledema with visual acuity loss. A lumbar puncture showed an opening pressure of 300 mmH₂O. After removal of 15 ml of CSF, her intracranial pressure decreased to 235 mmH₂O, and her headache transiently relieved after receiving mannitol.

Unfortunately, 2 days later, the patient developed explosive headaches and vision disturbances. Despite further intervention, she lost consciousness, and her condition deteriorated. Four hours later, the patient’s heart and breathing stopped due to irreversible intracranial hypertension.

## Systematic Review of the Literature

A complete PUBMED search for original articles reporting IIH occurring after spinal surgery was conducted. The search identified 219 articles, from which five cases were included. These cases were divided into two categories: IIH after spinal trauma surgery and IIH after spinal deformity surgery.

In 1995, Daftari et al. presented a case of IIH after occipitocervical arthrodesis. In 2004, Sussman presented a similar case of IIH after a traumatic C5-7 burst fracture. Additionally, in 2011, Kunes et al. reported IIIH after segmental spinal instrumentation for pediatric spinal deformity correction.

## Discussion

Although IIH has been recognized for over 100 years, its pathogenesis remains poorly understood. Several theories suggest abnormalities in CSF homeostasis, venous outflow obstruction, or spinal trauma contributing to IIH. Obesity, recent weight gain, and spinal surgery have all been identified as risk factors.

The diagnosis of IIH is made based on clinical criteria and excludes other causes of intracranial hypertension. Treatment aims to reduce intracranial pressure and prevent vision loss. First-line treatment includes acetazolamide, and surgical intervention may be required in severe cases.

IIH generally has a favorable long-term prognosis, but permanent vision loss can occur if not treated early.

## Conclusion

We present two cases of IIH after spinal surgery. Our systematic review shows that five cases of IIH after spinal surgery have been reported in the literature. Although rare, IIH after spinal surgery should be recognized, and prompt diagnosis and medical therapy can lead to a satisfactory clinical outcome. However, IIH after spinal surgery can also be lethal, and physicians should be aware of this possibility.
