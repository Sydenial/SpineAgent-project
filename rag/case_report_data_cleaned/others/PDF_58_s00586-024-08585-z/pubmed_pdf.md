# Osteolysis Cervical Disc Arthroplasty with Artificial Cervical Disc

## Abstract

**Purpose** Cervical total disc arthroplasty (TDA) is a motion-preserving alternative to anterior cervical discectomy and fusion that has gained popularity among spine surgeons. Although generally effective, TDA has been associated with rare cases of progressive osteolysis, a complication whose natural history and impact on clinical outcomes are not well understood. This case report presents a case of progressive osteolysis following cervical TDA with the SpinalKinetics® M6-C Artificial Cervical Disc and discusses the clinical approach and implications for patient management.

**Methods** We reviewed the clinical course of a patient who underwent cervical TDA with the SpinalKinetics® M6-C Artificial Cervical Disc and subsequently developed progressive osteolysis. The patient's symptoms, diagnostic findings, and treatment progression were documented, with a focus on the timing of symptom onset, imaging, and therapeutic interventions.

**Results** The patient demonstrated a delayed onset of symptoms related to osteolysis, which was identified through CT imaging as a progressive complication. The slow progression of osteolysis in this case underscores the need for careful monitoring, as early symptoms may be subtle but can lead to significant clinical implications if unaddressed.

**Conclusion** This case highlights the potential for progressive osteolysis as a complication following cervical TDA, emphasizing the importance of ongoing surveillance and increased awareness among spine surgeons. Early identification and monitoring of osteolysis may mitigate the risk of severe outcomes and guide timely intervention.

**Keywords** M6-C Artificial Cervical Disc; Cervical total disc arthroplasty; Osteolysis; Non-inflammatory bone loss; Anterior bone loss

## Introduction

Cervical total disc arthroplasty (TDA) was introduced as a motion-preserving alternative to anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) by addressing the problems associated with fusion. This is achieved through the maintenance of functional spinal kinematics, prevention of adjacent segment degeneration, and elimination of pseudarthrosis [1–3]. TDA has demonstrated a lower rate of revision and secondary surgery when compared to ACDF. However, it has also introduced new complications, similar to those observed in larger synovial joints [3,4].

Short-term, non-inflammatory bone loss is commonly observed after TDA but rarely requires revision. In contrast, osteolysis, although less frequently reported, tends to progress and may lead to device removal [3]. We present a case of progressive osteolysis following TDA with the SpinalKinetics® M6-C Artificial Cervical Disc.

## Case Presentation

A woman in her 40s, a smoker with no prior medical conditions, experienced left cervicobrachialgia and underwent double-level TDA with M6-C disc implants at C5–6 and C6–7 at another hospital. She reported an uneventful surgery but had no imaging follow-up. Five years later, she developed morning neck pain, neck weakness, minor left cervicobrachial pain, and occasional episodes of choking on saliva.

After persistent symptoms, she was evaluated by the neurology department, revealing limited neck strength (grade 4) and reduced left arm abduction due to shoulder pain, later diagnosed as calcifying tendinosis and acromioclavicular synovitis. No other neurological deficits were observed.

Electromyography showed only chronic cervical myotome changes, leading to a differential diagnosis of dropped head syndrome. Laboratory tests, including creatine kinase, anti-acetylcholine receptor antibodies, and anti-muscle-specific kinase antibodies, were negative. CT imaging revealed osteolysis around the implants at the endplates, while MRI ruled out myeloradicular compression. After multidisciplinary discussion, a watch-and-see approach was adopted.

Follow-up CT scans over the next four years demonstrated progressive osteolysis, which, in association with worsening cervicalgia, prompted surgical intervention. Preoperative bone scintigraphy showed slight osteoblastic activity around the implants without hypervascularization, ruling out infection.

During revision surgery, the previous right-sided incision was used. A significant greyish foreign body reaction was observed surrounding both implants, particularly pronounced at the upper level. At C5–6, the implant was completely destroyed, explaining the intense inflammatory reaction. At C6–7, an 8 mm tear was identified along the anterior upper border of the annular sheath, with extrusion of nylon thread in direct relation to the osteolytic cavity. A C6 corpectomy was performed, and both implants were removed. Considering the likelihood of an inflammatory reaction to implant components, an anterior iliac crest bone graft with titanium plate fixation from C5 to C7 was performed to minimize exposure to foreign materials.

Cultures were negative, and histology confirmed a foreign body reaction. Postoperative recovery was favorable, and the patient was discharged two days later with full recovery of left arm strength. She was instructed to wear a sterno-occipito-mandibular immobilizer. A follow-up CT scan approximately two months later showed discrete subsidence of the anterior construct. To enhance stability, a second surgery involving posterior fixation of the lateral masses of C5–C7 with a crosslink was performed. Subsequent CT scans confirmed successful fusion without complications.

The patient reported significant neck pain relief and resumed work and daily activities without difficulty.

## Discussion

Cervical TDA is increasingly favored due to its ability to preserve motion at the treated segment, potentially reducing adjacent segment degeneration, and its low rates of surgical complication (approximately 1.5%) and revision (0–0.4%) after long-term follow-up [2,5]. Comparative studies between ACDF and TDA have shown statistically superior clinical outcomes for TDA in terms of overall success, Neck Disability Index success, and neurological success [5]. Nevertheless, cervical disc replacement is not without complications, and data on long-term adverse events remain limited [2,6].

Reported complications include heterotopic ossification, recurrent or persistent stenosis, postoperative kyphosis, implant migration, and iatrogenic vertebral body fractures. Osteolysis following TDA has also been reported, although data on wear and failure mechanisms are sparse. In larger synovial joints, wear between articulating surfaces releases particulate debris that triggers an inflammatory response, ultimately resulting in osteolysis [2,3].

Wahbeh et al. emphasized the importance of standardized terminology to differentiate non-inflammatory bone loss from inflammatory osteolysis. Non-inflammatory bone loss represents a short-term adaptive response to mechanical changes and rarely necessitates revision. In contrast, osteolysis results from an inflammatory response, tends to progress, and may require device removal [3]. Inflammatory osteolysis typically presents as large, scooped-out lesions in the bone surrounding the implant, whereas non-inflammatory bone loss manifests as erosive remodeling, often at the peripheral or anterior vertebral body, also termed anterior bone loss [3,7].

The most common symptom of osteolysis is neck pain, followed by radicular pain and paresthesia. More severe secondary effects, including dysphagia, respiratory compromise, and neurological deficits, have also been reported [3,4].

The M6-C artificial cervical disc consists of a polycarbonate urethane core and an annulus made of ultrahigh-molecular-weight polyethylene fibers. The core and endplates are not bonded, allowing stress distribution and physiological motion. Titanium-coated keels promote bony ingrowth. Although a polymer sheath was designed to limit soft tissue ingrowth and contain wear debris, it has proven ineffective in preventing particle generation. These particles trigger a biological cascade involving phagocytosis and release of inflammatory mediators, leading to osteolysis, foreign-body reaction, mechanical instability, and neurological symptoms [1,4].

In July 2020, the Australian Therapeutic Goods Administration issued an implant hazard alert for the M6-C, recommending routine long-term clinical and radiographic monitoring. Changes in disc position, loss of height, and periprosthetic bone loss may indicate the onset of osteolysis [4].

This case illustrates a complication that remains relatively uncommon but may increase in frequency with the broader adoption of cervical TDA.

## Conclusion

While short-term, non-inflammatory bone loss after cervical TDA is common and rarely necessitates revision, osteolysis is a less frequent but progressive complication that can lead to severe outcomes requiring device removal. The slow onset of symptoms, combined with potentially devastating consequences, underscores the importance of long-term follow-up and heightened awareness among surgeons. Because osteolysis may be asymptomatic, careful consideration is required to balance the risks of revision surgery against the potential for disease progression. Adoption of standardized terminology to distinguish inflammatory from non-inflammatory bone changes is essential for accurate diagnosis and assessment. Further preclinical research and standardized clinical and imaging follow-up protocols are needed to enable early detection and optimal management of osteolysis.
