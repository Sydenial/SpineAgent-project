# Proximal Junctional Failure After Long-Segment Instrumentation for Degenerative Lumbar Kyphosis With Ankylosing Spinal Disorder

## Introduction

Proximal junctional failure (PJF) is an important complication that occurs subsequent to long-segment instrumentation for correction of adult spinal deformity. The phenomenon of PJF is distinct from proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK), in that PJF includes mechanical failure and/or spinal instability [1]. Thus far, many studies have focused on mobile junctions as a site at which PJK/PJF can occur [2,3], and little is known about the relationship between PJK/PJF and ankylosing spinal disorders such as diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH).

We report a case of PJF at the ankylosed junction, but not at the mobile junction, after segmental instrumented fusion for degenerative lumbar kyphosis with DISH. This case report suggests that PJF in the ankylosing spine may cause severe fracture instability and cord deficit.

## Case Report

An 82-year-old female had been experiencing low back pain since 2007. She presented in June 2014 with increasing low back pain. Neurological examination was normal. Standing sagittal radiographs showed decreased lumbar lordosis. A lateral thoracic radiograph and sagittal reconstructed computed tomographic scan revealed abnormal confluent hyperostosis in the anterior longitudinal ligaments from Th5 to Th10 and local reduction of trabeculae in the anterior vertebral body above Th10. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry showed a right femoral neck T-score of −0.6, suggesting absence of general osteopenia. The patient was diagnosed with degenerative lumbar kyphosis associated with DISH.

In October 2014, spinal instrumented fusion from Th10 to the sacrum was performed. Immediate postoperative imaging demonstrated increased lumbar lordosis. Two weeks after surgery, the patient developed severe thoracic back pain while attempting to rise from bed. Four weeks after surgery, she developed progressive lower extremity paresis and loss of distal sensation from the midthorax.

Radiographs and reconstructed computed tomography revealed a junctional fracture of Th10 and adjacent subluxation of Th9. These findings confirmed spinal cord injury caused by fracture of the uppermost instrumented vertebra with adjacent subluxation.

Revision surgery consisted of extension of fusion to Th5 with decompression at Th9–Th10. Th5 was selected as the uppermost instrumented vertebra because local reduction of trabeculae was least pronounced at this level. Postoperatively, thoracic back pain gradually improved, but no neurological recovery was observed.

## Discussion

This case highlights two important clinical issues.

First, PJF can occur at an ankylosing site adjacent to the uppermost instrumented vertebra after long-segment instrumentation for adult spinal deformity. DISH and ankylosing spondylitis are the two primary ankylosing spinal disorders, both leading to ankylosis of the spinal column. DISH, originally described as senile ankylosing hyperostosis of the spine, is characterized by ossifying diathesis with spinal and peripheral enthesopathy [4]. Its prevalence in patients aged 50 years or older exceeds 15% [5,6], suggesting that ankylosing pathology is common among patients with adult spinal deformity.

Numerous risk factors for PJF have been identified, including advanced age, abnormal preoperative sagittal alignment, large curvature correction, and fusion to the lower lumbar spine or sacrum [1,10]. However, an association between ankylosing spinal disorders and PJF has not been previously reported. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of PJF occurring at an ankylosed junction.

Second, PJF in the ankylosed spine may cause severe fracture instability and spinal cord deficit. PJF involves both kyphotic deformity and structural failure of the vertebral body and/or posterior ligamentous complex. Fracture of the uppermost instrumented vertebra followed by adjacent subluxation carries a high risk of neurological compromise due to spinal cord compression [11]. In patients with DISH, ankylosed spinal segments act as long lever arms, increasing fracture instability [12,13]. In this case, pre-existing ankylosis above Th10 combined with long-segment fusion likely resulted in excessive junctional stress, leading to catastrophic failure.

Local reduction of trabeculae in the anterior vertebral body above the uppermost instrumented vertebra was observed preoperatively. Stress shielding caused by load transfer through ossified ligaments may weaken the vertebral body [13]. These biomechanical factors may explain the proximal junctional fracture in the absence of generalized osteoporosis.

## Conclusion

PJF can occur at ankylosing sites above the uppermost instrumented vertebra following long-segment instrumentation for adult spinal deformity. PJF in ankylosed spines may result in severe fracture instability and irreversible neurological deficits. Selection of fusion levels is therefore critical. Ankylosed segments above the uppermost instrumented vertebra should be carefully considered during surgical planning. When fusion to ankylosed regions is unavoidable, extending fixation more than three levels into the ankylosed spine and avoiding vertebrae with stress-shielding-related trabecular reduction may help reduce the risk of PJF. Further studies are needed to clarify the prevalence and prognosis of PJF in ankylosing spinal disorders.

## Key Points

- PJF can occur at ankylosing sites above the uppermost instrumented vertebra after long-segment instrumentation for adult spinal deformity.  
- PJF in ankylosed spines may cause severe fracture instability and spinal cord deficit.  
- Ankylosed spinal segments should be incorporated into objective decision-making when selecting fusion levels.
