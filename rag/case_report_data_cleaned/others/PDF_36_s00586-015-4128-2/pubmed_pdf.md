# Giant cystic sacral schwannoma mimicking tarlov cyst: a case report

## Abstract

**Purpose**  
To present a rare case of a giant schwannoma of the sacrum mimicking a Tarlov cyst.

**Methods**  
A 58-year-old woman had a 1-year history of low back pain. MRI revealed a large cystic mass in the sacral canal with bony erosion. Radiological diagnosis of Tarlov cyst was made.

**Results**  
The patient underwent surgical treatment for the lesion, which revealed a solid mass. Histopathological examination of the tumor confirmed the diagnosis of schwannoma. The postoperative course was uneventful and the patient has had significant improvement in her pain 1 month postoperatively.

**Conclusion**  
Giant cystic schwannoma of the sacrum is a very rare diagnosis overlooked by practitioners for more common cystic etiologies, but its treatment is significantly different. Care should be taken to include this diagnosis in a differential for a cystic sacral mass.

## Introduction

Schwannomas are benign, slow-growing neoplasms that arise from myelinated nerve sheaths. Spinal schwannomas are the most common intradural extramedullary tumors, accounting for 25% of all primary spinal tumors [1, 2]. However, sacral schwannomas are very rare, accounting for only 1–5% [3]. Because of their indolent growth, the mobility of the sacral nerve roots, and the generous width of the sacral canal, these tumors can reach large sizes before becoming symptomatic.

Most schwannomas are solid or heterogeneous tumors, occasionally exhibiting hemorrhagic, calcific, or fibrotic degenerative changes. Cystic changes, however, are exceedingly rare, with only a few cases being reported in the literature [4–6]. We present a 58-year-old woman with a cystic giant sacral schwannoma mimicking an atypical Tarlov cyst.

## Case Report

A 58-year-old woman presented with a 1-year history of left buttock and lower back pain. Occasionally, she experienced left leg pain and rarely tingling sensations extending into her toes in a general sciatic distribution. Her symptoms were aggravated by sitting and progressively worsened throughout the day. Buttock pain improved when lying supine.

General physical examination revealed some loss of lumbar lordosis. Neurologic examination demonstrated bilateral patellar and Achilles hyperreflexia. MRI of the lumbar spine without contrast revealed an expansile $2.2 \times 3.5 \times 3.3$ cm cystic lesion expanding the sacrum beginning at S2–3. The mass principally involved the left S3 segment but crossed the midline and also involved the right S3 nerve root. Signal characteristics differed slightly from cerebrospinal fluid surrounding the nerve roots.

CT scan with contrast revealed a well-circumscribed, hypodense, non-enhancing cystic structure occupying the paramedian sacral central canal, extending from the superior aspect of S2 to the inferior aspect of S3, causing mild expansion and scalloping of S2–S3 and the posterior column.

The preoperative diagnosis was a Tarlov cyst. The patient underwent left sacral laminectomies of S1, S2, and S3 with mass resection and reconstruction using a paraspinal muscle flap. After removal of the thinned sacral lamina, a solid mass was identified beneath a thinned dural layer. The mass was rubbery, with a fibrin glue–like consistency, and was associated with the left S2 nerve root both within and external to the sacral dura.

The dura was slightly torn during bony removal, but no cerebrospinal fluid leak was identified. Normal nerve roots traversing the mass were observed both superiorly and inferiorly and were confirmed using intraoperative neuromonitoring; these were the S1 and S3 sacral roots and were not involved by the tumor. The S2 nerve root was atrophic and nonfunctional, as demonstrated by direct current stimulation. Portions of this nerve were inseparable from the mass and were resected along with the tumor and a smaller lesion of similar appearance. Closure was performed using a paraspinous muscle flap.

Histopathological examination demonstrated Antoni B–type tissue with nuclear palisading in a neurofibromatous background, confirming the diagnosis of cystic schwannoma.

The patient tolerated the procedure well. The postoperative course was uneventful, and she was discharged home 3 days after surgery. At 1-month follow-up, she reported significant improvement in left buttock pain, with symptoms decreasing from constant to intermittent and becoming more diffuse. Mild residual left leg pain persisted, but she was able to walk several miles without limitation.

## Discussion

Nerve sheath tumors such as schwannomas and neurofibromas account for approximately 30% of all intraspinal tumors [7], most commonly arising in the lumbar region with a preference for sensory nerves and dorsal roots [8]. Sacral schwannomas are particularly rare, with only about 50 cases reported worldwide [3]. Due to their slow growth and the mobility of sacral nerve roots, symptoms often do not manifest until tumors reach a considerable size [9].

Typical presenting symptoms include progressive lower back pain, numbness or paresthesias, neurological deficits, and failure of conservative management [10]. Acute pain may occur due to nerve root torsion or intratumoral hemorrhage [11, 12]. Nonspecific abdominal pain has also been reported [13].

Schwannomas with a predominantly cystic component are rarer still. Proposed mechanisms for cyst formation include ischemic necrosis, intratumoral hemorrhage, mucinous degeneration, and microcyst formation. The differential diagnosis for a large cystic sacral lesion is broad and includes abscess, arachnoid cyst, ependymoma, epidermoid cyst, cystic lymphangioma, cystic teratoma, and cystic meningioma.

MRI is the most useful diagnostic modality. Schwannomas typically demonstrate low- to intermediate-signal intensity on T1-weighted images and heterogeneous T2-weighted signal with areas corresponding to cystic degeneration [14, 15]. Rim enhancement after contrast administration is characteristic [15]. CT may show a well-circumscribed lesion with low or heterogeneous density and occasional central necrosis [16]. However, no radiographic features are pathognomonic for cystic schwannomas.

Tarlov cysts, which are commonly located between S1 and S4, arise from the nerve root sheath at the dorsal root ganglion and contain cerebrospinal fluid–like contents [17]. These overlapping characteristics frequently lead to misdiagnosis, as occurred in this case. Because treatment strategies differ substantially, cystic schwannomas should be considered in the differential diagnosis of cystic osteolytic sacral lesions.

Histopathologic examination is essential for definitive diagnosis. Schwannomas are characterized by Antoni A and Antoni B patterns, with spindle-shaped cells and nuclear palisading, and may contain Verocay bodies [18]. Tarlov cysts previously treated with fibrin glue can closely resemble solid tumors, further complicating diagnosis.

Complete surgical excision is the optimal treatment for giant spinal schwannomas and reduces recurrence risk associated with subtotal resection [6, 19, 20]. Although sacrifice of an involved nerve root may be required, complete excision without neurological deficit is often achievable, as nerve root entrapment occurs in only about 50% of cases [21–23].

The surgical approach depends on tumor extent. A posterior approach is suitable when the tumor does not extend anterior to the vertebral body, whereas a combined approach is warranted for anterior extension. Large sacral tumors pose challenges due to fragile tumor capsules, distorted arachnoid planes, increased vascularity, and potential spinal instability from bony erosion. Various stabilization techniques have been described [6, 24]. In this case, stabilization was unnecessary because the anterior sacral bone remained intact.

Giant cystic sacral schwannomas are exceedingly rare and may closely mimic other cystic lesions radiographically. Inclusion of this entity in the differential diagnosis is critical, as appropriate management differs significantly from that of more common conditions such as Tarlov cysts.
