# Metallosis Presenting as a Progressive Neurologic Deficit Four Years after a Posterior Spinal Fusion for Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis: A Case Report

## Abstract

**Study Design:** Case report.

**Objective:** To report a case of progressive pain and paraparesis secondary to metallosis four years after a pediatric posterior spinal fusion (PSF).

**Summary of Background Data:** Metallosis as a late complication of pediatric spinal surgery is rarely reported. Myelographic computed tomography (CT) can be helpful in establishing the diagnosis. The use of serum chromium levels as a means of definitive diagnosis has been suggested, but has only been reported retrospectively.

**Methods:** A 19-year-old male presented four years after PSF for adolescent idiopathic scoliosis with sudden onset of pain and neurologic deficits. Radiographs and CT scan suggested infection. Intraoperatively, purulent material was not noted, but black and yellowish corrosive debris was found around the instrumentation. The cavity was debrided and packed with tobramycin-impregnated calcium sulfate beads. After surgery, neurologic deficits worsened. CT myelogram showed irregular opacification of the thecal sac at the level of the conus. A posterior laminectomy and decompression was performed with removal of all debris and spinal instrumentation. Metallosis within the spinal canal was noted and serum chromium levels were obtained.

**Results:** The patient was discharged one week after admission with improvement of pain and gradual improvement in neurologic examination. Three years post-discharge, the patient is asymptomatic, though examination shows bilateral clonus. Serum chromium levels declined from a high of 4.5 μg/L intraoperatively to 0.8 μg/L at final follow-up (normal: 0.2–0.6 μg/L).

**Conclusion:** Metallosis should be included in the differential diagnosis of late neurologic deficits after pediatric PSF. CT myelography and serum chromium levels may help guide diagnosis; however, surgical exploration is required for definitive diagnosis and treatment.

**Level of Evidence:** 4

## Introduction

Metallosis is a process of local tissue damage and changes in tissue characteristics due to foreign metallic debris [1]. This results from direct pressure or displacement of tissue, chemical reactions to free metallic particles, or surrounding tissue reactions not directly in contact with the metallic debris [1]. Definitive diagnosis is made by histopathology [2]. Clinical symptoms often mimic infection and can include late operative site pain and, less frequently, neurologic deficits [3–7].

Although metal-related complications caused by orthopedic implants are well documented in the literature, there are only five documented cases of metallosis following posterior spinal fusion (PSF) [10–13]. We report a unique case of metallosis in a 19-year-old male presenting with pain and rapidly progressing neurologic deficits four years after PSF for adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS).

## Case Report

A 19-year-old male, four years after PSF from T4 to L1 for AIS with stainless steel implants, presented with three days of low back pain, urinary hesitancy, and paresthesias of the bilateral anterior thighs. He denied trauma but did report a recent episode of tonsillitis. Radiographs and computed tomography (CT) demonstrated cavitation around the right L1 pedicle screw. Laboratory values were normal except for a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 13.6 mg/dL and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 43 mm/hr.

Given concern for infection, surgical exploration was performed. No purulent material was identified; however, black and yellowish corrosive film and tissue were noted around the right L1 screw. There was no evidence of screw loosening. Antibiotic beads were placed, and the remaining instrumentation was left in situ.

Over the subsequent 48 hours, the patient developed progressive flaccid paraparesis below the knees, hyporeflexia, and urinary retention. CT myelography demonstrated irregular and inadequate opacification of the thecal sac at L1. A posterior laminectomy and decompression from T12 to L2 was performed with removal of all spinal instrumentation. Gritty yellow-black material was found tracking through the left L1 foramen and densely packed in the posterolateral epidural space between the ligamentum flavum and dura, causing thecal sac compression from T12 to L2. No pseudoarthrosis or additional areas of debris were identified.

Final pathology was consistent with metallosis. At discharge, the patient demonstrated near-complete sensory and motor recovery, with residual 2/5 plantarflexion strength and mild urinary retention. Serum chromium levels decreased from an initial value of 4.5 μg/L to 3.6 μg/L one week postoperatively (normal: 0.2–0.6 μg/L).

At three-year follow-up, the patient was pain free, without urinary retention, and demonstrated complete motor and sensory recovery. Bilateral clonus was present, sustained on the right and 3–4 beats on the left. Plain radiographs at two years showed no further lysis or cavitation at L1 and no progression of scoliotic deformity. Serum chromium levels remained mildly elevated at 0.8 μg/L.

## Discussion

This case differs from previously reported AIS cases in that there was no pseudoarthrosis or infection identified. In contrast, prior cases involved either Propionibacterium acnes infection or pseudoarthrosis with unstable fusion [10–13]. In all reported cases, including the present one, CT myelography proved useful for diagnosis and favorable outcomes were achieved following implant removal.

Metal debris in spinal instrumentation is primarily generated by fretting at modular junctions of dissimilar metals and, to a lesser extent, similar metals [5]. This process is exacerbated by pseudoarthrosis, even in titanium PSF constructs [5,14]. The resulting debris activates osteolytic pathways, immune exhaustion, and chronic inflammation, often accompanied by elevated CRP and ESR levels [5,15]. In the present case, metallosis occurred in the absence of pseudoarthrosis or implant loosening.

Serum chromium levels may assist in differentiating metallosis from infection. Studies of asymptomatic PSF patients have shown that serum chromium levels decline over time, ranging from 2.7 to 0.3 μg/L at two and four years postoperatively, respectively [16–18]. Other studies suggest that chromium levels greater than 0.6 μg/L indicate implant malfunction, while levels exceeding 3.75 μg/L suggest corrosion, even in asymptomatic patients [9,20]. The peak chromium level of 4.5 μg/L in this patient represents one of the few documented cases tracking serum chromium in a symptomatic individual.

Although limited pediatric spine literature exists regarding metal hypersensitivity or metallosis, related data are available from adult arthroplasty studies [21–23]. These studies do not recommend routine preoperative screening for metal hypersensitivity, reserving testing for patients with a history of metal allergy or prior aseptic implant failure. The patient in this report had no such history, consistent with these guidelines.

## Conclusion

Metallosis should be considered in patients with a history of PSF for AIS who present with late-onset symptoms mimicking infection and rapidly progressive neurologic deficits. CT myelography and serum chromium levels are valuable adjuncts in evaluation; however, surgical exploration remains necessary for definitive diagnosis and treatment.
