# Rigid occipitocervical instrumented fusion for atlantoaxial instability in an 18-month-old toddler with brachytelephalangic chondrodysplasia punctata: a case report

## Abstract

### Study Design
Case report.

### Objective
We report the treatment of an 18-month-old boy with brachytelephalangic chondrodysplasia punctata (BCDP) who underwent rigid occipitocervical fusion (OCF) using screw and rod instrumentation.

### Summary of Background Data
Cervical myelopathy caused by atlantoaxial instability can be life-threatening. Surgical cervical intervention in very young children is particularly demanding because of small and fragile osseous elements. To our best knowledge, this is the first report describing application of modern occipitocervical instrumentation techniques in a toddler patient with BCDP.

### Methods
A 16-month-old boy with BCDP presented with tetraplegia and swallowing disturbance. Imaging revealed hypoplasia of the odontoid process and atlantoaxial instability at the C1–C2 level. The pedicles were extremely small, whereas the laminae were comparatively thick. After preoperative halo-body jacket immobilization, rigid occipitocervical fixation was performed using occipital cortical screws, C2 intralaminar screws, rods, and autologous bone graft.

### Results
No major intraoperative or postoperative complications occurred. Postoperative halo-body jacket immobilization was maintained for several months. Mild clinical improvement in motor function and swallowing was observed at one year postoperatively. Computed tomography at 6 months confirmed solid occipitocervical bone fusion.

### Conclusions
Rigid fixation using screw, rod, and occipital plate instrumentation was successful in an 18-month-old toddler with BCDP and atlantoaxial instability. Stable bone fusion was achieved within 6 months postoperatively.

**Key Words:** brachytelephalangic chondrodysplasia punctata; toddler; instrumented fusion; atlantoaxial instability

## Introduction

Recent advancements in spinal instrumentation techniques have expanded the indications for rigid occipitocervical fusion. However, occipitocervical fusion remains technically demanding in very young children because of their small anatomy and fragile osseous structures. The use of screws, plates, and rods in toddlers with skeletal dysplasia is particularly challenging. To our best knowledge, this report represents the first application of modern occipitocervical instrumentation techniques in a toddler patient with BCDP.

## Materials and Methods

Informed consent was obtained from the patient’s guardians. The male infant was referred to our hospital at the age of 16 months because of tetraplegia and swallowing difficulty. He was unable to crawl, roll over, stand, hold objects, or elevate his upper limbs to grasp objects.

## Imaging Data

The atlantoaxial distance between the anterior surface of the odontoid process and the posterior surface of the anterior arch of the atlas was markedly increased, indicating severe instability. Neck flexion resulted in spinal cord compression that could not be sufficiently reduced. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated hypoplasia of the odontoid process. Although the pedicles and lateral masses of the cervical spine were small and immature, the laminae were relatively thick and well developed.

## Halo-Body Jacket Immobilization

Preoperative reduction of the atlantoaxial joint was successfully achieved using a halo-body jacket under general anesthesia. The reduction was maintained prior to definitive surgical fixation.

## Surgical Technique

A posterior occipitocervical fusion was performed using a plate-rod system. C2 and C3 intralaminar screws (3.5 mm diameter, 20 mm length) and occipital screws (4.5 mm diameter, 8–10 mm length) were placed. Navigation was used with caution because the presence of cartilage, which cannot be detected by computed tomography, may interfere with accuracy. Autologous cancellous bone graft was harvested and placed along the fusion bed. The operative time was 245 minutes, and intraoperative blood loss was 30 mL.

## Results

No major postoperative complications were encountered. Halo-body jacket immobilization was continued for several months. Magnetic resonance imaging at 5 months postoperatively demonstrated resolution of spinal cord compression with decreased T2 signal intensity. Computed tomography at 6 months confirmed solid occipitocervical bone fusion. At one year postoperatively, the patient showed mild neurological improvement: he could shake a toy, kick a blanket with his feet, orally ingest small amounts of food, and interact more actively with his parents.

## Discussion

Surgical treatment of cervical instability in very young children with skeletal dysplasia is challenging because of their small and fragile osseous structures. Previous reports have described mixed outcomes regarding bone fusion in patients younger than 2 years [7–11]. Some authors have reported successful fusion using rigid internal fixation, whereas others failed to achieve bone union despite adequate stabilization.

Sankaran et al. reported successful posterior fixation using sublaminar wires and contoured Luque rods in a 10-week-old infant with congenital subaxial cervical instability [11]. More recent analyses suggest that cervical spine screw fixation provides a significantly higher fusion rate and lower incidence of complications in pediatric patients younger than 2 years with BCDP.

In our experience, limitation of cervical rotation is observed in approximately 50–60% of patients after occipitocervical fixation, with further reduction over long-term follow-up. In the present case, fusion involved the C0–C3 levels, and long-term functional follow-up is warranted.

## Conclusion

Rigid occipitocervical fixation using screw, rod, and plate instrumentation can be safely and effectively performed in toddlers with BCDP and atlantoaxial instability. Solid bone fusion and neurological improvement were achieved without major complications, supporting the feasibility of this technique in carefully selected very young patients.
