# Guillain-Barré Syndrome Following Elective Spine Surgery

## Abstract

**Purpose**  
There is a paucity of literature describing Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) in the elective orthopedic patient. We aim to report one such case following spine surgery.

**Methods**  
A morbidly obese 52-year-old male developed diminished reflexes as well as left upper and lower extremity weakness following surgical decompression and fusion at L4–5. The patient had persistent weakness and progressed to areflexia, at which point urgent lumbar puncture supported a diagnosis of GBS.

**Results**  
The patient was promptly started on intravenous immunoglobulin and made significant clinical improvement with near-complete resolution of symptoms by the 3-month follow-up visit. By the sixth month, he was able to function and ambulate without a cane. GBS is a rare and potentially critical cause of diminished reflexes and weakness in the postoperative elective orthopedic patient. We propose that morbid obesity may have contributed to the patient’s susceptibility to developing GBS following surgery.

**Conclusion**  
Neurologic symptoms of this autoimmune condition may mimic the clinical picture of an elective spine patient, thus confounding diagnosis. If imaging cannot explain examination findings or new neurologic symptoms postoperatively, rare disease processes should be considered in the differential diagnosis.

## Introduction

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is an acute inflammatory demyelinating peripheral polyneuropathy with an incidence of 0.81–1.89 (median 1.11) per 100,000 person-years and is more common in men than in women (ratio 3:2). Initial presenting symptoms include cranial nerve deficits or pain, numbness, paresthesia, and limb weakness that can progress to symmetric ascending flaccid paralysis and areflexia. Prompt diagnosis is critical, as mild GBS can advance rapidly to involve the respiratory musculature and lead to death [1].  

Although GBS is usually preceded by infection, it has been reported following both nonoperative and operative orthopedic management, including total hip arthroplasty, pelvic fracture fixation, and spine surgery [2–5]. Symptoms typically present one week postoperatively, peak within four weeks, and then resolve over weeks to months [1].

Diagnosis of Guillain-Barré syndrome is primarily clinical, with supporting evidence from cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) studies and neurophysiological testing. CSF studies often show albuminocytologic dissociation, or increased protein concentration with a normal cell count. An increased number of mononuclear or polymorphonuclear cells in CSF ($>50$ cells per L) should point away from a diagnosis of GBS. Nerve conduction studies may show slowing or complete blockage of normal conduction. Areflexia and diffuse ascending weakness are hallmark clinical findings. Treatment begins with intravenous immunoglobulin and steroids, with plasmapheresis reserved for severe cases [1]. We describe the case of a patient diagnosed with GBS following elective spinal surgery.

## Case Report

Consent was obtained after the patient was informed that data concerning his case would be submitted for publication. A 52-year-old morbidly obese man (BMI = 49) with a past medical history of hypertension initially presented with progressive lower back pain radiating to both lower extremities. He was unable to ambulate without a cane due to severe pain. Examination demonstrated neurogenic claudication and radiculopathy, and MRI showed severe lumbar spinal stenosis at L4–5. Lumbar epidural injections provided moderate but temporary relief, and his pain persisted. He subsequently developed neurological deficits, including ankle weakness and worsening ambulation.

After failure of conservative treatment, the patient underwent posterior spinal decompression and fusion using a minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (MIS-TLIF) technique. He experienced almost immediate relief of radicular pain with residual left ankle weakness. He was discharged home within 24 hours and ambulated with minimal assistance. At two weeks postoperatively, he showed significant improvement. However, within 72 hours of that visit, he reported new-onset left arm and left leg weakness and was urgently admitted due to inability to ambulate and rapidly progressing unilateral weakness.

On examination, motor strength was 2/5 in all major muscle groups of the left upper and lower extremities. He was alert and followed commands appropriately. MRI of the brain and entire spine was obtained. Brain MRI was unremarkable. Cervical imaging showed multilevel disc displacement and facet osteoarthrosis from C2 to C5, most pronounced at C3–4 with severe central canal narrowing, cord impingement, and signal hyperintensity consistent with myelopathy. Moderate-to-severe bilateral neural foraminal narrowing was also present. Lumbar MRI demonstrated prior L4–5 laminectomy with moderate disc bulge and canal narrowing from L1 to S1. Neurology consultation ruled out intracranial pathology but noted diminished reflexes in the left upper and lower extremities.

The consulting neurologist recommended decompression of the cervical and lumbar spine due to symptomatic progression of spinal stenosis. The patient subsequently underwent anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) at C3–4 followed by revision posterior decompression from L1 to S1. Despite surgery, neurological function did not improve.

A second neurology consultation was obtained. Persistent left upper and lower extremity weakness and moderate right lower extremity weakness were observed. The most significant finding was complete absence of deep tendon reflexes. Lumbar puncture revealed elevated protein with normal cell count, confirming the diagnosis of Guillain-Barré syndrome. Intravenous immunoglobulin therapy was initiated immediately. The patient showed marked clinical improvement, with near-complete resolution of left arm weakness at three months. By six months, he was able to ambulate and function independently.

## Discussion

Guillain-Barré syndrome is most commonly a postinfectious disease, with many patients reporting antecedent respiratory or gastrointestinal infections. It has been associated with pathogens such as *Campylobacter jejuni*, *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*, cytomegalovirus, and Epstein–Barr virus [1]. These infections are thought to initiate immune responses that ultimately damage peripheral nerves.

Four major factors are implicated in the pathogenesis of GBS: antiganglioside antibodies, molecular mimicry and cross-reactivity, complement activation, and host susceptibility factors. Gangliosides are glycolipids widely distributed throughout the nervous system and are critical for peripheral nerve membrane integrity. Approximately half of GBS patients demonstrate serum antibodies against gangliosides. Antibodies generated against pathogen antigens may cross-react with host gangliosides, resulting in immune-mediated nerve injury. The clinical severity and subtype of GBS may depend on the triggering infection and antibody specificity [1,6].

Although uncommon, postoperative GBS has been reported after cervical, thoracic, lumbar, and sacral spine surgeries [5,7]. Prior studies suggest that surgical or anesthetic trauma to nerve roots may expose neural antigens and initiate autoimmune responses, leading to demyelination and neuropathy in susceptible individuals [8,9].

In this case, morbid obesity may have contributed to susceptibility. Obesity is known to alter immune responses and increase infection risk. Adipocytes secrete cytokines and adipokines, including leptin, adiponectin, TNF-α, and IFN-α, which influence inflammatory pathways. Dysregulated secretion of these mediators may affect immune cell interactions and macrophage differentiation, potentially increasing vulnerability to autoimmune conditions such as GBS [10].

This patient presented with progressive weakness and diminished reflexes following spinal decompression and fusion. Despite multiple surgeries addressing structural abnormalities, his neurologic deficits progressed to areflexia. This case highlights the importance of considering rare diagnoses when postoperative neurological findings cannot be explained by imaging alone. Although uncommon, GBS is potentially life-threatening and requires prompt recognition and treatment.
