# The Early and Late Stage of Crowned Dens Syndrome: Two Cases Report

## Abstract

**Background.** Crowned dens syndrome is a rare form of calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate deposition disease (CPPD) and often presents with recurrent neck pain, stiffness of the neck, increased erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and episodes of fever.

**Purpose.** The goal of this report is to identify the early stage and late stage of crowned dens syndrome (CDS) and its consequences as the result of repeated attacks of CDS at the cervical spine in its late stage.

**Study Design.** Case report.

**Methods.** We reported one case of early-stage CDS and one case of late-stage CDS.

**Results.** The two patients shared some common clinical features of acute attacks of CDS, such as increased ESR, CRP, episodes of fever, and increased white blood cell count along with high blood glucose levels. The first case showed the early phase of CDS, with CT scan demonstrating only mild calcification around the dens. The second case demonstrated the later stage of CDS with more severe chronic degenerative changes of the cervical spine.

**Conclusions.** Early-stage CDS can be difficult to identify due to mild clinical symptoms, but CT scan is a preferable method to demonstrate densities surrounding the top and sides of the odontoid process. In the late stage of CDS, radiographic features often include diffuse periodontoid calcifications, diffuse destructive discopathies, and apophyseal joint destruction, and patients might have severe neurological symptoms.

## Introduction

Bouvet first described crowned dens syndrome (CDS) in 1985 in four women who had neck pain and whose CT scans revealed radiopaque densities surrounding the top and sides of the odontoid process in a crown- or halo-like distribution [1]. The diagnosis of CDS is based on inflammation of all odontoid articular structures, including the synovial membrane, articular capsule, and ligaments [2]. It is known that patients with CDS present with recurrent neck pain, stiffness of the neck, increased erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and episodes of fever [3]. CT scan is the preferred method to detect calcifications around the odontoid process. Currently, there is no guideline regarding the treatment of CDS, and the mainstay of treatment is short courses of oral steroid therapy or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. The goal of this report is to describe early- and late-stage findings in CDS.

## Case Report

### Case 1

A 63-year-old man with a medical history significant for hypertension and diabetes was admitted to the hospital due to uncontrolled diabetes. He also reported a one-week history of headache, fever, neck pain, and difficulty moving his neck. He had experienced similar neck symptoms in the past, which were treated with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and rest. A spine surgery consultation was requested because of neck pain and stiffness.

Initial evaluation showed an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 60 mm/hour and 12 units, respectively. Physical examination showed tenderness over the neck and occipital regions. The range of motion of the neck, including flexion, extension, and lateral rotation, was limited. Neurological examination was normal.

CT scan of the cervical spine showed calcifications in soft tissues adjacent to the odontoid, characterizing CDS, and only mild degenerative changes in the lower cervical region. MRI showed no obvious compression of the cervical spinal cord. The diagnosis of CDS was made based on CT imaging findings and clinical symptoms.

### Case 2

An 81-year-old man with a medical history significant for hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, type II diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular disease presented with a one-month history of progressive neck pain and decreased muscle power in his upper limbs. One month prior, symptoms started suddenly with right shoulder pain and spread to the neck and scalp, accompanied by difficulty moving his neck. He also noted progressive weakness in his upper extremities.

On admission, musculoskeletal examination showed limited passive rotation of the neck. His right knee was swollen and tender, with moderate effusion. Neurological examination revealed muscle power of grade III/VI in both upper extremities. Laboratory evaluation showed an elevated ESR of 75 mm/hour and CRP of 9.8 units. Synovial fluid analysis demonstrated CPPD crystals with no organisms.

CT images on sagittal view revealed foci of increased densities surrounding the odontoid. Coronal views demonstrated multiple calcified foci around the tip of the dens due to atlantoaxial synovial calcifications in a crown- or halo-like distribution. CT imaging also showed diffuse destructive arthropathy in the cervical spine. Pelvic CT demonstrated multiple calcification foci around the pubic symphysis. MRI of the cervical spine showed enhancement of the odontoid process and adjacent soft tissues, compressing the spinal cord at the C2 level.

The patient was evaluated by the spine surgery team for cervical myelopathy and decreased muscle power of the upper extremities. He was treated conservatively with a cervical spine collar and started on 40 mg of prednisone for three days with gradual tapering. His muscle power and neck movement returned to normal within five days. Before discharge, his ESR decreased to 30 mm/hour, with significant improvement in his general condition.

## Discussion

Crowned dens deposits are caused by calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate (CPPD) crystal deposition. CPPD diseases result from the formation of CPPD crystals in joints and periarticular tissues. The diagnosis of CPPD disease is made clinically by chondrocalcinosis on radiographs and identification of CPPD crystals in synovial fluid [5]. CPPD deposits can also be observed in the spine, especially in intervertebral discs, posterior longitudinal ligament, facet joints, and ligamentum flavum [6]. Few studies have investigated the natural history of CDS, and the effectiveness of medical or surgical treatment with long-term follow-up has not been established. In this report, two cases of CDS in early and late stages were presented along with their clinical and radiographic features.

In the early stage of CDS, the spine may be the only site of CPPD disease and is generally asymptomatic [7]. The first case in this report involved a middle-aged man who had only mild neck symptoms and had never sought medical attention previously. The diagnosis of CDS was made based on typical clinical symptoms and CT findings, which showed only limited calcifications around the dens on coronal views, while other levels of the subaxial cervical spine were minimally affected. Sekijima et al. [8] reported a series of 14 CPPD patients with periodontoid calcification, showing that patients with isolated dens calcification were younger than those with more extensive cervical involvement. These findings are consistent with our first case and suggest that early-stage CDS may be easily missed. CT scanning allows easier demonstration of minute calcifications around the odontoid process, with sensitivity superior to MRI, especially in the early stage of CDS [9].

In the late stage, patients usually have severe neck symptoms with neurological deficits due to long-standing repeated attacks of CDS in the cervical spine [10]. CPPD crystals may deposit in intervertebral discs and apophyseal joints, causing chronic inflammation and destructive lesions. Our second patient had severe CPPD disease with erosive cervical spondyloarthritis, likely due to repeated CDS attacks. Imaging demonstrated periodontoid calcifications and advanced spondyloarthritis involving the atlantoaxial joint, intervertebral discs, and facet joints, resulting in diffuse destructive discopathies and joint destruction. Diffuse calcifications of the pubic symphysis were also present. Due to poor general condition and compromised bone quality, medical rather than surgical treatment was selected.

In summary, because of the rarity of the disease and its nonspecific presentation, early-stage CDS is often missed by clinicians and radiologists. Undiagnosed CDS can lead to severe chronic inflammatory destruction of spinal structures and neurological deficits, as demonstrated in our second case. Treatment of late-stage CDS can be challenging due to extensive bone destruction, poor bone quality, and neurological involvement. Early identification of CDS is crucial to prevent severe complications in the late stage.
