# The Crooked Rod Sign: A New Radiological Sign to Detect Deformed Threads in the Distraction Mechanism of Magnetically Controlled Growing Rods and a Mode of Distraction Failure

## Abstract

**Study Design:** Case report.  
**Objective:** To report a unique mechanical failure of magnetically controlled growing rods (MCGR) that is related to continuous rod slippage.  
**Summary of Background Data:** Despite the growing popularity of MCGR in the management of patients with early-onset scoliosis, postoperative complications and reoperations are not uncommon. Unique complications or unplanned reoperations are observed in patients with MCGRs that are not seen in traditional growing rods. The complications include rod slippage, mismatch between targeted and achieved distraction length, metallosis, and actuator pin fracture. We have identified an unreported failure mechanism whereby deformed threads occur in the internal distraction mechanism of the MCGR. This phenomenon may indicate increased MCGR internal screw friction and increased distraction resistance. This complication ultimately led to distraction failure and revision surgery.  
**Methods:** A girl with early-onset scoliosis was treated with dual MCGRs. She developed proximal junctional kyphosis and continuous rod slippage. Distractions in the outpatient clinic and under sedation did not achieve successful rod lengthening. A telltale “crooked rod” radiological sign was identified by angulation between the actuator and the extendable portion of the rod.  
**Results:** Rod exchange was performed, and she is now two years after revision surgery with successful lengthening episodes. Upon review of the extracted MCGR, distraction was not possible even after rod removal and was only resumed after the screw was manually reinserted along its correct threads.  
**Conclusions:** Deformed threads are a complication that cannot be reverted without rod removal and exchange. Users should be aware of this potential failure mechanism.  
**Level of Evidence:** 4.

## Key Points

1. Continuous distractions in the background of rod slippage are not recommended.  
2. Increased rod internal screw friction and resistance to distraction can cause thread deformation.  
3. With deformation, the magnetically controlled growing rod is unable to lengthen.  
4. A “crooked rod” sign identified on radiographs represents angulation in the rod resulting from dislodgement of the internal screw.

## Background

Magnetically controlled growing rods have become one of the mainstay treatment options for early-onset scoliosis. They allow more frequent non-surgical outpatient distractions to mimic physiological growth. More regular monitoring can be performed with ultrasound examinations, which have no radiation exposure. Clinical and radiological efficacy are on par with other methods [4–7]. Their applicability can also be extended to surgical correction of severe deformities [8]. Recent long-term results and studies of graduate cases report consistent spine and rod lengthening throughout growth and avoidance of autofusion of the spine.

Despite these benefits, complications and unplanned reoperations are not uncommon. Teoh et al. [7] reported a 50% failure rate, and Kwan et al. [10] indicated that 40% of patients develop distraction failure and proximal anchor loosening. Recently, several unique complications including rod slippage and mismatches between targeted and achieved distraction length have been reported. Current understanding of rod slippage, also known as clunking or stalling, is limited. Rod slippage indicates failed rotation of the magnets within the actuator in response to the external magnetic controller, leading to failed distraction of the rod [1]. It presents as a “clunking” sound compared with the usual smooth “wobble” sensation and continuous rotation. However, established protocols for appropriate intervention are lacking, and it remains uncertain whether distractions should continue and whether complications may arise from rod slippage.

A newly identified failure mechanism where deformed threads occur within the internal distraction mechanism of the MCGR is reported. It is suspected to be caused by continuous rod slippage. This event leads to distraction failure and ultimately revision surgery.

## Clinical Presentation

A girl diagnosed with Sotos syndrome presented at the age of 3 years with a double major curve of 41° from T6–T11 and 44° from T11–L4. MRI showed no intraspinal pathology. Despite good compliance with bracing, there was progression of the deformity to 54° from T1–L4. Dual MCGRs in standard and offset configuration were implanted at T5–L4 with a claw construct at the proximal foundation (T5–T6) and pedicle screws at the distal foundation (L3–L4). Postoperatively, she was given a corset with shoulder straps for three months before monthly outpatient distractions were performed.

Despite the finding of proximal junctional kyphosis five months after surgery, early distractions were smooth and successful, with monthly 2 mm lengthening achieved on both rods. Abruptly, rod slippage occurred in both rods, and no lengthening was observed. Targeted 2 mm distraction was continued at the next two clinic visits despite continuous rod slippage and failed lengthening. The difficulties were initially attributed to an uncooperative patient who became irritable during distraction sessions. Subsequently, a distraction session under sedation was performed to eliminate soft tissue resistance contributed by muscle contraction. Despite successful distraction on the left rod, continuous slippage was still observed on the right rod.

As further lengthening was unsuccessful, rod exchange was performed with proximal extension to T3–T4 to control the proximal junctional kyphosis. Intraoperatively, metallosis was observed around the screw–rod junction. After rod exchange, smooth distractions were again observed for both rods. The patient is now two years after the rod exchange with monthly successful lengthenings.

## Radiological and Mechanical Findings

Retrospective analysis of radiographs revealed rod angulation between the actuator and the extendable portion of the rod. Upon review of other patients in our series who experienced distraction failure and continuous rod slippage ($n = 8$), none showed a similar crooked rod sign on follow-up radiographs. The extracted MCGRs were studied in our mechanical laboratory. Externally, there was no rod fracture, but there were multiple wear marks at the junction between the extended portion of the rod and the actuator. Rod angulation was also observed by placing the rod next to a straight bench edge.

After dissection of the extended portion and actuator, it was revealed that the threads of the internal screw responsible for distraction were no longer in line, demonstrating deformation. As a result, the screw refused to rotate under the external magnetic force due to increased resistance. After the screw was dislodged and reinserted along the correct threads, it was able to rotate manually.

## Discussion

This report describes a new failure mechanism of MCGRs related to increased internal screw friction and resistance to distraction caused by deformation of the threads. This represents an additional mechanical failure mechanism beyond those previously reported, including pin fractures, corrosive debris, and metallosis [13–16]. This complication may be identified with meticulous scrutiny of radiographs during the period of interest.

It is prudent for MCGR users to closely examine radiographs for subtle angulation between the actuator and the extendable portion of the rod, particularly when rod slippage occurs. Although the immediate radiograph prior to continuous slippage showed no obvious abnormality, the subsequent radiograph obtained during distraction under sedation demonstrated angulation at the MCGR that should not be present in any functional rod. This finding is subtle and may only become apparent with careful retrospective assessment of plain radiographs, potentially aided by drawing reference lines along the borders of the actuator and extended portion of the rod. If these lines intersect, a deformed thread complication may be present. Due to overlap of dual rods on lateral radiographs, this sign may only be apparent on posteroanterior views.

The failed distraction can be explained by increased resistance from the deformed threads preventing rotation of the internal screw under the magnetic driving force of the external controller, thus preventing rod extension. The patient did not demonstrate significant axial growth during the months when rod slippage occurred, suggesting that soft tissue tension may have contributed significantly to failed lengthening. Continuous rod slippage may therefore serve as a warning sign to avoid further forced distraction attempts. Proximal junctional kyphosis may also have contributed, as increased kyphosis can apply additional anterior loading to the proximal end of the MCGR. The relationship between rod alignment, anterior loading, and thread deformation warrants further study.

Based on these observations, MCGR users are advised to avoid distraction attempts when continuous rod slippage occurs, as this may jeopardize the longevity of the implant. Radiographs should be carefully scrutinized for the “crooked rod sign,” which represents angulation between the extended portion and the actuator and indicates deformed threads within the internal distraction mechanism. Once this complication occurs, revision surgery with rod exchange is inevitable.
