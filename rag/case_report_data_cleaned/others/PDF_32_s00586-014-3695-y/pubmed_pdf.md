# Intrapiriformis Lipoma: An Unusual Cause of Piriformis Syndrome

## Abstract

We report on a patient with an unusual cause of non-discogenic sciatica. A 48-year-old woman presented with a 10-day history of gradually worsening left buttock pain radiating to the ipsilateral posterior thigh and calf. She had a similar episode of less intense pain 8 months before which lasted about 2 months. She denied any history of antecedent trauma or back pain. MRI scan revealed a well-defined, fat-containing lesion of the left piriformis muscle, similar to a lipoma, displacing the sciatic nerve but not invading it. The sciatica was relieved after excision of the lesion. The patient remained asymptomatic after the operation.

## Introduction

Piriformis syndrome (PS) is a neuromuscular disorder caused by the sciatic nerve becoming compressed in the infrapiriformis canal, characterized by sciatic-type pain, tingling, and numbness in the buttocks along the sciatic nerve pathway down to the lower thigh and calf. Patients usually benefit from nonoperative treatment; however, in a small subset of patients, non-operative measures are not successful and surgical decompression of the sciatic nerve at the greater sciatic notch is performed.

Lipomas are common benign tumors of mature fat. They may occur in a subcutaneous, intermuscular, or intramuscular location. Most remain asymptomatic, and symptoms caused by nerve compression are unusual, but a large intramuscular lipoma occurring in the piriformis muscle can be associated with compression of the sciatic nerve and PS. We report the rare case of a patient with a large intrapiriformis lipoma causing intense symptoms similar to piriformis syndrome.

## Case Report

A 48-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with a 10-day history of gradually worsening left buttock pain radiating to the left posterior thigh and calf. There was no back pain. The pain was exacerbated by movement, coughing, or sneezing and relieved by lying supine with fluctuating periods of relief throughout the day. She was reluctant to move while in bed and was unable to stand or walk due to severe leg pain.

The patient was not febrile on admission and had no known infections. She was unable to perform straight leg raise on the left side due to pain. Other hip movements, including flexion and internal rotation, were painful, and the pain worsened with ambulation. Lasegue's sign was positive. Buttock pain was localized by palpation next to the projection of the piriformis muscle and was reproduced by stretching (flexion-adduction-internal-rotation and Freiberg test) and contraction resisted maneuver (Beatty test). Neurological examination of the lower limb did not demonstrate any loss of sensation or reduced muscle power in any of the nerve root distributions. Her abdominal examination was unremarkable.

From the past medical history, the patient suffered from known polycystic kidney disease and had a similar episode of less intense pain in the left buttock radiating to the left posterior thigh and calf 8 months before. This episode lasted about 2 months. Blood results on admission showed normal electrolytes, normal full blood count, chronically raised creatinine to 119 mol/l, and urea to 9.0 mmol/l, raised C-reactive protein to 80 mg/l.

The plain radiograph images of her lumbar spine and pelvis were unremarkable. Ultrasound examination revealed both kidneys to be enlarged and containing multiple cysts of varying sizes throughout, with no obvious calculi or hydronephrosis, no evidence of hemorrhage into any cyst, and normal appearances of the urinary bladder.

MRI scan of the lumbar/sacral area of the spine and of the left hip revealed a well-defined, fat-containing lesion within the patient's left piriformis muscle, measuring approximately 7 cm mediolateral, 3 cm anteroposterior, and 6 cm craniocaudal. The sciatic nerve fibers were displaced anteriorly by this intramuscular mass with localized compression but with no encasement. There was no nerve root compression or bone-disc pathology of the lumbar/sacral spine.

## Outcome and Follow-up

Analgesic medication was grossly ineffective, and the patient continued to report intense symptoms after 1 month. Two months after the initial admission, the patient underwent surgical intervention. Tumor dissection was performed using a posterolateral approach to the hip, and the gluteal maximus muscle was dissected to provide access to the piriformis muscle. The intramuscular lipoma was subsequently exposed and excised with a marginal line of resection.

Postoperatively, there were no complications, and she was discharged, regularly reviewed in the clinic, with full resolution of symptoms and return to everyday activities. Final histology confirmed the mass to be a lipoma with no evidence of malignancy.

## Discussion

Piriformis syndrome is a neuromuscular disorder caused by the compression of the sciatic nerve in the infrapiriformis canal, leading to sciatic-type pain, tingling, and numbness in the buttocks, extending along the sciatic nerve pathway to the lower thigh and leg. The etiologies of sciatic nerve compression include inflammatory, traumatic, tumoral, and malformative causes.

In most cases, the compression is muscular, with the piriformis muscle being the primary suspect. Injury to the piriformis muscle, resulting in spasm, edema, and contracture, causes compression of the sciatic nerve, leading to buttock pain that spreads ipsilaterally.

In our case, an intrapiriformis lipoma enlarging the muscle was the cause of sciatic nerve compression and secondary piriformis syndrome. As the lipoma grew, symptoms appeared initially and recurred after 8 months. Compression of peripheral nerves by lipomas is rare in medical literature, with a greater predilection for lipomas involving upper extremity nerves.

To the best of our knowledge, an intramuscular lipoma of the piriformis muscle has not been previously reported in the literature as a cause of secondary piriformis syndrome.

Diagnosis of piriformis syndrome can often be missed or delayed due to its rarity, non-specific symptoms, and absence of definitive diagnostic tests. MRI imaging can be helpful for diagnosis, particularly when the focus is on the pelvis and ipsilateral hip, rather than the lumbar spine.

Intrapiriformis lipoma can be difficult to diagnose due to its deep location and may remain asymptomatic if it does not grow. However, if large enough, it can cause sciatic nerve entrapment. MRI imaging can effectively diagnose the lipoma and differentiate it from a soft tissue sarcoma, showing a well-demarcated lesion with the same signal characteristics as mature fat.

Treatment of piriformis syndrome with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory agents and physical therapy is well-documented. In patients who do not benefit from these measures, corticosteroid or botulinum toxin injections are recommended. Surgical treatment is typically performed when conservative measures fail, including excision of the intramuscular lipoma and release of the piriformis muscle.

In our case, conservative treatment failed, and MRI images indicated the need for surgical treatment. Surgery included excision of the intramuscular lipoma with a marginal line of resection and release of the piriformis muscle. The patient experienced immediate relief of symptoms after surgery.

Front-line medical practitioners should be aware of this condition and consider lipomas and other occupying lesions of the pelvic muscles as a differential diagnosis in patients presenting with radicular pain. Due to its relative rarity, it is often encountered infrequently, leading to diagnostic delays, unnecessary investigations, and increased morbidity.
