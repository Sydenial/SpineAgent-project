# Infiltrative Intramuscular Myxoma of the Cervical Spine: A Case Report

## Abstract

BACKGROUND CONTEXT: Myxomas are benign tumors of mesenchymal cell origin that usually present as solitary lesions. They are infrequently associated with fibrous dysplasia, as in McCune-Albright or Mazabraud syndrome. Myxomas can develop in a variety of locations, although the most frequent sites are the thigh, buttocks, shoulder, and upper arm. Intramuscular myxomas (IMs) refer to lesions that occur within muscle compartments. They have been infrequently reported in the neck musculature.

PURPOSE: To date, only five cases have been reported within the posterior neck muscles without associated intraspinal extension. To our knowledge, this is the first case of an IM presenting with extension into the spinal canal.

STUDY DESIGN: We report a case of posterior cervical IM with intraspinal extension presenting in a 63-year-old woman as a palpable mass.

METHODS: Complete intralesional resection of the tumor was achieved by standard midline posterior approach. Meticulous resection of the entire capsule was achieved and all margins were confirmed to be free of neoplasm.

RESULTS: A diagnosis of myxoma was provided on pathologic evaluation. Follow-up at 1.5 years confirmed maintained complete resolution of the preoperative symptoms, with no evidence of local recurrence on imaging.

CONCLUSIONS: Intramuscular myxomas should be included in the differential diagnosis of cervical paraspinal tumors. Furthermore, we suggest that masses involving the axial muscles should be closely monitored and the patient counseled regarding potential neurologic sequelae.

Keywords: Intramuscular myxoma; Cervical spine myxoma; Infiltrative myxoma; Spinal tumor; Cord compression; Lytic spine lesion

## Introduction

Although myxomas in general are common and have been known to occur in a variety of locations [1,2], intramuscular myxomas (IMs) are infrequently reported in the scientific literature, with an incidence of one in 1 million population per year [3]. Intramuscular myxomas affecting the cervical paraspinal musculature are rare [4], and despite an extensive search of the literature, we could not find any reports of extension into the spinal canal. Most IMs are slow-growing but nevertheless can be infiltrative, requiring prompt intervention to minimize potential morbidity.

## Case Report

A 63-year-old woman presented with complaints of neck and radicular right arm pain, with associated numbness and tingling, starting 2 years before presentation. Over time, her pain improved except for a palpable, mostly nontender mass over the posterior aspect of her neck on the right. She remained neurologically intact.

X-ray imaging showed a vague lucency involving the posterior elements at the C5 level, which was confirmed on computed tomography to extend into the right pedicle. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed an enhancing lesion at the level of C4–C5 measuring 27 × 16 × 38 mm, involving the right paraspinal soft tissues and causing mild mass effect on the thecal sac. Magnetic resonance angiography confirmed that the vertebral artery was not involved.

At 1.5 years after gross total resection of the tumor, the patient was symptom-free with return to normal activities. Follow-up contrast-enhanced computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging of the cervical spine showed no evidence of tumor recurrence.

## Surgical Technique

The patient was positioned prone with midline exposure from C3 to C6. On the right side, the plane surrounding the tumor was carefully identified starting superficially, then extending mediolaterally, and finally rostrocaudally. The tumor appeared primarily well circumscribed, encapsulated, and firm. Given this overall benign intraoperative appearance, an attempt at gross total resection was undertaken.

On reaching the lateral aspect of the tumor, large feeding vessels were identified, bipolar coagulated, and divided. The extradural portion was resected meticulously by removing the remaining portion attached to the C4–C5 right-sided facet joint. Further inspection revealed that the C4 and C5 right hemilaminae were grossly resorbed and infiltrated with tumor, with residual epidural tumor remaining. Right-sided C4 and C5 hemilaminectomies were performed, and the epidural component was dissected free. Residual tumor within the right-sided C4–C5 facet joint was scraped off to ensure gross total intralesional resection.

## Pathology

At low power, the lesion was paucicellular with a prominent myxoid background. At high power, cytologically bland fibroblasts were seen suspended within the myxoid matrix among scattered eosinophilic collagen fibers. The lesional cells displayed reactivity with vimentin, CD34 (focal), and S-100 protein.

## Discussion

Although very infrequently reported in the neck muscles, myxomas have been known since Rudolf Virchow coined the term in the eighteenth century to describe a benign tumor resembling mucinous umbilical cord tissue [1,4]. These tumors may occur in various locations. Intramuscular myxomas were described as a distinct subtype in 1965 by Enzinger [5] and comprised only 17% of all soft-tissue myxomas in that series.

The majority of IMs present in elderly female patients [3,5]. They have been reported in the neck region in children as young as 22 months. The incidence follows a bimodal distribution, peaking in the first to second decades and again from the fifth to seventh decades. Most IMs arise in the extremities, particularly the thigh muscles [6]. Intramuscular myxomas rarely occur in the neck, with only four cases previously reported in the cervical paraspinal muscles [1,4,7,8].

Intramuscular myxomas typically present as slow-growing painless masses that may produce symptoms through compression of surrounding structures [1,7,8]. In pediatric patients, presentation is often within a year, whereas adults tend to present after several years. Only approximately 20% are painful [5].

In the present case, pain evolved over more than 2 years with progressive enlargement of a posterior cervical mass. Although this presentation was consistent with previously reported neck IMs, the tumor was infiltrative, resulting in erosion of the bony laminae, epidural extension, and spinal cord compression. These features are atypical and appear unique to this case.

Imaging findings of IMs are nonspecific and overlap with other fluid-filled soft-tissue masses. Radiologic differential diagnoses include cystic hygroma, cystic teratoma, lipoma, hematoma, and abscess [4]. Histologic differential diagnoses include juxtaarticular myxoma, myxofibrosarcoma, and low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma. Juxtaarticular myxomas are more fibroblastic and cystic and less homogeneous, whereas sarcomas demonstrate pleomorphism and cytologic atypia [6].

Grossly, myxomas are typically gray-white depending on collagen and myxoid content. Although usually circumscribed, microscopic infiltration into surrounding soft tissues is common. They consist of lobules of myxoid matrix rich in glycosaminoglycans and hyaluronic acid, with scattered bland fibroblasts and variable collagen fibers. Features of malignancy are consistently absent. Although typically vimentin-positive and negative for desmin, S-100 protein, and CD34, focal S-100 protein and CD34 positivity was observed in this case, of unclear significance [3,6].

Surgical excision is the treatment of choice for IMs. Needle or open biopsy should be considered if malignancy is suspected. Although metastasis or malignant transformation has not been reported, recurrence may occur after incomplete excision or enucleation [9,10]. Complete resection with normal tissue margins is therefore recommended. In this case, gross total intralesional resection was achieved, with no evidence of recurrence at 1.5-year follow-up.

## Conclusion

Intramuscular myxomas are infrequent in the neck. Although typically benign, this case demonstrates that IMs can be infiltrative. Given their proximity to critical structures such as the spinal cord, timely intervention for cervical paraspinal IMs is warranted to minimize neurologic complications. Complete resection minimizes the risk of recurrence and the need for further surgical intervention.
