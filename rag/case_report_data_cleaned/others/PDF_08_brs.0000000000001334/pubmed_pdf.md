# Magnetic Resonance Imaging of the Codman Microsensor Transducer Used for Intraspinal Pressure Monitoring

## Abstract
Study Design: Laboratory and human study. Objective: To test the Codman Microsensor Transducer (CMT) in a cervical gel phantom and during intraspinal pressure monitoring in a patient with spinal cord injury. Background: We recently introduced intraspinal pressure (ISP) monitoring using the CMT to guide management of traumatic spinal cord injury (TSCI), analogous to intracranial pressure monitoring in traumatic brain injury. It is unclear whether MRI is safe with the intraspinal CMT in situ. Methods: We measured CMT heating in a gel phantom under various configurations using a 3 T MRI with body transmit and spine array receive coils. We then inserted a CMT subdurally at the injury site in a patient with TSCI and performed MRI at 1.5 T. Results: In the phantom, heating up to 5°C occurred when the transducer wire ran straight through the magnet bore; coiling the wire and routing it away from the bore abolished heating. In the patient, the wire was coiled and passed away from the bore; good‑quality T1/T2 images were obtained without neurological deterioration, and the transducer remained functional after MRI. Conclusion: These data suggest the CMT is MR‑conditional when used in the specified spinal configuration. Larger clinical series are needed to confirm safety.

Keywords: heating, human, ISP, magnetic, MRI, paralysis, radiofrequency, safety, spinal cord injury, spinal cord perfusion pressure, transducer.

## Introduction
The Injured Spinal Cord Pressure Evaluation (ISCoPE) study was initiated to develop ISP monitoring to guide management of severe TSCI, analogous to intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring in traumatic brain injury [1]. We monitor ISP and arterial blood pressure to compute spinal cord perfusion pressure (SCPP = mean arterial pressure (MAP) − ISP) and a spinal pressure reactivity index at the injury site. Experimental and clinical data show that after TSCI the cord swells at the injury level, causing raised ISP, reduced SCPP, and impaired pressure reactivity; increasing SCPP improves motor outcomes assessed by motor‑evoked responses and limb motor scores [2]. Surgical decompression with laminectomy and expansion duroplasty reduces ISP, increases SCPP, and improves pressure reactivity more effectively than laminectomy alone [3].

MRI is invaluable in TSCI pre‑ and postoperatively to define edema extent, detect hematoma, and assess decompression effectiveness. Because ISP monitoring uses the CMT, it is necessary to establish MRI safety of the device in a spinal configuration.

Prior work assessed the CMT for ICP monitoring in MRI, evaluating static magnetic field effects, gradient switching, and radiofrequency (RF) exposure [4,5]. RF‑induced resonance in the CMT lead produced significant heating, which was reduced by coiling the wire. The device has been termed MR‑conditional when used under specified conditions, consistent with ASTM terminology distinguishing MR‑safe and MR‑conditional devices. Spinal use differs from cranial use because: (1) potential injury is to eloquent spinal cord; (2) greater length of uncoiled wire may be exposed to RF; and (3) the cranial access bolt (a source of artifact) is not used. We therefore studied RF heating with a body transmit and spine receive coil setup applicable to spine imaging, determined a configuration that abolishes heating, and reproduced the setup in a human TSCI subject.

## Methods

### CMT/MRI Setup
The CMT system (Depuy Synthes, Leeds, UK) comprises a pressure probe tip, lead, connector, and a cable to a pressure monitor positioned outside the 5‑gauss line of the MRI unit. Phantom imaging used a 3 T Siemens Magnetom Verio (body transmit, spine array receive). Patient imaging used a 1.5 T Philips Intera system, which deposits approximately fourfold less RF energy per kilogram than 3 T under comparable conditions.

### CMT Setup in Gel Phantom
A spinal cord phantom was constructed from a 15 mL centrifuge tube filled with agar gel. Temperature was measured with a Luxtron 3100 fluoroptic thermometer using four fiberoptic thermocouples (FT1–FT4): FT1 at the CMT tip, FT2 at 1 cm, FT3 at 2 cm from the tip along the lead, and FT4 outside the phantom (ambient). The CMT lead with sensors was inserted longitudinally to the gel’s midline.

### CMT Testing in Gel Phantom
After equilibration to ambient temperature, FT1–FT4 were recorded every 2 s during a FLAIR sequence, starting 10 s before and ending 10 s after imaging. FLAIR was chosen for higher RF deposition and switching rates versus typical T1/T2 sequences. Parameters: field of view (FOV) 179–224 mm; resolution 0.7 × 0.7 × 4 mm; matrix 256 × 320; TR 7 s; TE 96 ms; turbo factor 16. The sequence was repeated on two days.

### Protocols
We varied: distance d from bore center (0, 10, 20, 30 cm, measured radially parallel to the floor); wire path (away from the bore vs through the bore); wire coiling; and connection to the monitoring box.

- Protocol i: Wire uncoiled, routed away from the magnet bore, not connected; d = 0, 10, 20, 30 cm.
- Protocol ii: Wire uncoiled, routed through the magnet bore, not connected; d = 0, 10, 20, 30 cm.
- Protocol iii: Wire coiled into 5 cm diameter alternating (non‑inductive) loops, configured as the condition producing the largest temperature rise (protocol ii at d = 30 cm), not connected.
- Protocol iv: Wire coiled in the configuration producing no temperature rise (d = 0 cm, routed away from bore), tested disconnected and connected to the monitoring box.

### Postoperative TSCI Patient Imaging
Under approval from the institutional research ethics board as part of ISCoPE, we implanted the CMT subdurally at the injury level during surgery as previously described [2]. Briefly, after laminectomy and fixation, the dura was punctured distal to the injury with a 21‑gauge needle bent to 90°, and the CMT was advanced so the tip lay at maximal swelling between cord and dura. MRI was performed on postoperative day 6 at 1.5 T using a body transmit/receive coil per manufacturer guidance. The CMT lead was coiled into 5 cm loops at the skin exit site, routed away from the bore near midline without touching patient or bore; the monitoring box was disconnected during imaging.

## Results

### CMT Testing in Gel
Ambient temperature was 20.2°C ± 0.0 (day 1) and 20.1°C ± 0.0 (day 2), with identical phantom readings across days. Tip temperature (FT1) fluctuated at ~0.1 Hz, with rises aligned to RF pulses and partial cooling between pulses. Heating localized to the tip in all configurations. Temperature rose rapidly after sequence start and decayed rapidly after cessation.

- Protocol i (uncoiled, away from bore, not connected): maximal rise 0.8°C at d = 30 cm.
- Protocol ii (uncoiled, through bore, not connected): maximal rises 2.4°C at d = 0 cm and 5.2°C at d = 30 cm; this was the worst‑case condition.
- Protocol iii (coiled, worst‑case geometry): coiling abolished detectable heating.
- Protocol iv (coiled, away from bore, d = 0 cm): no detectable heating whether disconnected or connected to the monitoring box.

### Patient
A 65‑year‑old man with ASIA grade C cervical TSCI after a road traffic accident had CT/MRI showing C4/5 disc disruption with ligamentous injury and cord edema, without fracture. He underwent anterior C4/5 discectomy with cage and plate fixation and posterior C4–C5 lateral mass screws/rods with C4–C5 laminectomies. The CMT was inserted subdurally at C4/5 and tunneled 3 cm subcutaneously.

### Postoperative Imaging in TSCI Patient
MRI on postoperative day 6 used sagittal FOV 275 mm, slice thickness 4 mm; TE 120 ms/TR 3500 ms and TE 10 ms/TR 400 ms; axial FOV 225 mm, slice thickness 4 mm; TE 80 ms/TR 3078 ms. The CMT lead was coiled at the exit site, routed away from the bore, and kept off the skin with gauze; the monitoring box was disconnected.

T2 images showed the cord clearly despite intradural CMT and anterior/posterior titanium instrumentation. The subcutaneous course of the lead and the intradural tip were visible. There was no change in neurological status after MRI. ISP and SCPP signals before and after imaging were comparable; CMT function was unaffected.

## Discussion
MRI of the cervical spinal cord with an intradural CMT for ISP monitoring is feasible when the lead is coiled and routed away from the bore. The International Commission on Non‑Ionizing Radiation Protection recommends limiting clinical tissue temperature rise to 0.5°C; our configuration produced no detectable heating at 3 T in the phantom and enabled safe 1.5 T clinical imaging with preserved device function and stable neurology.

The CMT uses a silicon strain gauge in a titanium housing (tip diameter 0.7 mm) connected by copper wires in nylon. Static magnetic field effects (torque/translation) are negligible for the CMT up to 3 T; the connector is weakly ferromagnetic with force less than gravity and can be secured to prevent movement [5]. RF fields are the principal source of device heating. Standing RF waves along conductive leads induce currents with maximal density at the tip, producing localized heating. Severe heating has been reported for straight, taped leads during cranial monitoring, including tissue injury and lead damage, whereas coiling abolishes heating [8].

Our phantom results replicate prior cranial findings: worst heating occurs with a straight path through the bore and increases with distance from bore center; coiling eliminates the rise, likely by (1) forming an inductive element that impedes induced current and (2) shortening the effective resonant length, shifting resonance outside the RF frequency.

Clinically, spine MRI poses challenges (field inhomogeneity, small cord cross‑section, physiological motion, metallic fixation). In our case, titanium cervical constructs did not appreciably degrade cord imaging. Thoracic imaging may be more challenging due to longer/thicker constructs. While our single‑patient experience suggests safety, larger series are required to draw definitive conclusions about RF safety across field strengths, sequences, and implant geometries.

## Conclusion
The Codman Microsensor Transducer appears MR‑conditional in the cervical spine configuration tested. Coiling the lead and routing it away from the bore abolished phantom heating at 3 T, and clinical MRI at 1.5 T yielded good‑quality images without neurological deterioration or device malfunction.

## Key Points
- Intraspinal pressure monitoring can guide acute TSCI management by optimizing SCPP.
- RF‑induced heating of the CMT is configuration‑dependent; a straight, through‑bore path produces the greatest heating in phantom.
- Coiling the lead into non‑inductive loops and routing it away from the magnet bore abolished heating in phantom tests.
- Using the coiled/away‑from‑bore configuration enabled safe, good‑quality cervical spine MRI at 1.5 T in a TSCI patient.
- The CMT remained functional after MRI, and the patient had no neurological deterioration.
