# A New Technique: Endoscopic Transmass Odontoidotomy

## Abstract

Basilar invagination (BI) is characterized by odontoid protrusion causing brainstem compression, often requiring anterior decompression combined with posterior stabilization. Traditional methods for odontoid resection include transoral, transnasal, and endonasal approaches; however, these techniques are associated with significant risks and limited surgical exposure due to anatomical constraints. These limitations have prompted spine surgeons to seek alternative strategies.

This report presents a case of BI managed using an endoscopic posterolateral transmass odontoidotomy, representing an innovative surgical approach. We describe our experience with partial removal of the odontoid via a posterolateral endoscopic technique, thereby avoiding additional anterior approaches and their associated complications.

A 16-year-old male presented with imbalance and dysphagia. Neurological examination revealed upper extremity weakness, ataxic gait, and swallowing difficulty. Following the diagnosis of BI, posterior occipitocervical fusion was performed. Six months later, the patient returned with worsening symptoms. During revision surgery, the odontoid body was resected via a posterolateral transmass endoscopic approach, preserving the odontoid tip.

Postoperative computed tomography demonstrated approximation of the odontoid tip to the base and restoration of the subarachnoid space. One-year follow-up imaging showed complete migration of the odontoid tip to the base of C2 with effective brainstem decompression.

Our findings indicate that partial or complete odontoid resection via a posterolateral approach is feasible using endoscopic techniques. Endoscopic posterolateral transmass odontoidotomy may represent a viable alternative for patients requiring partial or total odontoidectomy.

## Introduction

Basilar invagination (BI) is associated with anomalies of the craniovertebral junction and cervical spine and poses a significant risk due to compression of the brainstem and upper cervical spinal cord. This compression results from upward migration of the odontoid process, narrowing the spinal canal and foramen magnum. The primary goals of treatment are decompression of neural structures and correction of cervical alignment. The invaginated and posteriorly displaced odontoid process is the main contributor to neurological injury, and its removal may exacerbate preexisting instability [1–2].

Standard surgical strategies include anterior transoral or transnasal odontoidectomy and posterior suboccipital craniectomy with craniocervical fusion, often followed by cervical laminectomy. Resection of the migrated odontoid is commonly performed via an endoscopic endonasal approach [3]. However, complications such as vertebral artery injury, infection, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, and swallowing or respiratory dysfunction limit the widespread use of anterior approaches [4].

The endoscopic endonasal approach has gained popularity due to reduced morbidity [5], yet its narrow working corridor restricts exposure and surgical maneuverability. To overcome these limitations, posterior approaches such as the transaxis technique have been introduced, allowing safe single-stage surgery [6]. Inspired by cadaveric studies demonstrating posterior access to C1–2 lesions via partial lateral mass resection [7], we hypothesized that a posterior endoscopic transmass approach could be advantageous for odontoid resection.

In this technical case report, we describe a patient with BI who developed symptom recurrence following posterior craniocervical fusion. Using a posterolateral endoscopic transmass approach, we successfully removed the odontoid body. Follow-up imaging demonstrated inferior migration of the remaining odontoid tip under CSF pulsation, relieving compression and restoring CSF flow. This report may represent an initial step toward establishing a novel posterolateral endoscopic transmass odontoidotomy technique.

## Case Report

Under general anesthesia, the patient was intubated in the supine position, after which the head was secured in a Mayfield skull clamp and the patient repositioned prone. The shoulders were secured to facilitate exposure. A midline incision from the prior surgery was reopened, extending from the inion to the C6 spinous process. Paravertebral muscles and scar tissue were dissected subperiosteally to remove the occipital plate and existing C2 pedicle and C3–5 lateral mass screws.

Under microscopic visualization, the C1–2 facet joints were exposed. The C2 nerve roots were sacrificed after bipolar cauterization. A 2-mm diamond burr was used to shave the superomedial aspect of the bilateral C1–2 facet joints, creating a working corridor for endoscopic access to the odontoid.

At the C2–3 intervertebral disc level, approximately 5 cm lateral to the midline, bilateral 7-mm skin incisions were made for endoscopic entry. Using an interlaminar endoscopic system, the endoscope was first introduced on the right side, targeting the medial aspect of the C1–2 facet joint with a medial angle of 40° and a cranial angle of 15–20°. Due to the patient’s shoulder anatomy, scoliosis, and Klippel–Feil syndrome, cranial angulation was limited to 20°. Following the trajectory of the C2 pedicle and the sacrificed nerve root, the endoscope reached the base of the odontoid without dural contact.

Using a high-speed drill with a diamond burr and an endoscopic Kerrison rongeur, the inferior half of the odontoid body was removed until the anterior longitudinal ligament was encountered. Limited cranial angulation prevented access to the odontoid tip, which was therefore preserved. The same procedure was performed on the contralateral side.

Continuous irrigation provided effective hemostasis. To restore stability, lateral mass screws were revised and autologous bone graft was applied. A subfascial drain was placed, and the wound was closed in layers. The patient was discharged without complications, demonstrating near-complete resolution of symptoms. Postoperative CT and MRI confirmed decompression of the brainstem and restoration of CSF flow.

## Discussion

Surgical management of C1–2 pathology is challenging due to proximity to the vertebral arteries, foramen magnum, and spinal cord, as well as the risk of iatrogenic instability. The odontoid process, in particular, is difficult to access. Although transoral and transnasal odontoidectomy techniques are well established, their anterior trajectory is associated with limited exposure and significant morbidity [3].

The endoscopic endonasal approach offers improved visualization but remains constrained by a narrow working corridor and often necessitates additional posterior fusion. Motivated by cadaveric evidence of posterior access to C1–2 lesions [7], we employed a novel endoscopic transmass approach for odontoid removal.

In this patient, severe kyphoscoliosis and Klippel–Feil syndrome limited cranial angulation, precluding complete odontoid tip removal. We hypothesized that CSF pulsations would promote inferior migration of the residual tip after removal of its base and loosening of the transverse ligament. Follow-up imaging confirmed this mechanism, resulting in effective decompression.

The posteromedial C1–2 facet corridor provided direct access to the odontoid without dural violation and avoided the venous plexus typically encountered around the C2 nerve root. However, this approach requires sacrifice of the C2 nerve root and may not be suitable for all patients. In individuals with normal cervical and shoulder anatomy, greater angulation may allow complete odontoidectomy. Although partial resection proved effective in this young patient, older individuals with degenerative changes may not exhibit similar odontoid mobility.

## Limitations

This report reflects a single-patient experience. Additionally, the patient underwent prior open and microscopic surgeries before the endoscopic procedure, limiting assessment of a purely endoscopic approach. Anatomical constraints prevented complete odontoid removal, although odontoidotomy was sufficient for decompression. Larger studies involving patients with favorable anatomy and exclusively endoscopic techniques are required to validate this approach.

## Conclusion

Endoscopic transmass odontoidotomy offers a promising alternative to anterior approaches for alleviating brainstem compression in basilar invagination. In this case, it demonstrated safety and effectiveness with favorable clinical and radiological outcomes. Larger case series are necessary to further evaluate the efficacy and safety of this technique.
