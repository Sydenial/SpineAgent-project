# A rare cause of recalcitrant coccydynia: benign dermoid cyst masquerading as coccygeal pain

## Abstract

**Purpose**  
Coccydynia is a common entity in orthopedic practice, and various etiologies have been described for it. However, benign dermoid cyst causing coccydynia has not yet been reported.

**Methods**  
A 20-year-old male presented with typical symptoms of coccydynia recalcitrant to conservative treatment for 2 years. Since pain interfered with his daily activities, magnetic resonance imaging was performed, which showed a circumscribed precoccygeal cystic lesion.

**Results**  
The patient underwent coccygectomy along with cyst excision. Histological examination revealed features of benign dermoid cyst. After surgery, the patient had excellent relief of his symptoms.

**Conclusion**  
This case report highlights that the treating surgeon should be aware of benign dermoid cyst as one of the treatable but rare causes of intractable coccydynia, and MRI should be performed in patients with persistent coccygeal pain.

## Introduction

Coccydynia is defined as pain in and around the coccyx region. It is mainly post-traumatic and idiopathic [1–3]. Various other rare causes of coccydynia have been described in the form of individual case reports and include bone tumors [4,5], glomus tumor [6], perineural cysts [7], infection [8], and neural tumors [9,10]. Usually, the diagnosis is made by typical history and clinical examination, and radiological evaluation is performed only in patients with chronic and persistent symptoms. There are no standard indications described in the literature for performing an MRI scan for patients with coccydynia. Most patients with coccydynia have good relief of symptoms with conservative treatment. Surgical excision is advised in patients with persistent pain.

## Case Report

A 20-year-old college student presented with unbearable pain in the gluteal cleft region near the coccyx for the past 2 years. Pain had started insidiously without any antecedent trauma. It was initially mild but became unbearable in the last 5 months. The pain was aggravated by sitting and was not associated with radicular, neurological, or constitutional symptoms. There was no history of difficulty in defecation or micturition.

The patient’s body mass index was 22.55 (weight 62 kg, height 166 cm). Local examination revealed normal overlying skin with significant tenderness over the coccyx. Radiograph of the coccyx showed normal coccygeal morphology. He was treated initially with analgesics and a ring pillow and was advised to avoid direct pressure over the coccyx. However, he did not respond to conservative treatment given for 2 months.

An MRI scan of the coccyx was performed, which showed a normal coccyx but revealed a well-circumscribed cystic lesion measuring $1.5 \times 1.8$ cm situated anterior to the coccyx. The cyst was isointense on T1-weighted images, hypointense on T2-weighted images, and hyperintense on STIR images. Since it was difficult to determine the source of pain clinically (either the cyst or the coccyx), the patient was treated with coccygectomy and cyst excision under general anesthesia.

Through a posterior midline approach, subperiosteal dissection was performed, and the coccyx was removed along with meticulous en bloc resection of the cyst. The anterior cyst wall was situated just posterior to the rectum. The cyst was white in appearance and contained cheesy material. Histopathological examination showed an epidermal-lined cyst wall with keratin contents, characteristic of a benign dermoid cyst. The patient’s symptoms resolved postoperatively, and he remained asymptomatic at 12-month follow-up.

## Discussion

The term coccydynia was coined by Simpson in 1861 for painful coccyx [11]. Coccydynia is a common condition seen in orthopedic practice. Even though it is often self-limiting, it can be very debilitating and affect a patient’s quality of life. The most common cause of coccydynia is trauma [3]. Maigne et al. [3] suggested that only an atraumatic event occurring within 1 month of onset is significant in increasing the risk of instability and subsequent coccydynia.

Idiopathic coccydynia is considered a diagnosis of exclusion and has been described in the absence of any obvious pathological changes involving the coccyx [12]. In idiopathic cases, pain is thought to result from spasticity of the pelvic floor musculature. Morphological abnormalities of the coccyx such as spicule formation, increased intercoccygeal angle [1,2], and retroversion [13] are other described causes. Obesity is three times more common in patients with coccydynia than in the normal population and decreases pelvic rotation while sitting, increasing the risk of coccygeal subluxation [14].

Although rare, various pathological conditions can present as coccydynia, including tumors of the sacrum or coccyx [4,5], glomus tumors [6], perineural cysts [7], infection [8], and intradural tumors such as schwannoma or ependymoma [9,10]. Few cases similar to the present one have been reported. Precoccygeal epidermal inclusion cysts and retrorectal dermoid cysts have been described, often discovered incidentally [15,16].

Physical examination should include inspection for pilonidal sinus or discharge. Palpation may reveal localized tenderness, swelling, or a mass. Imaging studies are invaluable in evaluation. Standard anteroposterior and lateral radiographs may reveal destructive or lytic changes in cases of tumor or infection. Dynamic radiographs and MRI have been recommended for acute coccydynia refractory to conservative treatment or symptoms lasting more than 2 months [14]. MRI may reveal inflammatory, infective, or tumor pathology.

In the present case, MRI was performed due to persistent pain and normal radiographs, revealing a pericoccygeal cystic lesion. The cyst was excised completely along with the coccyx, resulting in excellent pain relief. Histopathology confirmed a benign dermoid cyst. Dermoids are ectoderm-lined inclusion cysts containing sebaceous glands, sweat glands, and squamous epithelium. They arise from trapped ectodermal elements or failure of separation from the neural tube and are usually benign, slowly growing cystic masses [17]. Although often asymptomatic, surgical excision is indicated in symptomatic, recalcitrant cases. In the absence of MRI, local steroid injection could have been potentially dangerous in this patient.

## Conclusion

Coccydynia is most commonly post-traumatic or idiopathic and usually responds well to conservative treatment. This report highlights the importance of performing MRI in cases of coccydynia not responding to prolonged conservative management. A pericoccygeal dermoid cyst, although very rare, should be considered as a possible pathological cause of intractable coccydynia.
