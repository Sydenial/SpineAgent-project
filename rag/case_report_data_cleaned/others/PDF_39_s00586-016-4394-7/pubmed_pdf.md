# Esophageal Perforation After Anterior Cervical Surgery: A Review of the Literature for Over Half a Century with a Demonstrative Case and a Proposed Novel Algorithm

## Abstract

**Purpose**: To review relevant data for the management of esophageal perforation after anterior cervical surgery.  
**Methods**: A case of delayed esophageal perforation after anterior cervical surgery has been presented, and the relevant literature between 1958 and 2014 was reviewed. A total of 57 papers regarding esophageal perforation following anterior cervical surgery were found and utilized in this review.  
**Results**: The treatment options for esophageal perforation after anterior cervical surgery were discussed, and a novel management algorithm was proposed.  
**Conclusion**: Following anterior cervical surgery, patients should be closely followed up in the postoperative period for risk of esophageal perforation. Development of symptoms like dysphagia, pneumonia, fever, odynophagia, hoarseness, weight loss, and breathing difficulty in patients with a history of previous anterior cervical surgery should alert us to a possible esophageal injury. Review of the literature revealed that conservative treatment is advocated for early and small esophageal perforations. Surgical treatment may be considered for large esophageal defects.

**Keywords**: Algorithms, Esophageal perforation, Anterior cervical surgery, Abscess, Spinal fusion

## Introduction

The anterior approach to cervical spine treatment for spinal diseases has been well established and considered a standard procedure, first introduced by Cloward and Smith-Robinson in 1958 [1, 2]. However, anterior cervical surgery is not without complications [3-5]. One serious complication of this operation is esophageal perforation [4, 6, 7].

Esophageal perforation after anterior cervical spine surgery is uncommon but well-recognized and potentially life-threatening. The incidence of esophageal injuries ranges from 0.3% to 4% [8, 9]. Most perforations tend to appear preoperatively, related to direct iatrogenic injury, and are diagnosed in the early postoperative period [5, 9, 10]. Delayed perforations usually occur due to chronic compression of prominent hardware [8, 10, 11]. The most common symptoms after esophageal perforation are dysphagia, pneumonia, fever, odynophagia, hoarseness, and breathing difficulty, while less common ones include chest pain, weight loss, and gastrointestinal bleeding [4, 8, 9, 12]. Asymptomatic esophageal perforations have also been reported [5]. Early diagnosis and appropriate treatment of esophageal perforations are very important to prevent morbidity and mortality.

There are various treatment strategies for esophageal perforation, such as conservative treatment or surgical approaches. To date, there is no definitive treatment algorithm for esophageal perforation after anterior cervical surgery. We present a case of delayed esophageal perforation after anterior cervical surgery and review the English literature between 1958 and 2014, discussing the treatment options for esophageal perforation after anterior cervical surgery. We propose a management algorithm for esophageal perforation after anterior cervical surgery.

## Materials and Methods

In this paper, the English literature was reviewed for esophageal perforation related to anterior cervical surgery. A detailed electronic search was carried out using the medical subject headings (MeSH) term "esophageal perforation" in the MEDLINE and PubMed databases for studies published from January 1958 to December 2014. Fifty-seven papers were found related to esophageal perforation following anterior cervical surgery. The related papers were evaluated based on the type of anterior cervical surgery before esophageal perforation, timing of diagnosis, size of perforation, treatment options (conservative or surgical), duration, and results of treatment. A table was prepared summarizing the reviewed cases (Table 1) [4, 5, 7-11, 13-47]. Primary suturing of the esophageal defect with or without flap was considered surgical treatment, and other surgical and endoscopic interventions like drainage of abscesses, esophageal stent application were considered conservative surgery and are not mentioned in the table. A total of 43 papers, including our case, were depicted in the table. Fifteen papers regarding esophageal perforation after anterior cervical surgery were excluded from the table due to a lack of detailed explanation of the treatment process, patient information, or treatment results.

## Case

A 40-year-old female patient had undergone surgery 12 years ago for a C6 vertebral fracture due to a traffic accident. Following anterior surgical decompression with C6 corpectomy, stabilization and fusion of the C5–7 segment was achieved with an autogenous bone graft and anterior plate system.

The patient presented with complaints of dysphagia and weight loss continuing for 3 months. Neurological examination revealed pain and hypoesthesia related to the right C5 root dermatome. On X-ray imaging, there was a plate and screw malposition, and a solid fusion was visible between the C5 and C7 vertebral segments. On cervical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), an adjacent level disc herniation at C4–5 level was detected. On esophagogram with barium, anterior plate contact with the esophagus was observed. CT showed osteophytic changes between C5 and C7, and fusion between C5 and C7 vertebrae was determined. The endoscopic examination confirmed the diagnosis of a 2-cm esophageal perforation caused by the cervical plate, and the patient was operated upon based on these findings.

Plate invasion into the esophagus was confirmed during the operation, and the anterior plate was removed. The perforated part of the esophagus was primarily sutured in collaboration with thoracic surgeons. After debriding the edges of the perforation, the esophageal tear was sutured with absorbable interrupted stitches. The suturing was done in a tension-free way to avoid secondary stricture of the esophagus. No late stricture developed, and no need for endoscopic dilatation arose after the suturing of the esophagus. A soft wound drain was placed between the esophagus and the vertebrae for drainage of any esophageal leakage. Additional C4–5 discectomy and peek cage fusion were performed due to adjacent level disease, followed by posterior stabilization. In the postoperative period, the patient was started on nasogastric (NG) feeding. Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotic treatment consisting of metronidazole, teicoplanin, and meropenem was administered for 6 weeks. On the fifth postoperative day, extensive drainage from the incision site was observed. Although the patient did not exhibit fever or signs of sepsis, conservative treatment was continued for 4 weeks with a drainage catheter, NG feeding, and broad-spectrum antibiotics. The leakage persisted, and an esophageal fistula was finally discovered at the end of the fourth week after several endoscopic examinations. The esophageal fistula was repaired with an endoscopic plastic stent application by ENT surgeons. Conservative treatments with NG feeding and broad-spectrum antibiotics were also continued. After the first postoperative day, the spit discharge from the fistula draining to the skin ceased. The wound drain was removed, and the patient was started on oral feeding 1 week after endoscopic stenting. The NG tube was removed 2 weeks after stenting. After 4 weeks, the esophageal stent was removed, and spontaneous recovery was confirmed with endoscopic examination. The patient was discharged 6 weeks after the endoscopic intervention without dysphagia and with recovery of neurological symptoms. As of 1 year follow-up, the patient was in good condition and symptom-free, requiring no further endoscopic follow-ups.

## Discussion

Esophageal perforations were classified as "early" if diagnosed within 1 month after the operation and "late" if diagnosed after the first month. Early esophageal perforation after anterior cervical surgery is mostly caused iatrogenically due to inappropriate placement or dislodgement of sharp-toothed retractor blades in the esophagus. Late esophageal perforation is mostly caused by chronic compression or contact, resulting in necrosis, abscess formation due to graft dislodgement or screw migration, with or without plate failure. Reusing the same hardware for any revisional surgery is not recommended, as it may lead to esophageal perforation due to easier plate or screw extrusion. Replacement of the hardware is suggested to reinforce the fixators and avoid extrusion. Dislodgement of a bone graft without the use of a cervical plate may increase the risk of esophageal injury. Even without dislodgement, chronic pressure necrosis or micro-trauma from metallic hardware can also lead to esophageal perforation.

Diagnosis of esophageal complications is made by radiological studies, endoscopic examination, and clinical evaluation. Radiological signs such as air in the anterior cervical area, subcutaneous emphysema, or widening of the retropharyngo-esophageal space should raise suspicion for esophageal injury. A contrast-enhanced esophagogram may show perforation, but it has a false-negative rate of 10%. Cervical CT and MRI, with contrast, can show dislodged hardware and esophageal morphology. Endoscopy is essential for diagnosis and assessment of the perforation's size.

## Conclusion

Successful management of esophageal perforations after anterior cervical surgery depends on early diagnosis and treatment. Removal of instrumentation, primary suturing of the perforated area with or without a muscle flap, bypassing the esophagus with either NG feeding or gastrostomy/jejunostomy, and wide-spectrum antibiotic therapy are the essential first-step treatment options for this condition.
