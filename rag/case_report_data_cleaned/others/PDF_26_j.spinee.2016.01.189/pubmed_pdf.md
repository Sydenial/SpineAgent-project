# Scissors Stab Wound to the Cervical Spinal Cord at the Craniocervical Junction

## Abstract

### Background Context
Stab wounds resulting in spinal cord injury at the craniocervical junction are rare. A scissors stab wound to the cervical spinal cord has only been reported once in the literature.

### Purpose
To report a case of Brown-Séquard-plus syndrome in an 8-year-old boy secondary to a scissors stab wound at the craniocervical junction.

### Study Design
Case report and review of the literature.

### Case Report
An 8-year-old boy was accidentally stabbed in the neck by scissors thrown as a dart by his little brother while playing. He presented to the emergency department with a laceration at the craniocervical junction. The patient developed Brown-Séquard-plus syndrome on the left side of his body. MRI revealed a laceration of the spinal cord at the craniocervical junction with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage. Treatment included careful cleansing, sutures to prevent CSF leakage, and rehabilitation therapy. After 10 months, the patient showed complete recovery.

## Introduction

Scissors stab wounds resulting in spinal cord injury at the craniocervical junction are rare. There is only one large review of such injuries published from Cape Town by Peacock et al. Most of the remaining data have come from case reports. According to the review, 5% of spinal cord injuries have been reported due to sharp injuries, with 4.2% of these resulting from stabbings.

Scissors stab wounds have only been reported once before by Karadag et al., a case of penetrating cervical spinal trauma with an ipsilateral vertebral artery injury. We report a unique case of an 8-year-old boy with a scissors stab wound at the craniocervical junction that resulted in Brown-Séquard-plus syndrome.

## Case Report

### History and Examination
An 8-year-old boy was accidentally stabbed in the neck by scissors, which were thrown as a dart by his little brother. The boy immediately fell to the ground and could not stand up. He was transferred to our hospital for further treatment. At the time of admission, the blood pressure was 110/65 mmHg, the heart rate was 96 beats/min, the respiratory rate was 20/min, and arterial oxygen saturation was 100%. The physical examination revealed two sutured skin wounds in the occipitocervical region with minor seeping of blood and exudate. Neurological examination showed motor weakness in the left upper and lower extremities, sensory disturbances, and impaired pain and temperature perception on the right side.

### Imaging
T2-weighted MRI showed a laceration of the spinal cord at the craniocervical junction with cerebrospinal fluid leakage and fluid collection in the paravertebral region at the level of C2.

### Management
The patient was managed conservatively. Wounds were carefully cleansed and sutured to prevent CSF leakage. He was treated with glucocorticosteroids, prophylactic antibiotics, anti-dehydration therapy, and neurotrophic drugs. Rehabilitation therapy was started two days later.

### Post-operative Course
On the 4th day of admission, the muscle strength of the left upper and lower limbs showed improvement. By the 27th post-injury day, sensory and motor function had normalized. Upon discharge, the patient had a stable gait, with muscle strength in the left limbs improving to grade 5/5 and bladder function restored. Follow-up at 8 months post-injury showed complete recovery.

## Discussion

Stab wounds to the spinal cord have rarely been reported. Cases of stab wounds caused by various sharp objects have been documented. Scissors stab wounds to the spinal cord have only been reported once by Karadag et al. The stabbing instrument usually slides along the undersurface of the occipital squama, being deflected by the bone and mastoid process into the atlanto-occipital or atlanto-axial interspace. In our case, the scissors entered obliquely through the atlanto-occipital interspace and partially severed the spinal cord. Unlike other stabbing instruments, scissors have both a sharp and blunt side, with the sharp side lacerating the spinal cord and the blunt side causing displacement. MRI indicated that more than half of the cervical cord tract was preserved.

We suggest that spreading the scissors blades can create resistance to prevent deeper penetration. The mechanism of acute neurological deficits secondary to a penetrating stab injury to the spinal cord can be due to direct penetration of spinal cord neural elements or spinal cord infarction. The presence of Brown-Séquard-plus syndrome further suggests additional neurological findings involving the eyes, bowel, or bladder.

## Conclusion

Treatment of scissors stab wounds to the cervical spinal cord should be individualized based on the patient's history, examination, and imaging findings. Despite conservative management, complete recovery was achieved, attributed to the initial mild laceration of the spinal cord and ipsilateral spinal cord functional compensation.
