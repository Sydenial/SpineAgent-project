# Abdominal Aortic Penetration During Vertebroplasty Procedure

## Abstract

Vertebroplasty is a minimally invasive and widely performed procedure in elderly and high-risk patients, though it carries the risk of life-threatening complications, including aortic injury. This report describes a case of iatrogenic aortic penetration occurring during vertebroplasty. An 80-year-old female patient underwent vertebroplasty for vertebral fractures at Th12 and L1. Upon advancing the needle into the L1 vertebral body, arterial blood spurted, and fluoroscopic imaging revealed aortic penetration. After careful management, including blood pressure control and consultation with specialists, the patient was successfully treated conservatively. Follow-up imaging showed no complications, and the patient recovered without sequelae.

## Introduction

Vertebroplasty is a common procedure for treating vertebral fractures, particularly in elderly patients with osteoporosis. Despite its minimally invasive nature, complications, including aortic injury, can occur. This case report presents a rare occurrence of aortic penetration during vertebroplasty and discusses the treatment approach.

## Case Report

An 80-year-old female with vertebral fractures at Th12 and L1 due to osteoporosis was scheduled for percutaneous vertebroplasty under general anesthesia. After positioning the patient prone, two 14G bone access needles were inserted into the vertebral bodies at Th12 and L1. When the L1 needle's inner cylinder was removed, arterial blood spurted, and fluoroscopic imaging revealed that the needle had penetrated the posterior wall of the descending aorta. Immediate efforts to minimize bleeding included administering muscle relaxants and depressors, along with securing venous and arterial lines. The patient was stabilized, and a cardiothoracic surgeon was consulted.

Contrast medium injected through the needle revealed that a hematoma had formed around the aorta, pushing the aortic wall away from the needle. The vital signs remained stable, and the hematoma appeared to coagulate. After 55 minutes, the needle was withdrawn carefully, and the procedure on Th12 continued.

After aortic angiography showed no extravasation, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit. Postoperative imaging confirmed no further complications. The patient was extubated on day 1 and discharged on day 21 after an uneventful recovery. Follow-up for 2 years revealed no arterial complications.

## Discussion

Aortic injury during vertebroplasty is rare but can be severe. In this case, the needle penetrated the posterior wall of the aorta, leading to hematoma formation. Conservative management was deemed appropriate due to stable vital signs and the tamponade effect provided by the hematoma. Similar cases reported in the literature suggest that immediate removal of the instrument is not always recommended, as significant bleeding may occur. Managing the injury by holding the needle in place and controlling blood pressure helped reduce bleeding and facilitated the coagulation of the hematoma.

Given the patient’s age and osteoporotic condition, these were likely contributing factors to the injury. Frequent imaging and confirmation of needle placement are crucial, especially in patients with fragile bones. Conservative treatment is viable when vital signs remain stable, and the tamponade effect is effective in controlling bleeding.

## Conclusion

This case highlights a rare but significant complication of vertebroplasty. Conservative treatment can be an effective approach when the tamponade effect is sufficient and vital signs are stable. Early recognition and careful management are critical to minimizing the risks associated with iatrogenic aortic injury during spinal procedures.

## Key Points

- Aortic penetration by a needle during vertebroplasty is a rare but life-threatening complication.
- Conservative management with hypotension and stabilization of the needle can effectively control bleeding and prevent further complications.
- Immediate surgical intervention is not always necessary if vital signs are stable and a tamponade effect is observed.
