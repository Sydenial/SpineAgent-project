# Posttraumatic Cervical Nerve Root Avulsion: Direct and Indirect MR Myelography Findings

## Abstract

An 17-year-old, otherwise healthy man with a history of a motorcycle accident was evaluated. On physical examination, right-sided ulnar nerve injury findings were determined. A magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was obtained with intrathecal contrast-enhanced MR myelography sequences and a three-dimensional (3D) constructive interference in steady state (CISS) sequence, which depicted contrast passing through the right C8 nerve root on MR myelography images. At this level, the nerve root was not visualized on the 3D CISS sequence, and a pseudomeningocele appearance was shown. Additionally, axial and sagittal STIR images showed a retracted nerve root (Fig. 1).

## Introduction

The use of 3D CISS sequences provides excellent topographical data that aids in delineating the precise location of cervical nerve root avulsions. As recommended by several researchers, these sequences provide high-quality images and their diagnostic accuracy is comparable to that of CT myelography or conventional myelography. 3D sequences like CISS are especially useful due to superior spatial resolution and the ability to reconstruct the dataset in any specified plane. In conclusion, the 3D CISS sequence is helpful in the evaluation of cervical nerve root avulsions without the utilization of intrathecal contrast agents.

## Methods

A magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed using intrathecal contrast-enhanced MR myelography sequences and a 3D CISS sequence. The CISS images were used to observe the passage of contrast through the C8 nerve root, and 3D CISS sequences were used to evaluate the nerve root and pseudomeningocele appearance. Additional axial and sagittal STIR images were taken to further investigate the retracted nerve root.

## Results

The MRI images, including MR myelography, 3D CISS, and STIR sequences, revealed a retracted nerve root at the C8 level. A pseudomeningocele appearance was observed, and no nerve root was visualized on the 3D CISS sequence at that level. The findings suggested a cervical nerve root avulsion with associated pseudomeningocele formation.

## Discussion

3D CISS sequences are valuable in imaging traumatic cervical nerve root injuries and avulsions. These sequences offer high resolution and are able to provide detailed anatomical information, especially when traditional contrast-enhanced myelography or CT myelography may not be ideal or available. The CISS sequence is particularly useful in evaluating nerve root avulsions in the cervical spine, allowing for accurate depiction of nerve root discontinuity and associated pseudomeningocele formation.

## Conclusion

The 3D CISS sequence is a reliable imaging tool for evaluating cervical nerve root avulsions. Its ability to reconstruct data in any plane and provide detailed anatomical information makes it an important diagnostic tool in neuroimaging for cervical spine injuries.
