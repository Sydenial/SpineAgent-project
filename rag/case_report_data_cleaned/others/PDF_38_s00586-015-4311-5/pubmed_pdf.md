# Changes in multifidus and abdominal muscle size in response to microgravity: possible implications for low back pain research

## Abstract

Purpose: In microgravity, muscle atrophy occurs in the intrinsic muscles of the spine, with changes also observed in the abdominal muscles. Exercises are undertaken on the International Space Station and on Earth following space flight to remediate these effects. Similar effects have been seen on Earth in prolonged bed rest studies and in people with low back pain (LBP). The aim of this case report was to examine the effects of microgravity, exercise in microgravity, and post-flight rehabilitation on the size of the multifidus and antero-lateral abdominal muscles.  
Methods: Ultrasound imaging was used to assess size of the multifidus, transversus abdominis, and internal oblique muscles at four time points: pre-flight, and after daily rehabilitation on day one ($\mathbb{R}+1$), day 8 ($\mathbb{R}+8$), and day 14 ($\mathbb{R}+14$) after return to Earth (following 6 months in microgravity).  
Results: Exercises in microgravity maintained multifidus size at L2–L4, however, after spaceflight, size of the multifidus muscle at L5 was reduced, size of the internal oblique muscle was increased, and size of transversus abdominis was reduced. Rehabilitation post-space flight resulted in hypertrophy of the multifidus muscle to pre-mission size at the L5 vertebral level and restoration of antero-lateral abdominal muscle size.  
Conclusions: Exercise in space can prevent loss of spinal intrinsic muscle size. For the multifidus muscles, effectiveness varied at different levels of the spine. Post-mission rehabilitation targeting specific motor control restored muscle balance between the antero-lateral abdominal and multifidus muscles, similar to results from intervention trials for people with LBP. A limitation of the current investigation is that only one astronaut was studied; however, the microgravity model could be valuable as predictable effects on trunk muscles can be induced and interventions evaluated.  
Level of Evidence: Case series.

Keywords: Ultrasound imaging · Rehabilitation · Lumbar spine · Paraspinal muscles · Exercise therapy · Trunk muscles

## Background

While many lumbo-pelvic muscles contribute to the control and support of the lumbar region, key muscles identified in several studies include the lumbar multifidus and the antero-lateral abdominal muscles. The lumbar multifidus muscle contributes to localized control of segments of the lumbar spine and thereby controls the lumbar lordosis. By controlling the amount of vertebral rotation, the segmental multifidus can control load transmission to various anatomical structures and affect the capacity of the spine to bear axial load. In addition, the multifidus muscle plays a key role in neuromuscular control of the trunk. This role, which is heavily reliant on feedback from proprioceptors in the muscle itself, has been ascribed predominantly to the multifidus muscle, due to its dense concentration of muscle spindles. Transversely orientated muscles of the lumbo-pelvic region, such as the transversus abdominis (TrA), internal oblique (IO), piriformis, and coccygeus muscles act to stiffen the lumbo-pelvic region. In particular, the TrA muscle stiffens the sacroiliac joint and increases intra-abdominal pressure, thereby contributing to intervertebral stability.

Changes in the function of the multifidus and abdominal muscles have been demonstrated in people with low back pain (LBP). Acute first episode LBP has been shown to be associated with vertebral and side-specific changes in the size of the multifidus muscle. This was most commonly seen at the lumbo-sacral junction. Even though the symptoms associated with the LBP resolved, and subjects resumed normal work, sport, and leisure activities, persistence of decreased size of the multifidus muscle was associated with increased recurrence of episodes of LBP. Atrophy of the multifidus muscles has also been demonstrated in people with chronic LBP. Changes in timing of recruitment of the TrA muscle have been documented in people with LBP, and increased recruitment of the superficial abdominal muscles has also been demonstrated. Such changes occurring between the trunk flexor and extensor muscles can be considered as a muscle imbalance.

People exposed to microgravity conditions or prolonged bed rest have been shown to exhibit similar patterns of muscle imbalance of trunk muscles to people with LBP. Atrophy of the lumbar multifidus is greatest at the lower levels of the lumbar spine in response to prolonged bed rest, with atrophy of 4, 7, 13.5, and 12.2% reported for L2, L3, L4, and L5 vertebral levels, respectively. The abdominal flexor muscle group has been shown to collectively increase in size during bed rest. The increase in size could relate to the trunk being maintained in a flexed position and may reflect muscle shortening. Of interest is that the changes can be long-standing. After return to normal activity post-bed rest, a lack of recovery in the multifidus muscle was still evident 3 months later, and while abdominal size did decrease to baseline levels in the recovery period, increased activation of these muscles persisted for as long as 1 year following bed rest.

While bed rest studies provide a more feasible experimental model than experiments performed in microgravity (because they can be conducted on Earth and are not reliant on recruiting astronauts as participants), the microgravity model has some additional benefits. The effects of the microgravity environment on the musculoskeletal system are rapid. LeBlanc et al. showed that the CSA of the intrinsic spinal muscles occurred quickly in a shuttle mission of only 17 days. In addition, in microgravity, the astronauts can move freely, whereas in bed rest studies, movement is restricted.

For this reason, examination of the effects of exposure to microgravity on trunk muscles presents a valuable model. Studying astronauts pre- and post-spaceflight provides a unique model where predictable and rapid effects, which parallel findings seen on Earth, can be induced, and the effects of rehabilitation approaches can be evaluated.

The aims of this case study were to assess:
- (a) the size of the lumbar multifidus and antero-lateral abdominal muscles using ultrasound imaging pre- and post-space flight, to determine the effects of microgravity (and exercise in microgravity) on these muscles.
- (q) the response of the multifidus and antero-lateral abdominal muscles to rehabilitation.

## Case Description

### Assessment Protocol

The astronaut who participated in this case study was a 38-year-old male, of height 185 cm and weight 83 kg. Repeated measures of the multifidus, TrA, and IO muscles were conducted using ultrasound imaging at: pre-flight, and on Day 1 ($\mathbb{R}+1$), Day 8 ($\mathbb{R}+8$), and Day 14 ($\mathbb{R}+14$) following return to Earth after a period of six months in microgravity on the International Space Station (ISS). The astronaut provided informed consent regarding the use of his data.

### Multifidus and Abdominal Muscle Assessment

Ultrasound imaging was used to assess the cross-sectional area (CSA) of the multifidus muscle at vertebral levels L2–L5 as per the protocols described previously by Hides et al. and Wallwork et al. For the antero-lateral muscles, the protocol of Hides et al. was used. The assessor performing the ultrasound imaging assessments of muscle size had intensive training in this assessment technique, and prior to this study demonstrated very high intra-rater reliability ($\mathrm{ICC}_{1,1}$ range of 0.83–0.99) and interrater reliability ($\mathrm{ICC}_{2,1}$ range of 0.88–0.99) for measurement of the multifidus, TrA, and IO muscles.

The ultrasound imaging apparatus used (GE LOGIQ e) was equipped with a 5-MHz convex array transducer. For collection of images of the multifidus muscles, the astronaut was positioned in a prone lying position and the lumbar spinous processes were palpated and marked with a pen to assist with identification of vertebral level. Electro-conductive gel was applied to the transducer, which was placed transversely over the relevant spinous process.

### Exercise on the ISS

In microgravity, there is marked deconditioning of the body, requiring an extensive in-flight countermeasure program to counteract adaptation to this changed environmental condition. The exercise program focused on cardiovascular exercise and resistance exercise, concentrating on leg and overall trunk strength retention. The countermeasure training performed during this mission comprised intensive strength and cardiovascular exercises.

### Post-Mission Rehabilitation

Post-mission rehabilitation is essential, with a focus on stimulation of the motor control system, recovery of the gravity axis, and gradual progression of load-bearing exercises to retrain the stabilizing and protective function of the muscles on return to full function under Earth's gravity.

### Outcomes

The response of the multifidus muscle to microgravity varied for different vertebral levels. For vertebral levels L2 and L3, CSA was retained between pre-flight and $\mathbb{R}+1$ time points, with a 7% increase in size. For the L4 vertebral level, there was little change in CSA (1%). For the L5 vertebral level, a large decrease in CSA (29%) was seen post-flight. The response of the TrA muscle to microgravity was a decrease in thickness, while the thickness of the IO muscle increased.

### Discussion

Exposure to microgravity resulted in a muscle imbalance between the trunk flexor and extensor muscles. The greatest loss of multifidus muscle size occurred at the L5 vertebral level. For the antero-lateral abdominal muscles, the thickness of the TrA muscle decreased, while the thickness of the IO muscle increased.

### Effects of Post-Flight Rehabilitation

Post-flight rehabilitation was effective in restoring CSA of the lumbar multifidus muscles, and for the L4 and L5 vertebral levels, post-rehabilitation values exceeded pre-flight muscle CSA values, indicating muscle size restoration. Rehabilitation also restored the thickness of the abdominal muscles.

### Future Directions

Studying changes in muscle consistency occurring in astronauts could provide further insights, which would be difficult to achieve on Earth. The microgravity model could offer valuable insight into conditions seen on Earth, including low back pain, prolonged bed rest, and muscle imbalance in sporting populations.
