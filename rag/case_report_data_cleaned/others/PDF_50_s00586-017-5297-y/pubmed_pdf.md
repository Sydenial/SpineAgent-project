# Surgical treatment of a lumbar aneurysmal bone cyst using percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy

## Abstract

**Purpose**  
Aneurysmal bone cysts of the lumbar spine are usually treated by curettage followed by bone or bioactive ceramics grafting. Here, we present the first case of an aneurysmal bone cyst of the lumbar spine treated by percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (PELD).

**Methods**  
We describe the clinical characteristics of the patient, including the radiological and pathological findings of the tumor and the surgical technique used.

**Results**  
A 15-year-old boy presented with low back pain and was diagnosed with an aneurysmal bone cyst of the L3 vertebra based on radiological findings, including plain radiograph, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging. The technique and equipment of PELD were used to perform curettage of the tumor cavity and fill it with hydroxyapatite granules. The skin incision was only 8 mm. The patient was discharged 1 day postoperatively and could walk without assistance. The postoperative course was uneventful and the symptoms improved following surgery.

**Conclusion**  
Endoscopic surgery via PELD can be a treatment option for aneurysmal bone cysts of the lumbar spine.

## Introduction

An aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC) is a benign tumor-like lesion that mainly affects the diaphysis and metaphysis of long bones in the first three decades of life. Traditionally, intralesional curettage followed by bone or bioactive ceramics grafting is the mainstay of treatment. ABCs occurring in the spine are relatively rare compared to those in the extremities. We report a case of an adolescent patient with an ABC affecting the lumbar spine who was treated using an endoscopic technique that was less invasive than traditional surgery.

## Case Report

A 15-year-old boy presented with low back pain that began 1 year earlier and worsened with prolonged sitting. On clinical examination, no neurological deficits were noted and the straight leg raise test was positive on the right side. Plain radiographs showed an expanding, destructive lesion involving the lamina and unilateral pedicle and body of the third lumbar vertebra. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated an expansile osteolytic lesion that appeared dark on T1-weighted images and bright on T2-weighted images, extending into the pedicle and posterior part of the vertebral body. No extraskeletal mass was identified. Computed tomography showed an expansile lesion with a thinning cortex and bony intralesional septations. Calcification and new bone formation were not observed. Based on these findings, the lesion was diagnosed as a benign bone tumor or tumor-like lesion, such as an ABC or solitary bone cyst.

Biopsy and curettage were performed under general anesthesia using the technique and equipment of percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy. The patient was placed in the prone position under radiological guidance. An 8-mm skin incision was made on the right side at the level of the third lumbar vertebra. After insertion of a cannula behind the vertebral arch, a hole was created at the most vulnerable site of the cystic lesion using an obturator, allowing entry into the lesion cavity. Approximately 20 mL of fresh blood-like contents were aspirated. An arthroscope was inserted through the same hole, enabling observation of the inner cavity.

The resected surgical specimen was submitted for frozen section examination to exclude malignancy. After curettage of the tissue on the inner walls of the lesion, the single approach was considered insufficient for adequate curettage, and an additional hole was drilled from another direction. Following complete curettage of the cavity wall, 20 g of hydroxyapatite granules were inserted into the cavity via a funnel-shaped tube through the cannula and packed into the deepest part of the cavity using an impactor. The arthroscope used had an outer diameter of 8 mm with a working channel of 4.1 mm. A bipolar coagulator was used for hemostasis. The operative time was 1 hour and 37 minutes, and blood loss was less than 10 mL.

Histopathological examination revealed that the aspirated contents were predominantly blood with neutrophil infiltration. Apparent tumor tissue containing multinucleated giant cells characteristic of ABC was not identified.

The patient was discharged 1 day postoperatively and was able to walk unassisted. Mild low back pain persisted at 5 months after surgery but resolved by 8 months postoperatively. The Oswestry Disability Index improved from 14% preoperatively to 0% at 3 years after surgery. No evidence of recurrence was observed at the 3-year follow-up.

## Discussion

ABC is a non-neoplastic expansile bone lesion that most frequently affects the pelvic bones and the metaphysis of long bones, such as the femur and tibia, and accounts for approximately 2% of all bone tumors [1]. About 20% of cases involve the spine, where lesions may occur in any region [2]. Radiologically, ABCs appear as radiolucent, expanding, ballooned-out lesions with an eggshell-thin cortex and intralesional septations. Histologically, the cavity walls are lined with fibrous connective tissue containing proliferated spindle cells and osteoclast-like multinucleated giant cells, sometimes with small amounts of osteoid [3].

In the present case, characteristic spindle cells and multinucleated giant cells were not identified histologically. Although the lesion was diagnosed as an ABC based on imaging findings, fresh bloody contents, and the absence of fracture, a solitary bone cyst could not be definitively excluded. Curative surgery with intraoperative biopsy was therefore performed without a preceding biopsy. Previous studies have reported a high diagnostic yield for intraoperative pathology consultation in bone tumors [4], and the risk of surgical field contamination is minimal in small-incision procedures such as this.

Treatment options for ABCs include curettage with or without bone grafting, complete excision, selective arterial embolization, radiation therapy, or combinations of these approaches. Radiation therapy carries a risk of radiation-induced sarcoma or myelopathy [5], while embolization, although effective in many cases [6], poses a risk of compromising spinal cord blood supply in certain regions [7]. Pharmacological treatment with denosumab has shown promising results in selected cases [8].

Simple curettage is associated with a recurrence rate of approximately 19% due to incomplete removal of the cavity lining [3]. Total excision reduces recurrence but may cause spinal instability requiring fusion [9–12]. Less-invasive alternatives, such as cement augmentation or percutaneous injection of osteoconductive materials, have also been reported with favorable outcomes [13,14]. Although endoscopic techniques have been described for bone tumors in the extremities [15,16], reports of endoscopic treatment for spinal tumors are limited [17]. To our knowledge, this is the first report describing the use of PELD for the treatment of an ABC of the lumbar spine.

PELD, originally derived from percutaneous nucleotomy, allows for a small skin incision, excellent visualization through continuous irrigation, and reduced approach-related morbidity. Compared with conventional open surgery or microscopic surgery, PELD provides multidirectional visualization, effective hemostasis, and preservation of spinal stability without the need for fusion.

The disadvantages of PELD include the potential for incomplete curettage and its steep learning curve. Nevertheless, this case demonstrates that endoscopic surgery via PELD can be a viable treatment option for aneurysmal bone cysts of the lumbar spine.
