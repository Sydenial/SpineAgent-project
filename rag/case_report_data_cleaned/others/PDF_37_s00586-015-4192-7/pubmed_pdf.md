# The occurrence of vascular displacement into intervertebral disc space following the compensated sagittal imbalance of the spine: a case report and review of literature

## Abstract

**Background**  
It is known that sagittal compensating mechanisms are created for counteracting sagittal imbalance problems; however, they can sometimes be associated with incidents which affect the plan of management.

**Purpose**  
The purpose of this study was to report a case of the occurrence of common iliac vessel displacement into the intervertebral disc space following one of the spinal compensatory mechanisms.

**Materials and Methods**  
The authors demonstrated this case by presenting the patient history, physical examination, imaging studies, and treatment strategy, as well as reviewing related literature.

**Results**  
An 81-year-old woman presented with a long history of low back pain with claudication. Upright plain radiographs and flexion–extension studies demonstrated progressive local thoracolumbar kyphosis and loss of lumbar lordosis with significant widening of the L4–L5 intervertebral disc space. MRI and three-dimensional volume-rendered spiral computed tomography revealed abnormal content within the disc space, identified as the common iliac vessels. This represents a rare case of common iliac vessel displacement into the intervertebral disc space associated with a spinal compensatory mechanism.

**Conclusion**  
Vascular displacement into the intervertebral disc space related to lumbar hyperextension as a compensatory mechanism is rare but possible. When abnormal widening of the intervertebral disc space is observed, particularly at the lower lumbar level, surgeons should be alert to the risk of vascular injury during disc procedures. Thorough imaging evaluation and careful selection of surgical strategy are essential.

## Introduction

In recent decades, increasing attention has been directed toward spinal sagittal balance, which significantly influences the management of spinal diseases, particularly degenerative pathologies and spinal deformities. Compensatory mechanisms—including reduction of thoracic kyphosis, intervertebral hyperextension, retrolisthesis, pelvic retroversion, knee flexion, and ankle extension [1]—have been thoroughly described. These mechanisms counteract sagittal imbalance but may also be associated with secondary pathological conditions that affect treatment planning.

In this report, we describe an unusual case of common iliac vessel displacement into the intervertebral disc space associated with a sagittal compensatory mechanism. The case is illustrated through patient history, physical examination, imaging findings, and treatment strategy, together with a review of relevant literature.

## Case Report

An 81-year-old woman presented with a 20-year history of low back pain accompanied by claudication. Her symptoms, including claudication and a stooped posture, had progressively worsened over the previous 10 years. She had been treated conservatively and had no history of trauma or prior spinal surgery.

Physical examination revealed significant tenderness in the lower back and local thoracolumbar junctional kyphosis. Although she could initially stand upright with compensation through hip extension and knee flexion, a stooped posture became apparent after walking a short distance. No neurological deficits were identified.

Imaging evaluation included upright plain radiographs and dynamic flexion–extension studies, which demonstrated angular instability with widening of the L4–L5 intervertebral disc space. Comparison of full-length standing radiographs from 2007 and 2014 confirmed progressive sagittal imbalance. Sagittal reformatted CT images revealed widening of the L4–L5 disc space filled with abnormal tissue. Subsequent MRI and three-dimensional volume-rendered CT confirmed displacement of the common iliac vessels into the intervertebral disc space.

Surgical management involved posterior instrumentation. The patient was positioned prone, and spinal alignment was confirmed fluoroscopically. Pedicle screw fixation from L3 to S1 combined with bilateral iliac screw fixation was performed, followed by posterior and posterolateral fusion, decompression at L4–L5, and insertion of hyperlordotic rods. Although ideal spinopelvic parameters were not fully achieved, intraoperative imaging demonstrated acceptable alignment. At six-month follow-up, the patient’s symptoms had gradually improved, and she was able to ambulate independently in an erect posture.

## Discussion

Since the initial description of compensatory mechanisms for progressive kyphosis in osteoporotic patients [2], sagittal balance and its adaptations have been widely discussed. Compensatory changes may occur in the spine, pelvis, and lower extremities, depending on the severity of sagittal imbalance [3]. Although these mechanisms aim to restore upright posture and horizontal gaze, they can generate secondary pathological conditions.

In cases of decreased lumbar lordosis due to degenerative disc disease, pelvic retroversion is often the initial compensatory response [4,5]. Concurrently, posterior spinal muscles attempt to restore lordosis through adjacent segment hyperextension, which can increase muscular fatigue and stress on facet joints [5].

The present case illustrates a rare phenomenon: displacement of the common iliac vessels into the intervertebral disc space associated with lumbar hyperextension. Lumbar hyperextension, particularly at upper lumbar levels, is considered a compensatory response to anterior shift of the gravitational axis [3,6]. Only isolated reports have described intradiscal herniation of iliac vessels [7], and these were not clearly linked to compensatory mechanisms. Unlike vascular injuries caused intraoperatively [8], the present case developed gradually as part of a chronic compensatory process.

Progressive widening of the L4–L5 disc space was observed over time, corresponding with worsening thoracolumbar kyphosis. Isthmic lysis of L4 further supported the role of sustained hyperextension. Anatomically, attenuation or disruption of the anterior longitudinal ligament and outer annulus fibrosus may allow anterior abdominal vessels to migrate into the disc space.

The primary surgical goal was stabilization of a severely unstable lumbar segment, while a secondary goal was partial correction of sagittal alignment. Vascular injury was the principal concern, as such complications, though rare, carry high mortality rates [9–14]. Lumbar hyperextension with disruption of anterior stabilizing structures may increase the risk of vascular injury during disc procedures, particularly posterior lumbar interbody fusion. Most reported vascular injuries during lumbar disc surgery occur at the L4–L5 level [15], coinciding with the level affected in this case.

Given the patient’s advanced age, aggressive corrective osteotomy was deemed to carry excessive risk. Maintaining a compensated sagittal balance was considered preferable to achieving ideal alignment at the expense of increased morbidity [17]. Instrumented posterior lumbar fusion was therefore selected. A notable pitfall observed postoperatively was that the upper end of instrumentation terminated at the apex of kyphosis, a factor known to increase the risk of surgical failure [18,19].

## Conclusion

Vascular displacement into the intervertebral disc space associated with lumbar hyperextension as a compensatory mechanism is rare but clinically significant. When abnormal widening of the intervertebral disc space is identified, particularly at lower lumbar levels, surgeons should be alert to the risk of vascular injury during disc procedures. Comprehensive imaging evaluation and carefully tailored surgical strategies are essential to minimize complications.
