# Asymmetric C7 Pedicle Subtraction Osteotomy for Correction of Rigid Cervical Coronal Imbalance Secondary to Post-Traumatic Heterotopic Ossification: A Case Report, Description of a Novel Surgical Technique, and Literature Review

## Abstract

**Purpose**  
Deformities of the cervical spine are uncommon in the coronal plane. In this report, a unique case of a 31-year-old male with a fixed, 30° left coronal deformity due to heterotopic ossification 3 years status post polytrauma was treated with an asymmetric C7 pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO).

**Methods**  
Case report.

**Results**  
Pre-operatively, the patient had a fixed 45-degree left tilt of his neck and radiographs demonstrated a rigid 30° scoliosis, 7 cm coronal imbalance, and 4 cm negative sagittal balance, with diffuse bridging bone between the spinous processes and the facet joints of C5 to T1 bilaterally. An asymmetric C7 PSO with C2–T3 posterior spinal fusion was completed without complication. There was residual 9° coronal deformity, 2.9 cm left coronal imbalance, and 2.3 cm sagittal imbalance. He had marked improvement in function, as assessed by the SF-36 physical component score (pre-op 31.1; post-op 44.7) and mental component score (pre-op 46.0; post-op 66.8). Post-operatively, neck disability index scores also improved (pre-op 38; post-op 16). Although the patient passed away from a drug overdose 14 months post-operatively, he did not report neck pain, did not seek evaluation from another physician for his neck, and did not undergo a subsequent neck operation prior to his passing.

**Conclusion**  
In this single patient, an asymmetric C7 PSO was performed safely. While it was effective in addressing a fixed cervical coronal imbalance, its efficacy and safety profile should be confirmed in larger cohorts.

## Introduction

Post-traumatic heterotopic ossification (HO) of the cervical spine is uncommon. Nevertheless, it may cause restricted range of motion, nerve impingement, pain, soft tissue ulceration, and joint ankylosis [1–3]. Resultant deformity in the sagittal or coronal planes may adversely affect one’s quality of life [4]. While deformity of the cervical spine often occurs in the sagittal plane, coronal plane deformity is also recognized [5–7]. Cervical kyphotic deformity has been successfully and safely treated with a pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO) at the cervicothoracic junction [8–10]. To the best of our knowledge, we detail the first successful use of an asymmetric PSO at C7 to correct a rigid coronal cervical deformity due to post-traumatic heterotopic ossification.

## Case Report

### History, Physical Examination, and Imaging

A 31-year-old male presented to the outpatient spine clinic with a chief complaint of neck tilting and pain. Three years prior, he had suffered a motorcycle crash resulting in a traumatic brain injury, right upper extremity amputation, left lower extremity amputation, and prolonged intubation. After discharge, he developed progressive torticollis despite physical therapy and Botox injections, causing functional impairment of vision, voice, and ambulation. Additional details regarding the extent of these impairments were unavailable.

Clinically, he demonstrated a fixed 45-degree tilt of the neck to the left. Imaging demonstrated 7 cm of coronal imbalance to the left and a 4 cm negative sagittal imbalance. Standing radiographs of the full spine could not be obtained due to the prior left lower extremity amputation. Diffuse bridging bone between the spinous processes and the facet joints of C5 to T1 bilaterally was noted. This bridging bone was presumed to represent heterotopic ossification given its extensive involvement of the subaxial cervical spine and the patient’s traumatic brain injury history [4]. There was no facet dislocation; sagittal images demonstrated well-aligned facets and no anterolisthesis at any level.

To address the multiplanar, fixed cervical deformity, the patient was scheduled for a C2–T3 posterior spinal fusion with a C7 PSO. Correction planning was based on pre-operative coronal malalignment and negative sagittal alignment, with the goal of centering the patient’s head between his shoulders without significantly altering sagittal alignment. Given the rigidity of the deformity, a three-column osteotomy was deemed necessary. The distal extent of the construct was set at T3, based on principles of fixation following PSO. Although the proximal construct could have ended in the subaxial spine, fixation was extended to C2 using pedicle screws due to superior screw purchase compared with lateral mass screws.

### Surgical Technique

The patient underwent general anesthesia and endotracheal intubation in the supine position, followed by halo placement. He was then positioned prone on a flat table with a Jackson four-post frame, and the halo was rigidly attached to the operating table. Given the fixed coronal deformity, initial halo positioning was dictated by the relationship of the head to the body.

A midline incision from C2 to T3 was made, and the spine was exposed subperiosteally. Pedicle screws were placed bilaterally at C2, T1, T2, and T3, and lateral mass screws were placed bilaterally at C3, C4, and C5. The inferior and superior articular processes at C6, C7, and T1 were autofused due to extensive heterotopic ossification. The C7 level was confirmed by identifying the T1 ribs bilaterally and dissecting one level proximally. Laminectomies were performed at C6, C7, and T1, and the C7 and C8 nerve roots were identified and followed through their foramina.

The C7 pedicles were skeletonized, and the transverse processes were removed bilaterally. Sequential lumbar taps were used to decancellate the vertebral body through the pedicles. The lateral walls were prepared and removed using rongeurs and osteotomes. The middle column was removed with curettes to create an asymmetric closing wedge resection of approximately 30° on the right side. No provisional stabilization was used during the osteotomy, as the head was rigidly fixed to the table via the halo.

After completion of the osteotomy, gradual correction of the coronal deformity was achieved by manually translating the halo to the right while visualizing closure of the osteotomy site. Correction was performed only in the coronal plane, with minimal change to sagittal alignment. The degree of correction was guided by trunk alignment and neuromonitoring signals, with neurologic safety prioritized. Desired correction was achieved without changes in neuromonitoring. The halo was then resecured, and rods were placed from C2 to T3. Decortication was performed, and allograft bone was placed posterolaterally. No bone graft was placed directly in the osteotomy site, acknowledging an increased risk of nonunion; the authors note that they have since employed four-rod constructs across PSO sites to improve stability.

### Post-operative Course

The patient tolerated the procedure well and was discharged on post-operative day 7 with an intact neurologic examination. At 6-week follow-up, he demonstrated improved head balance, level shoulders, and improved vocal and ambulatory function, although detailed assessment was unavailable. Radiographs demonstrated a residual coronal deformity of 9°, left coronal imbalance of 2.9 cm, and negative sagittal imbalance of 2.3 cm. Functional outcomes improved significantly, with increases in SF-36 physical and mental component scores and improvement in neck disability index scores.

The patient did not return for further follow-up after relocating out of state for military service. He passed away 14 months post-operatively due to a drug overdose. Prior to his death, he did not report neck pain, seek additional medical evaluation for his neck, or undergo further cervical surgery.

## Discussion

Fixed coronal cervical deformity is rare. While mild deformities often do not result in functional impairment, severe deformities pose significant challenges for both patients and surgeons. Existing literature on cervical deformity primarily focuses on sagittal plane pathology and pediatric cases involving hemivertebra resection [11, 12]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report describing the use of an asymmetric C7 PSO to correct fixed cervical coronal imbalance in an adult.

This technique represents a modification of previously described cervicothoracic PSOs used for correction of cervical sagittal imbalance [8–10]. Consistent with prior reports, cervical PSO in this case was safe and effective, resulting in improved posture, mobility, and quality of life. Alternative techniques for cervical coronal deformity correction are limited to rare pediatric cases involving congenital hemivertebrae, which differ significantly in pathology and surgical strategy [11, 12].

Despite its novelty, this report has limitations. It represents a retrospective review of a single case at a single institution, limiting generalizability and introducing bias. Pre- and post-operative assessments of vocal and ambulatory function were incomplete, and imaging from the time of the initial injury was unavailable. Full assessment of global spinal alignment was also limited by the patient’s prior lower extremity amputation. Additionally, clinical and radiographic follow-up was limited to 6 weeks, precluding assessment of long-term outcomes. Although long-term evaluation was not possible due to the patient’s death, he reported no neck-related issues more than one year after surgery.

## Conclusion

Proper patient selection, thorough pre-operative planning, and patient education are essential for successful correction of rigid cervical coronal deformity using cervicothoracic junction PSO. While this isolated case demonstrates that asymmetric C7 PSO can be performed safely and effectively, its efficacy and safety should be validated in larger patient cohorts.
