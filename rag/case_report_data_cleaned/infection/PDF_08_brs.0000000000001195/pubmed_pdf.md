# Treatment of Extreme Tuberculous Kyphosis Using Spinal Osteotomy and Halo-Pelvic Traction: A Case Report

## Abstract
Spinal tuberculosis causes destruction, deformity, and paraplegia. Long-standing kyphosis may progress with growth in children, producing respiratory insufficiency and neurologic deficits. Surgery may help prevent or reverse neurological deterioration while improving pulmonary function in cases with significant spinal deformity.

## Introduction
Spinal tuberculosis is a severe condition that can result in deformity and paraplegia. The treatment of extreme kyphotic deformities, especially those resulting from spinal tuberculosis, remains challenging. In this report, we describe the treatment of a 24-year-old female patient with extreme tuberculous kyphosis using spinal osteotomy and halo-pelvic traction.

## Case Report
A 24-year-old female with tuberculous angular kyphosis presented with bilateral lower extremities paresis and obvious humpback (Figure 1). Tuberculosis of the thoracic vertebra was diagnosed 22 years ago due to dorsal pain and prolonged low-grade fever. The patient had received anti-tuberculosis medications but underwent lesion clearance, posterior fusion, and a plaster-of-Paris jacket 15 years ago due to mobility limitations.

### Initial Treatment
The patient’s thoracic kyphosis had a Cobb's angle of 180° (Figure 2 A, B: radiographs, C: CT image, and D: MRI image). A halo-pelvic apparatus was fitted onto the patient (Figure 3). Distraction was initiated, and the patient’s neurological status was carefully monitored.

### Surgical Intervention
After 6 months of distraction, the Cobb's angle decreased to approximately 120° (Figure 4). Due to insufficient flexibility of the kyphosis, the patient underwent posterior release surgery. A rectangle bone groove (0.5 cm wide and 2/3 depth of the lamina) was created on each of the fused vertebral lamina using a high-speed drill (Figure 5).

After surgery, the halo-pelvic traction continued for another 4 months until the Cobb's angle decreased to about 80°. Pedicle subtraction osteotomy, pedicle screw fixation, and autologous bone grafting were performed to achieve final correction. The angular kyphosis was corrected to a Cobb's angle of 30° from T1 to T12 (Figure 7), and the patient’s height increased by approximately 30 cm.

### Postoperative Follow-up
There were no complications related to the halo-pelvic distraction during the treatment. The patient’s bilateral lower extremities paresis gradually improved. At 2 years of follow-up, the correction angle and sagittal balance were well maintained, and the humpback was not visible (Figure 9).

## Discussion
Spinal tuberculosis can cause severe destruction, deformity, and paraplegia. Long-standing kyphosis may progress with growth, producing significant neurologic deficits. Surgical correction of severe kyphosis, along with decompression and debridement of the tuberculosis-destroyed vertebral bodies, is the most effective method to avoid late-onset paraplegia. Surgical techniques for spine deformity correction vary, but one-stage corrections increase the risk of complications, such as large-vessel injuries, neurological injuries, and stabilization failure.

Halo-pelvic traction, although not widely used recently, has demonstrated excellent clinical effects in treating severe spinal deformities. Despite its drawbacks, such as patient discomfort and the burden of wearing the apparatus for extended periods, the halo-pelvic apparatus offers the advantage of gradual correction, which can be safer for patients with very rigid and angular kyphosis.

## Conclusion
The halo-pelvic apparatus produces high corrective forces over a long period and provides a slow, safe correction of deformity. In cases of extreme kyphotic deformity, halo-pelvic traction is an appropriate technique that avoids many complications associated with rapid, one-stage correction.

## Key Points
- The patient presented with extreme tuberculous kyphosis with a Cobb's angle of 180°.
- Treatment involved four steps: halo-pelvic traction, posterior release surgery, continued halo-pelvic traction, and final correction with pedicle subtraction osteotomy and pedicle screw fixation.
- The angular kyphosis was corrected to a Cobb's angle of 30° without complications or neurological deterioration during treatment. The correction angle and sagittal balance were maintained over 2 years of follow-up.
- In cases of extreme kyphotic deformity, halo-pelvic traction is an effective technique, avoiding many serious complications from rapid, one-stage correction.
