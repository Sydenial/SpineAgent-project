# Whole cerebrospinal axis infection after lumbar epidural injection: a case report

## Abstract

**Purpose**: Lumbar epidural injection is a popular treatment for degenerative lumbar disease. Although post-procedural epidural infection is rare, meningitis and epidural abscess are life-threatening conditions, requiring additional medical and surgical intervention. This article reports a patient with fatal whole cerebrospinal axis infection after lumbar epidural injections.

**Methods**: A 64-year-old female patient presented with septic shock and quadriparesis. She had previously received lumbar epidural injections for degenerative spondylolisthesis at L4-5. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed epidural abscesses, compressed dura, and spinal cord from C1 to S2, along with cerebral meningitis. Laminectomies and abscess removal were performed. After three weeks, her mental status deteriorated, and brain computed tomography (CT) revealed hydrocephalus, necessitating ventriculoperitoneal shunting.

**Results**: Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus epidermidis was cultured from blood samples. The infection was controlled, and her mental status improved to alert, though her quadriparesis persisted.

**Conclusions**: While lumbar epidural injection is an effective procedure for lumbar radicular pain, it can induce fatal complications such as sepsis and epidural abscess.

## Introduction

Spinal pain block procedures, such as interlaminar or transforaminal epidural injections, are common treatments for spinal radicular pain. In degenerative lumbar disease, lumbar epidural steroids and local anesthesia can significantly improve symptoms without requiring surgery. However, complications from these procedures, although rare, include meningitis, epidural abscess, spondylitis, and discitis, all of which may require additional surgical intervention and prolonged antibiotic therapy. In some cases, infection can lead to motor weakness and life-threatening complications, such as the fatal whole cerebrospinal axis infection seen in this patient.

## Case report

A 64-year-old female patient was referred from the infectious disease department to our neurosurgery department due to weakness in both legs (G2 of 5). Over the past 11 months, the patient had received lumbar epidural injections monthly for degenerative spondylolisthesis at L4-5. The most recent injection occurred approximately three weeks prior.

Her medical history, aside from hypertension, was unremarkable. Six days before admission to our department, the patient had been admitted to the infectious disease department with a fever (39.1°C) of unknown origin. Initial laboratory tests revealed elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, as well as increased white blood cell and neutrophil counts. Despite receiving empirical intravenous antibiotics, the patient's fever remained uncontrolled, and her blood pressure dropped, leading to weakness in both legs.

Thoracolumbar MRI showed epidural abscesses and compressed dura and spinal cord from T9 to S2. A muscular abscess was also detected in the lumbar multifidus area.

## Surgical Intervention

Intermittent laminectomies and abscess removal were performed from T9 to S1. However, the patient's bilateral lower extremity weakness did not improve, and her upper extremity strength decreased to grade 3/5 after surgery. A cervical MRI revealed a ventral epidural abscess at the C1-2 level, which was compressing the spinal cord. Additionally, meningeal enhancement indicated cerebral meningitis. C1-2 hemi-laminectomies and abscess removal were immediately performed.

Three weeks postoperatively, the patient's mental status deteriorated to a deep, drowsy state. A brain CT scan showed hydrocephalus, and ventriculoperitoneal shunting was performed after cerebrospinal fluid drainage. Initial cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed elevated protein levels, and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus epidermidis (MRSE) was detected in the initial blood culture.

The infection was fully controlled with eight weeks of intravenous vancomycin, and the patient's mental status improved. Her quadriparesis showed partial improvement.

## Discussion

Epidural spinal injections are commonly performed to treat pain in patients with lumbar degenerative disease. However, complications such as epidural hematoma, nerve injury, and infection are potential risks. Infection rates after epidural injections are low, but when they occur, they can lead to severe complications, including epidural abscess, spondylitis, and meningitis. If spinal epidural abscesses are not managed properly, they can result in neurological compromise, requiring further surgical intervention.

In this case, the infection was detected through blood cultures, as cultures of the epidural pus and CSF were negative. Empirical antibiotic therapy should include coverage for Gram-positive cocci, such as Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis. Additionally, MRI is crucial for diagnosing spinal epidural abscesses, which can extend throughout the spine.

This case also highlights the importance of maintaining aseptic conditions during procedures and the potential risks associated with frequent epidural injections. Glucocorticoid injections can suppress immune responses, increasing the risk of infection. Therefore, pain management guidelines recommend limiting the number of epidural injections per year to reduce the likelihood of complications.

## Conclusion

Although lumbar epidural injections are an effective treatment for lumbar radicular pain, they can lead to fatal complications such as meningitis and epidural abscess. Careful attention to aseptic technique, the use of glucocorticoids, and limiting the number of procedures performed are essential in preventing these life-threatening conditions.
