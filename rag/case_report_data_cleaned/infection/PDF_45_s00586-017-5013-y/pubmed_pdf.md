# Acute Non-Traumatic Idiopathic Spinal Subdural Hematoma: Radiographic Findings and Surgical Results with a Literature Review

## Abstract

**Purpose**  
Intraspinal hematoma is a serious condition, and early diagnosis is necessary to permit emergency treatment. Among such hematomas, non-traumatic spinal subdural hematoma is a rare occurrence. We have experienced three patients with surgically proven subdural spinal hematoma, and here we report these cases with a review of their clinical and imaging characteristics.

**Methods**  
All three cases were idiopathic with no history of disease, no coagulopathy, and no trauma. All had acute onset that brought about paralysis of the lower limbs with severe pain. Early surgery was performed based on a relatively early diagnosis using thoracolumbar MRI and CT.

**Results**  
Since the epidural fat is not affected by bleeding, the normal structure remains, and the boundary between hematoma and fat is a significant feature in MRI and CT. Partial gadolinium enhancement in MRI and ring enhancement in contrast CT were also apparent. Two of the cases had subarachnoid hematomas.

**Conclusions**  
Preoperative diagnosis of spinal subarachnoid hematoma is difficult because there are no specific radiological findings and confirmation can only occur intraoperatively. In particular, one case had a massive hematoma causing canal stenosis, and it was difficult to distinguish between intradural and extradural hematoma. In all cases of subarachnoid or subdural hematoma, decompression was performed within 24 h after onset, and consequently, the patients had relatively good outcomes.

**Keywords**  
Hematoma; Diagnostic imaging; Spinal arachnoid hematoma; Spinal subdural hematoma; Surgery

## Presentation of Cases

### Case 1

A 43-year-old man presented with sudden bilateral severe lower leg pain and numbness. He then suffered dysuria and motor loss of the lower extremities. He had no history of trauma. A manual muscle test (MMT) of the lower extremities gave a score of 3/5 with hypesthesia below the L1 dermatome. MRI revealed a dorsal subdural hematoma extending from the L1–L2 level with compression of the spinal cord. The lesion was hyperintense on T2-weighted images and hypointense on T1-weighted images. Preoperative blood tests indicated that platelet count, prothrombin time, partial thromboplastin time, and international normalized ratio were normal.

### Case 2

A 61-year-old woman presented with sudden severe low back pain and paraparesis. She had no history of trauma or bleeding diathesis. The MMT score for the lower extremities was 3/5. Thoracolumbar MRI revealed a dorsal subdural hematoma extending from the L2 level with compression of the spinal cord. The lesion was hyperintense on T2-weighted and T2-weighted fast spin echo images. Preoperative blood tests were normal.

### Case 3

A 60-year-old woman developed acute onset of right extremity weakness with bilateral severe lower leg pain and was unable to walk independently. She had no major medical history, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or other chronic disease. Cranial nerve and cerebellar tests were normal. Muscle power decreased in the lower extremities, with an MMT score of 3/5. MRI revealed a mass posterior-laterally compressing the spinal cord at the Th12 level, with partial gadolinium enhancement. Ring enhancement was seen on contrast CT. Preoperative blood tests were normal.

## Historical Review

Intraspinal hematoma is an emergency condition that requires early diagnosis for treatment with a successful outcome. The condition is divided into extradural hematoma, subdural hematoma, subarachnoid hematoma, and hematomyelia, depending on the location [1]. Spinal extradural hematoma is the most frequent entity, whereas subdural hematoma is rare and the frequency of subarachnoid hematoma is very low. Spinal subarachnoid hematoma accounts for less than 1% of all cases of subarachnoid hematoma [5]. Among 106 surgeries for spinal subdural hematoma, subarachnoid hematoma accounted for 13% [4].

The thoracolumbar and lumbar regions are the most frequently involved spinal segments. There is no gender predominance, with a slightly higher incidence in the fifth to sixth decades of life [4].

The etiology of intraspinal hematoma includes coagulopathy, iatrogenic causes (spinal or epidural anesthesia, lumbar puncture, spinal surgery), spinal trauma, bleeding disorders, arteriovenous malformations, and tumors [1–3, 6, 7]. Spontaneous spinal subdural hematoma is rare, and all cases in our series were idiopathic. Proposed mechanisms include collapse of microvasculature between the dura and arachnoid with minor trauma [6], or spread of a subarachnoid hematoma into the subdural space [7].

Symptoms of spinal subdural hematoma include sudden-onset back pain, which may radiate to the limbs or trunk, weakness, and urinary or fecal incontinence. Physical findings vary by lesion level and include motor, sensory, and sphincter deficits [1–3]. Early diagnosis using thoracolumbar MRI and CT is particularly important to facilitate early surgery.

Differentiation from epidural hematoma is based on the positional relationship between the hematoma and the dura mater. Since epidural fat is not affected by bleeding, preservation of epidural fat with a clear boundary is a key imaging feature. MRI is the modality of choice. The classic inverted Mercedes-Benz sign may be seen due to enhancement of the filum terminale and nerve roots [11]. CT is also useful because hematoma appears hyperdense while fat appears hypodense, facilitating localization [12].

In cases with massive hematoma, preoperative distinction between subdural and subarachnoid hematoma may be impossible, and definitive diagnosis may only be achieved intraoperatively.

## Rationale for Treatment and Evidence-Based Literature

For patients with paralysis, emergency surgery with preparation for an intradural procedure is desirable. Conservative treatment may be justified in cases with minimal neurological deficits, but patients with major deficits or rapidly deteriorating clinical and radiological findings are likely to benefit from surgery [14–16]. Early decompressive laminectomy with evacuation of the hematoma is considered the best treatment [13]. Patients presenting with paraplegia and bowel or bladder dysfunction have the poorest prognosis, regardless of treatment modality [15, 18, 19].

## Procedures

### Case 1

The patient underwent emergency L1–L2 bilateral laminectomy. No extradural hematoma was found. After opening the dura, the spinal cord at the conus region was retracted, revealing a dorsal hematoma. The hematoma was removed, and the spinal cord was decompressed.

### Case 2

Emergency L1–L3 bilateral laminectomy revealed a normal epidural space. Opening of the dura and arachnoid membrane exposed a hematoma, which was completely removed, resulting in decompression of the spinal conus. A diagnosis of subarachnoid hematoma was made.

### Case 3

Emergency bilateral laminectomy at Th11–Th12 revealed no extradural hematoma. After opening the dura and arachnoid membrane, a ventral hematoma was identified and removed. These findings led to a diagnosis of subarachnoid hematoma.

## Outcome and Follow-Up

### Case 1

Three months after surgery, motor function and dysuria had recovered.

### Case 2

Three months after surgery, the patient was free of neurological deficits.

### Case 3

Numbness in the lower extremities resolved after surgery, and motor weakness had disappeared six months later.
