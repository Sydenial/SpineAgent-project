# Minimally invasive percutaneous endoscopic treatment for acute pyogenic spondylodiscitis following vertebroplasty

## Abstract

I first report of minimally invasive endoscopic treatment for acute spondylodiscitis caused by vertebroplasty. A 60-year-old female was transferred with the symptom of right hip flexion weakness for 1 day. The patient underwent a vertebroplasty procedure because of L3 osteoporotic compression fracture at another hospital 6 weeks ago. Endoscopic procedures, including biopsy, debridement, decompression, and drainage, were performed. One week after endoscopic treatment, her symptoms of back pain and nerve palsy improved significantly. The patient underwent conservative treatment with appropriate antibiotics according to bacterial culture test results. Six weeks postoperatively, spontaneous fusion between L2 and L3 was observed.

**Keywords**: Vertebroplasty, Spinal infection, Spondylodiscitis, Endoscopic surgery

## Introduction

Percutaneous vertebroplasty is a widely used treatment for osteoporotic spinal fractures. However, as the number of vertebroplasties increases, complications such as pyogenic spondylodiscitis can occur. The main treatment for infected vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty is operative debridement and stabilization. Yet, invasive surgeries, including anterior debridement, corpectomy, and posterior spinal fusion, carry a high perioperative morbidity, especially in elderly or medically compromised patients. In contrast, minimally invasive surgery may be a safer and effective option. We report a case of minimally invasive endoscopic treatment for pyogenic spondylodiscitis following vertebroplasty.

## Case Presentation

A 60-year-old female was transferred with right buttock and leg pain, unable to raise her right leg for 1 day. The patient had a medical history of rheumatoid arthritis and had undergone vertebroplasty for an L3 osteoporotic compression fracture 6 weeks prior. She complained of worsening back pain after the procedure. Physical examination revealed right hip flexion weakness (Grade 2), but no other motor impairment. Lab results showed a white blood cell (WBC) count of 16,800 cells/L and C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 3.3 mg/L. Plain radiographs demonstrated destruction and collapse of the L3 body with leaked cement. MRI showed L2/3 spondylitis with a left-sided psoas abscess and discitis, consistent with acute pyogenic spondylodiscitis.

The patient underwent endoscopic treatment using a posterolateral percutaneous approach under local anesthesia and conscious sedation. A spinal needle was inserted into the targeted disc, and the abscess was aspirated for microorganism cultures. Endoscopic procedures, including debridement of infected disc material, necrotic bone, and foreign body (cement), were performed. Large cement fragments were removed with the endoscope. A drainage tube was inserted into the debrided disc space, connected to a negative-pressure pump. One week after the procedure, the patient showed significant improvement in symptoms. The drainage tube was removed after 10 days, and antibiotic treatment was started based on bacterial culture results. Six weeks postoperatively, the patient was pain-free with no neurological deficits, and spontaneous fusion between L2 and L3 was observed.

## Discussion

Spinal infections following vertebroplasty are rare but significant. The infection rate after vertebroplasty has been reported to range from 0.5% to 1.9%. Traditional treatment involves operative debridement and stabilization, but these procedures can be invasive and have high morbidity, especially for older or immunocompromised patients. Advances in minimally invasive techniques, particularly endoscopic spine surgery, have made it possible to treat spinal infections with lower morbidity. Several studies have shown that percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (PELD) is effective for treating spinal infections, providing good outcomes with less morbidity than open surgery.

Endoscopic treatment for vertebroplasty-induced spondylodiscitis is still rare. Although the cement attached to bone is typically difficult to remove with endoscopic techniques, advances in endoscopic instruments have made it feasible. The success of endoscopic treatment depends on the operator's skill and the stage of infection. In some cases, reoperation may be necessary if the infection recurs, but endoscopic surgery through the original incision site can be an effective option. This case demonstrates that endoscopic treatment can be a viable alternative to open surgery for treating pyogenic spondylodiscitis following vertebroplasty.

## Conclusions

Based on the results from this case, we propose that percutaneous endoscopic treatment is an effective minimally invasive method for treating pyogenic spondylodiscitis after vertebroplasty.
