# Recurrent Anterior Cervical Wound Abscesses Following Cervical Corpectomy and Fusion Surgery from an Odontogenic Source Mimicking an Esophageal Perforation: A Case Report

## Abstract

### Background Context
Infection is an uncommon complication of anterior cervical spine surgery. Most deep postoperative infections are thought to be related to occult esophageal perforation. Direct inoculation from the oropharynx has not been previously reported in the literature.

### Purpose
The purpose of this study is to report a case of recurrent infection after anterior cervical decompression and fusion suspected to have resulted from direct communication between the oropharynx and deep neck space.

### Study Design
This study is a case report.

### Methods
This study included longitudinal clinical and radiological follow-up.

### Results
A 48-year-old female who underwent anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion from C3–6 and posterior spinal fusion from C3–7 presented at two weeks and five months postoperatively with a deep neck space infection. She underwent surgical debridement each time. Workup of the second infection found a subtle cortical breach in the mandible at the site of prior invasive dental work.

### Conclusions
This case describes the workup and management of a patient who presented with recurrent deep neck space infection following anterior cervical spine surgery. This is the first report of a postoperative infection related to direct breach of the mandible, with communication between the oropharynx and deep neck space via a cortical defect.

Keywords: cervical fusion; recurrent abscess; odontogenic infection; cervical spine.

## Introduction

Anterior cervical decompression and fusion is a well-accepted treatment for patients with cervical radiculopathy and myelopathy, comprising over 100,000 procedures in the United States between 2002 and 2009 [1,2]. The most common complications are dysphagia, wound hematoma, and recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy [3]. Wound infections and abscesses are rare, with a reported incidence between 0.1–1.6% [3–5], and are typically associated with esophageal perforations [6]. We report a patient who developed recurrent wound abscesses after an anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion.

## Case Report

A 48-year-old female with symptomatic cervical kyphosis, stenosis, and myeloradiculopathy underwent an uncomplicated anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion from C3–6 with fibular strut grafting and posterior spinal fusion from C3–7 using iliac crest autograft. Vancomycin 1 g was given preoperatively and for twenty-four hours postoperatively. An anterior Jackson-Pratt drain was removed on postoperative day one, and she was discharged home without complication on postoperative day three.

Two weeks later, she reported purulent drainage from the drain site with neck movement and swallowing but denied dysphagia or odynophagia. She presented with swelling over her healed incision with frank pus expressible from the drain site. A surgical incision and drainage was performed, and a 2 × 2 cm abscess was found underneath the platysma. The esophagus was fully intact on external visualization as well as on esophagoscopy by the otolaryngology service. Cultures grew microaerophilic streptococci, which the infectious disease service determined had originated from the oropharynx. A negative barium esophagram ruled out occult esophageal injury. It was later revealed that she had undergone a tooth extraction and root canal within one month prior to her index surgery. The patient improved with two weeks of oral moxifloxacin.

Five months later, she returned with a 3 cm mass in the anterior cervical wound area that was erythematous and tender to palpation. White blood cell count and C-reactive protein were elevated. By the next morning, her neck swelling had doubled and spread across the midline with extension of erythema to the mandible. Another workup for esophageal perforation was initiated, and a barium esophagram was again negative. Further questioning revealed that she had three abscessed teeth extracted within six weeks and two fillings placed within days of symptom onset. Computed tomography scan of the mandible demonstrated a lateral cortical breach at the site of a prior tooth extraction adjacent to a recently filled tooth, resulting in a potential tract between the oropharynx and neck. The abscess was debrided, intraoperative cultures were negative, and the infectious disease service recommended six weeks of oral moxifloxacin. Oral and maxillofacial surgery recommended against surgical treatment of the breach or inpatient treatment of her abscessed teeth. She subsequently underwent outpatient extraction of her remaining infected teeth. She has had no further recurrence of infection in the two years since her second debridement.

## Discussion

Infections following anterior cervical decompression and fusion are rare, with a reported incidence between 0.1–1.6% [3–5]. Most cases are associated with esophageal injuries, but two cases of late infection have been reported with unclear etiologies. In one case, a retroesophageal abscess developed four years after a single-level fusion [7], while another reported a prevertebral abscess two years after a single-level fusion [6]. In both cases, no esophageal perforation was identified and the source of infection remained unclear.

In this case, the patient developed abscesses twice within six months of the index surgery. During workup of the first abscess, no esophageal perforation was found, but an organism associated with the oropharynx was identified. Therefore, the initial infection was likely due to hematogenous spread, as she had a tooth extraction four weeks and a root canal nineteen days prior to her index surgery. The patient returned five months later with a recurrent abscess temporally related to invasive dental work. It was initially hypothesized that this recurrence was again caused by hematogenous spread of infection, but a CT scan of her mandible demonstrated perforation of the lateral mandibular cortex, creating a potential path for direct local spread to the neck.

The anatomy of deep neck space infections is well studied. The cervical fascia is comprised of superficial and deep layers, which create potential spaces in the neck [8]. The superficial layer of the deep cervical fascia generally forms the superficial border of deep neck space infections. If this layer is intact, infectious material is often unable to extravasate and can accumulate to the point of spreading into the mediastinum or causing airway compromise. The middle layer of the deep fascia, or pretracheal fascia, forms the base of deep neck space infections. Odontogenic infections typically communicate with the submandibular space, which is also bordered by the superficial layer of the deep fascia. A breach of the outer mandibular cortex would lead to direct communication with the submandibular space and therefore the deep space of the neck. Deep neck infections from the submandibular space have an odontogenic source in 85–100% of cases [9,10].

This case represents a rare complication of anterior cervical decompression and fusion. While some infections after this procedure have been attributed to esophageal perforations, no evidence of such an injury could be found here. A thorough history raised suspicion for an odontogenic source, and a communicating tract between the oropharynx and deep space of the neck was identified on CT imaging. This is the first report of an anterior cervical wound infection caused by direct spread from an odontogenic source. Given the close anatomic relationship between the mandible, the submandibular space, and the surgical planes involved in deep neck dissection, there is value in carefully investigating a patient’s dental history and recognizing the possibility of direct spread from the oropharynx, particularly in the setting of invasive dental procedures.
