# Delayed Presentation of Infected Common Iliac Artery Pseudoaneurysm Caused by Malpositioned Pedicle Screw After Minimally Invasive Scoliosis Surgery

## Abstract

**Purpose**: To report delayed onset common iliac artery perforation and infected pseudoaneurysm caused by malpositioned pedicle screw after minimally invasive scoliosis surgery (MISS).  
**Methods**: A 21-year-old female was referred to our hospital with a 1-week history of abrupt right-sided low back pain, lower abdominal pain, and fever, with a history of MISS using cannulated pedicle screws 18 months earlier. Paravertebral arterial erosion with pseudoaneurysm and retroperitoneal and paraspinal abscess were suspected.  
**Results**: We performed resection of the pseudoaneurysm, vascular repair of the right common iliac artery by angioplasty with a bovine patch, and removal of the implant. At 6 months after the last surgery, she had no limitations or problems in her daily activities, with no recurrence of low back pain, abdominal pain, or fever, as well as no loss of deformity.  
**Conclusions**: Our case showed that misplaced pedicle screws can cause potentially fatal complications, such as infected pseudoaneurysm, even in the late postoperative period.

**Keywords**: Abscess, Common iliac artery, Fistula, Pseudoaneurysm, Malpositioned cannulated pedicle screw, Minimally invasive scoliosis surgery

## Introduction

Pedicle screw instrumentation has been used to treat various complex spinal disorders, including spinal deformity correction. However, damage to major vessels, including the aorta and iliac arteries, due to pedicle screw fixation has been reported. Most acute major vessel injuries cause sudden hemodynamic instability, which may inadvertently lead to a life-threatening condition. More rarely, however, major vessel injuries after pedicle screw misplacement may be found with delayed symptom presentation or incidental findings in asymptomatic patients.

We experienced delayed onset common iliac artery perforation and infected pseudoaneurysm caused by a malpositioned pedicle screw after minimally invasive scoliosis surgery (MISS). Our report highlights that even posterior instrumentation surgery of the lower lumbar vertebra may cause major arterial injury in the late postoperative period, and a malpositioned cannulated screw could be a facilitating conduit to the spread of infection.

## Materials and Methods

A 21-year-old female was referred to our hospital with a 1-week history of abrupt right-sided low back pain, lower abdominal pain, and fever. About 18 months previously, she had undergone minimally invasive scoliosis surgery (MISS) for the treatment of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis using a cannulated pedicle screw system from T4 to L4. Abdomino-pelvic computed tomography (AP-CT) revealed a collection of pre- and paravertebral fluid surrounding a rim-like enhancement around L3-5, containing lobulated spaces filled with high-density contrast material, predominantly at the L4–5 level. In addition, a cystic lesion with a similar appearance was observed around the adjacent paraspinal muscle area, from T12 to the right S1 level.

Subsequent lumbar spine X-ray and CT revealed that the right L4 screw was positioned too far anteriorly with periscrew halo formation, adjacent vertebral body erosion, and detachment of the transverse process. The medical records and postoperative radiographs from the hospital where the first operation was performed confirmed the surgical details. No specific finding on pre- and postoperative periods indicated infection except for the long operation time (about 10 hours).

Clinically, paravertebral arterial erosion with pseudoaneurysm and retroperitoneal and paraspinal abscess were suspected, and surgical treatment was inevitable. We used an interdisciplinary single-session two-step approach with a vascular surgery team. The first step involved retroperitoneal exploration through an anterior midline abdominal incision. Intraoperatively, a 3-mm perforation at the posterior wall of the right common iliac artery and a pseudoaneurysm behind the artery with surrounding abscess were identified. After drainage of the abscess, vascular repair of the right common iliac artery was performed with a bovine patch angioplasty. The second step included removal of the right-sided instrument and paravertebral abscess drainage. 

Postoperatively, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) was identified in the tissue culture from the abscess, and the patient was treated with intravenous ciprofloxacin. At postoperative day seven, the patient recovered to ambulation without any assistance and was discharged without pain or fever. At the 6-month follow-up after the last surgery, she had no limitations or problems in her daily activities, with no recurrence of low back pain, abdominal pain, or fever. 

## Discussion

The rate of pedicle screw misplacement varies from 5% to 41% for the lumbar spine, depending on underlying pathology, surgical technique used, and the site of screw fixation. The pedicles of the lumbar spine are larger than those of the thoracic spine, reducing the risk of screw misplacement in the lumbar spine. However, complications from lumbar screw misplacement have been consistently reported.

Major vascular injury is one of the most fatal complications of malpositioned pedicle screws. The overall reported incidence of major vascular injury during spine surgery is less than 0.01%. Major vessel injury can remain undiscovered even in follow-up and may become symptomatic in the late postoperative period. The patient may be asymptomatic for months or years after the initial operation. Delayed presentation is most commonly associated with pseudoaneurysm and arteriovenous fistula.

In our case, the patient presented a sudden symptom about 18 months after MISS. While MISS offers multiple benefits, such as less blood loss and shorter hospital stays, it can also lead to complications, such as screw loosening or malposition. The initial entry point of the misplaced screw was targeted too laterally, and the screw tip was placed too far anteriorly, possibly in contact with the common iliac artery. This poor bone-screw anchorage likely resulted in continuous and repetitive motion around the misplaced screw, leading to chronic erosive damage to the adjacent iliac artery and contributing to the formation of a delayed pseudoaneurysm.
