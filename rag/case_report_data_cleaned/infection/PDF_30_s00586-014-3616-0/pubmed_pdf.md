# Spontaneous Epidural Spinal Haematoma in Children Caused by Vascular Malformations

## Abstract

**Purpose**  
The occurrence of spinal epidural haematoma of spontaneous origin in adults is a well-documented entity, though it is rare in children. In the literature to date, there are few cases of this kind of spontaneous haematoma proven to be due to an underlying vascular abnormality.

**Methods**  
Retrospective review of two cases of children under 15 years of age with spontaneous epidural spinal haematoma.

**Results**  
Underlying arteriovenous malformations were identified in both cases. Intra-operative findings and histological sections of these anomalies are presented.

**Conclusion**  
These are the first two such cases described with clinico-pathological correlation.

## Introduction

Spontaneous epidural spinal haematoma (SESH) is a rare cause of spinal cord compression in children [1]. Although well recognised in adults, reports of this entity in the paediatric population are few and are often ascribed to idiopathic causation. We present two children with their surgical management, outcomes, and pathological features.

## Case 1

A previously healthy 8-year-old girl developed severe neck pain while dancing. She reported feeling a “pop” as the pain developed. Over a 6-week period, she became increasingly lethargic with progressively worsening mobility. After a further week, she attended the emergency department of her local district general hospital unable to walk.

Clinical examination on admission revealed Medical Research Council (MRC) grade 3/5 spastic tetraparesis and urinary retention. Spinal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed an epidural lesion compressing the thecal sac on the right from C4/5 to C7/T1 levels. The lesion was centrally hyperintense on T1-weighted imaging with a peripheral rim of lower signal intensity, consistent with an acute or subacute epidural haematoma. No other abnormality was seen in the neuroaxis. All haematological investigations on admission were within normal limits.

The patient underwent urgent C5–C7 laminoplasty and evacuation of the haematoma. Significant bleeding was encountered from abnormally dilated arterialised epidural veins, which was rapidly controlled by diathermy. These veins extended caudal to the laminotomy. As no vascular imaging was available to define the vascular architecture, the exposure was not extended further within the immature spine. With the existing exposure, the abnormal vessels were dissected from the dural sac and excellent haemostasis was achieved. Once the veins at the level of exposure had been excised, bleeding from the remaining veins was no longer arterial.

Post-operatively, the child awoke immediately and made a good neurological recovery over the subsequent 3 weeks. At discharge, she was independently ambulant with normal bladder function. A post-operative time-resolved imaging of contrast kinetics (TRICKS) MRI scan demonstrated no residual haematoma or abnormal vasculature.

Histological examination revealed blood clot and a complex arrangement of vessels with varying wall thickness, including venous-like structures with fibrous thickening and arterialised vessels with thinner walls. These features were consistent with a vascular malformation, in keeping with an arteriovenous malformation (AVM).

## Case 2

An otherwise healthy 13-year-old boy developed sudden-onset interscapular pain with no obvious precipitating cause. The following day, he developed progressively severe lower limb weakness and urinary retention. He was admitted to a local children’s hospital, where neurological examination revealed paraplegia (MRC grade 0/5) with areflexia, loss of anal tone, and a T4 sensory level.

MRI of the thoracic spine demonstrated a mixed-signal epidural lesion located posteriorly and to the left, extending between C7/T1 and T4/5. The lesion displaced the spinal cord anteriorly, and its signal characteristics were most consistent with an acute or subacute epidural haematoma.

He underwent emergency T1–T5 laminoplasty with evacuation of the epidural haematoma and resection of an AVM. Histological analysis revealed fragments of haemorrhagic tissue containing vascular channels with abnormally thick and irregular walls, with areas of marked thinning, consistent with an arteriovenous malformation.

Post-operatively, the patient’s neurological deficit improved to MRC grade 4/5. At discharge, he experienced intermittent urinary incontinence but was able to walk independently. By 3 months post-operatively, he was independently ambulant with only very mild right lower limb weakness evident on close gait examination, although intermittent urinary incontinence persisted. Follow-up MRI demonstrated no flow voids or abnormal contrast enhancement to suggest residual vascular malformation.

## Discussion

Spontaneous epidural spinal haematoma is an uncommon cause of spinal cord compression in children, with 37 cases previously reported in the English literature. Clinical presentation may be variable or non-specific, making diagnosis challenging. Spinal pain at the level of the haematoma is the most common presenting symptom and may or may not be accompanied by radicular pain [2,3]. In some cases, irritability may be the only early feature. Misdiagnosis has been reported, highlighting the importance of timely and appropriate imaging [4].

MRI is considered the gold standard for diagnosing spinal epidural haematomas [3,5]. It allows identification of the haematoma, estimation of its age, and may suggest the presence of an underlying vascular malformation. TRICKS MRI provides high-resolution vascular imaging and is particularly useful in evaluating spinal AVMs, often obviating the need for digital subtraction angiography, which is more invasive and technically demanding.

In the first case, there was a prolonged interval between symptom onset and neurological deterioration. Previous reports describe pain preceding neurological signs by hours to days [2]. Delayed or recurrent presentations have also been described, sometimes without identification of an underlying vascular lesion at initial surgery or imaging [6,7].

In children, epidural haematomas are most commonly dorsal and frequently occur between C5 and T1 vertebral levels [8]. An underlying abnormality is identified in only 50–60% of cases [9], and bleeding is generally thought to be venous in origin. Activities that alter intra-abdominal or intrathoracic pressure may precipitate bleeding from the valveless epidural venous plexus [2,8]. Other potential causes include coagulopathies, AVMs, or neoplasms, although in many cases no definitive cause is found [10].

The intra-operative finding of abnormal epidural veins, as seen in the first case, has been reported previously [11]. However, identification of an AVM as the underlying cause of SESH is particularly rare, accounting for approximately 2% of spinal lesions in children under 10 years of age [12]. Even when suspected, vascular malformations may not always be demonstrable on imaging or at surgery [7,9].

Spinal digital subtraction angiography is generally reserved for cases in which vascular malformations are suggested on MRI [1,13]. Delayed angiography may occasionally demonstrate abnormalities not seen on earlier studies, although surgical exploration may still fail to identify a lesion.

The presence of a vascular malformation should be considered in any child presenting with spontaneous epidural haematoma, even in the absence of clear MRI evidence of abnormal flow voids. Given the potential for significant arterial bleeding, surgical evacuation is best undertaken by experienced surgeons. These two cases represent the first reported instances of spontaneous epidural spinal haematoma in children with confirmed clinico-pathological correlation. The optimal duration and nature of follow-up, as well as the natural history of asymptomatic lesions, remain to be defined.
