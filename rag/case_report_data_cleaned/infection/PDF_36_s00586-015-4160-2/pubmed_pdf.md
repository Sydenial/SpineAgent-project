# Nontraumatic spinal subdural hematoma complicating direct factor Xa inhibitor treatment (rivaroxaban): a challenging management

## Abstract

**Purpose**  
We report on a 72-year-old male patient who developed a nontraumatic spinal subdural hematoma (SSDH) during rivaroxaban therapy, a relatively new orally administered direct factor Xa inhibitor.

**Case description**  
The patient sustained a sudden onset of interscapular pain, followed by gait impairment and paraplegia. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the spine demonstrated SSDH from T6 to T8. Laboratory tests revealed a high rivaroxaban level, associated with a major hemorrhagic risk. Surgery was therefore performed the following morning, after normalization of coagulation parameters.

**Conclusion**  
Determining the time of safe surgery remains challenging when hemorrhagic complications happen with direct factor Xa inhibitors, especially when neurological prognosis is engaged. Spinal subdural hematoma has not previously been reported following rivaroxaban therapy.

## Introduction

Spinal subdural hematoma is a rare entity (about 3% of all spinal hematomas) and is far less frequent than spinal epidural hematoma or subarachnoid hemorrhage [1].

The well-known limitations of vitamin K antagonists (VKA) in clinical practice, especially the need for frequent laboratory monitoring (INR), have led to the development of new oral anticoagulants that do not require such burdensome monitoring. Rivaroxaban is a new orally administered direct factor Xa inhibitor and is increasingly prescribed. The targeting of factor Xa, a key component in the coagulation cascade, has the benefit of being an effective antithrombotic therapy with a lesser potential risk of bleeding, both highly dose-dependent. Nevertheless, complications may occur, and spinal epidural hematoma complicating rivaroxaban medication has already been described [2].

We report here the first case, to our knowledge, of nontraumatic spinal subdural hematoma complicating rivaroxaban therapy.

## Case Report

A 72-year-old man was admitted to our institution 24 hours after a sudden onset of interscapular pain associated with rapidly worsening gait impairment. He had received rivaroxaban (20 mg per day) for 3 years for atrial fibrillation. This medication had been chosen to avoid INR monitoring. His latest intake was the day before symptom onset.

Clinical examination revealed flaccid paraplegia with bilateral Babinski signs, thermoalgic hypoesthesia predominantly in the left lower limb with a T6 sensory level. Knee and ankle reflexes were absent bilaterally. Rectal examination showed decreased anal tone.

Spinal MRI demonstrated, on sagittal sequences, heterogeneous signal intensity consistent with blood, possibly subdural, from T6 to T8. Axial slices showed inner lobulations with right anterolateral predominance and associated posterior and lateral displacement of the spinal cord. No abnormal spinal cord signal was present, and epidural fat was preserved. The diagnosis of thoracic spinal subdural hematoma from T6 to T8 was made. Neither MR angiography of cranial vessels nor spinal MRI revealed any vascular malformation.

Laboratory tests revealed a rivaroxaban level, measured by chromogenic anti-Xa activity testing, of 331 ng/ml (reported $C_{\max}$ range approximately 40–400 ng/ml), an activated clotting time (ACT) of 31.6, and a prothrombin time (PT) of 50%.

Two hours after the initial measurement, neurological deficits were unchanged, and repeat testing still showed a high rivaroxaban level (301 ng/ml), associated with a high per-procedural hemorrhagic risk. Surgery was therefore delayed, and coagulation parameters were first normalized using prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC).

Surgery was performed the following morning, when the rivaroxaban level had decreased to 127 ng/ml. A thoracic laminectomy from T6 to T8 was carried out, with opening of the dura mater at these levels. An intradural collected hematoma was immediately visible and was released from the dentate ligament and nerve roots. After evacuation, the spinal cord appeared normal, with no evidence of vascular malformation or neoplasm.

Postoperatively, the patient was treated with intravenous corticosteroids and received physical and occupational therapy throughout his hospital stay. However, despite surgery and appropriate care, only mild sensory improvement was observed after 6 months of follow-up.

The final diagnosis was spinal subdural hematoma complicating rivaroxaban therapy, based on clinical presentation supported by radiological, biological, and surgical findings.

## Discussion

Although spontaneous or nontraumatic spinal subdural hematomas are occasionally reported in the literature, they remain rare in clinical practice [3].

Symptoms of SSDH include sudden-onset back pain, sometimes radiating to the limbs or trunk, weakness, numbness, and urinary or fecal incontinence. Autonomic disturbances may also be present [4].

Anatomical considerations regarding the spinal canal provide useful clues for diagnosing lesions related to specific spinal compartments and aid radiological diagnosis of SSDH. Subdural hematomas are contained by the dura, do not encroach on epidural fat, may show inner lobulations, and can displace nerve roots. They occur most commonly at the thoracic level. T1- and T2-weighted signal characteristics vary depending on the age of the hematoma [5]. In the acute stage, the hematoma may have signal intensity similar to cerebrospinal fluid, potentially misleading T2-weighted imaging. Subdural hematomas generally extend more cranio-caudally than epidural ones, usually over 2–3 vertebral levels [6]. They often show a crescent shape, although biconvex, circumferential, or mixed morphologies are possible. The “Mercedes-Benz sign” refers to encasement of the filum terminale and exiting nerve roots, resulting in a star-like appearance when the lumbar region is involved [7].

In some cases, subacute SSDH may mimic spinal epidural lipomatosis on MRI, with high signal intensity on both T1- and T2-weighted images [8]. Fat-suppressed T1-weighted imaging is helpful in these situations. In contrast, epidural hematomas displace the dura, efface epidural fat, contact bone, and often have a lentiform or convex appearance. On sagittal views, a “cap” of epidural fat may be visible at the upper and lower margins. Gadolinium enhancement at the acute stage is possible [9].

No consensus exists regarding the management of patients receiving new antithrombotic medications who require urgent surgery. In our case, waiting for a second plasma measurement was initially chosen because the neurological deficit was stable and the rivaroxaban level was high. Surgical evacuation was then rapidly decided to avoid significant clinical worsening and potentially improve neurological outcome.

For planned surgery, proposals from the French Society of Anesthesiology and Reanimation suggest the following [10]: a rivaroxaban plasma concentration below 30 ng/ml is considered an acceptable safety threshold for surgery [11]; for concentrations between 30 and 200 ng/ml, waiting 12 hours and reassessing levels is recommended, with possible use of PCC or FEIBA if surgery cannot be delayed and bleeding risk is significant; for concentrations between 200 and 400 ng/ml, surgery should be postponed as long as possible, with reassessment after 12–24 hours; levels above 400 ng/ml indicate overdose with major bleeding risk, and surgery is not recommended.

When specific rivaroxaban assays are unavailable, normal PT and ACT are considered reliable indicators of plasma concentrations below 30 ng/ml. Anti-Xa activity may also be useful, as activity ≤0.1 U/ml indicates absence of active drug.

The interval between symptom onset and surgical intervention is critical for neurological recovery [1]. In this case, surgery was delayed briefly due to high bleeding risk and stable neurological status, but it was unlikely that improvement would occur without evacuation. Surgery was therefore performed the day after symptom onset.

## Conclusion

Nontraumatic spinal subdural hematoma should be considered in patients receiving anticoagulant therapy who present with abrupt onset of signs and symptoms of acute spinal cord or cauda equina compression. Determining the timing of safe surgery remains challenging when hemorrhagic complications occur with direct factor Xa inhibitors, particularly when neurological prognosis is at stake.

The availability of a specific antidote to reverse rivaroxaban would be highly valuable in emergency surgical situations. New molecules such as idarucizumab, andexanet alfa, and ciraparantag show promising data and may soon become available for clinical use [12].
