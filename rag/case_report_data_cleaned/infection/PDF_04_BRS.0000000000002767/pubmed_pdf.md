# Post Dengue Fever Epidural Abscess With or Without Spondylodiscitis: Case Series of Eight Cases  
Does the Dengue Have a Predilection for the Neural Tissue?

## Abstract

### Study Design
A retrospective case series.

### Objective
To report spondylodiscitis with or without epidural abscess occurring within 6 weeks following dengue viral infection and to explore the possibility of post-dengue immunosuppression and secondary bacterial infection, with a potential predilection for neural tissue/spine involvement.

### Summary of Background Data
This is the largest case series of epidural abscess with or without spondylodiscitis within 6 weeks following dengue virus infection.

### Methods
We performed a retrospective review of eight cases treated for epidural abscess with or without spondylodiscitis within 6 weeks following dengue virus infection in our center during a 3-month period from June 2017 to August 2017.

### Results
Of the eight cases, three were infected with Methicillin Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and all of them grew the organism in blood culture. Three had Methicillin Sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA), including one with MSSA septicemia; one had associated Elizabethkingia meningoseptica infection; and one patient had Acid Fast Bacilli (AFB) positivity on smear. Two patients were infected with Pseudomonas. Some patients had multisite abscesses and epidural abscess without spondylodiscitis. Five had neurological deficit, which recovered with abscess drainage and antibiotic treatment. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), and serum ferritin were elevated.

### Conclusion
This is a large case series of epidural abscess with or without spondylodiscitis within 6 weeks following dengue virus infection. The predilection of dengue virus for neural tissue should be further investigated. Post-dengue immunosuppression also needs further study.

### Key Words
Acid Fast Bacilli; dengue virus; Elizabethkingia meningoseptica; epidural abscess; immunosuppression; Methicillin Resistant Staphylococcus aureus; Methicillin Sensitive Staphylococcus aureus; Pseudomonas; retrospective study; spondylodiscitis.

## Introduction
Dengue fever is caused by dengue virus of the Flaviviridae family and is a pandemic disease spread by Aedes aegypti mosquitoes. Dengue fever has a wide range of manifestations, both typical and atypical. Some authors state that unusual presentations may be due to concurrent infections caused by fungi, bacteria, or protozoa. Recently, clinical reports have been emerging on possible neurological complications associated with dengue. Unusual presentations include rhabdomyolysis, acute renal failure, encephalopathy, and Guillain-Barré syndrome.

Associated bacterial infection may be considered if fever goes beyond 7 days along with elevated ESR and CRP. Here, we report spondylodiscitis with or without epidural abscess in patients with a recent history (within 6 weeks) of dengue fever. From June 2017 to August 2017, the total number of dengue cases reported or diagnosed in our hospital was 1407. Eight developed dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF). Nine cases presented in our department with recurrence of fever and were diagnosed as spondylodiscitis with or without epidural abscess. These patients presented with variable severity of fever and back pain.

## Methods
We performed a retrospective review of all cases treated for epidural abscess with or without spondylodiscitis within 6 weeks following dengue virus infection in our center during the period of June 2017 to August 2017.

## Immunosuppression Due to Dengue Virus
The neuropathogenesis in dengue infection is poorly understood. It may be caused directly by the virus or by postinfectious immune-mediated injury. One study suggests that postinfectious onset of neurological symptoms and flaccid paraplegia can be due to direct invasion of neural tissue by the virus, whereas late-onset neurological symptoms and spastic paraplegia are attributed to immunologically mediated neurological injury.

In experimental studies, dengue virus has been shown to reduce cell-mediated immunity. Major factors include reduced lymphocyte proliferation and inhibition of leukocyte migration. In vitro, splenic T lymphocytes from dengue virus–infected mice produce a cytotoxic factor (CF) that kills various immune cells (mainly macrophages, helper T cells, and T effector lymphocytes). Survived macrophages produce another cytotoxic substance (CF2), amplifying the cytotoxic effect. Induction of an antigen-specific suppression pathway has also been demonstrated in vivo, involving sequential recruitment of suppressor T-cell subpopulations and suppression of IgM antibody-forming cells. Human leukocytes treated in vitro with CF and CF2 show diminished phagocytic activity; similar effects were observed in monkeys inoculated with CF.

Thrombocytopenia and leucopenia are reported in dengue. Platelet count decreases from the 3rd day up to the 7th day. The actual mechanisms of virus-induced bone marrow suppression during the acute phase remain unclear. In early disease, bone marrow shows hypocellularity and arrest of megakaryocytic maturation. Histopathological studies of bone marrow from patients with severe dengue infection suggest marrow suppression characterized by generalized hypoplasia by the 4th day, returning to normal by 7 to 10 days.

## Spine
No recorded data are available on the incidence of epidural abscess in patients with dengue fever.

## Case Report

### Case 1
A 63-year-old man with diabetes mellitus was diagnosed with dengue infection (positive NS1 antigen test) and treated symptomatically elsewhere. Six weeks later, he was admitted with high-grade fever, swelling over the left sternoclavicular joint, swelling of the right knee, and low backache radiating to both lower limbs. There was a $2\times1$ cm abscess over the left sternoclavicular joint and effusion in the right knee joint. Blood culture, knee aspirate, and pus from the sternoclavicular joint yielded MRSA sensitive to meropenem and teicoplanin. MRI of the lumbosacral spine showed epidural abscess at L4–5 extending up to S3 and paraspinal muscle abscess from L4 to S2. He underwent right-sided hemilaminectomy at L4–5 and L5–S1 with drainage of epidural and paraspinal abscess. MRSA was isolated from culture. There was no spondylodiscitis; only epidural and paraspinal abscess were present. He was treated with meropenem 1 g IV three times daily for 2 weeks and teicoplanin 600 mg IV for 6 weeks; rifampicin was added after discussion with the clinical microbiologist. Blood culture again showed MRSA at 2-week review, so teicoplanin was increased to 800 mg IV. At 6 weeks, repeat MRI showed no collection, and the patient was asymptomatic.

### Case 2
A 62-year-old woman was diagnosed with dengue fever (NS1 antigen positive) and treated symptomatically elsewhere. Six weeks later, she developed gradual onset neck pain and numbness of the right upper limb; she was afebrile. There was tenderness over the cervicothoracic junction with right grip weakness (grade 3, MRC grading) and paresthesia over C7 and C8 dermatomes. Blood culture showed no growth. MRI cervical spine showed C7–T1 discitis with epidural and paravertebral abscess extending from C7 to T1. She underwent abscess drainage, C7–T1 discectomy, and interbody fusion with iliac crest bone graft without instrumentation via an anterior approach. Culture yielded MSSA. AFB staining for pus was positive; AFB culture was negative at 6 weeks. She was treated with clindamycin 600 mg twice daily per sensitivity, and anti-tuberculous therapy (INH, rifampicin, ethambutol, pyrazinamide). Neurological status and blood parameters improved. Clindamycin was given for 6 weeks; anti-tuberculous therapy continued for 1 year. At 6 weeks, she had completely recovered and follow-up MRI showed no abscess.

### Case 3
A 75-year-old man was diagnosed with dengue fever (positive NS1 antigen) and treated symptomatically elsewhere. After 1 week of being well, he developed low backache and low-grade fever. Blood culture yielded no growth. MRI lumbosacral spine showed spondylodiscitis at L3–4 with prevertebral, paravertebral, and epidural abscess extending from L3 to L4. He underwent left hemilaminectomy, abscess drainage, and L3–4 discectomy. Culture grew Pseudomonas species. He was treated with IV piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g three times daily for 6 weeks; levofloxacin was added for 3 weeks. At 6-week follow-up, he was clinically and radiologically well.

### Case 4
A 71-year-old man was diagnosed with dengue fever (positive NS1 antigen) and treated symptomatically elsewhere. Four weeks later, he developed acute neck pain and tingling in both upper limbs, with tenderness over the lower neck. He had grip weakness bilaterally (grade 3) and sensory blunting over C5, C6, and C7 dermatomes. MRI showed C5–6 discitis with epidural abscess causing spinal cord compression at C5–6 and C6–7 levels, with prevertebral and paravertebral collection from C3 to T1. Blood culture grew MRSA. He underwent abscess drainage, C5–6 discectomy, and fusion with iliac crest graft; culture grew MRSA. Although neurologically normal before surgery, 24 hours postoperatively he developed acute quadriplegia (Frankel grade B). Repeat MRI showed spinal cord signal changes from C3 to D2 with epidural collection from C4 to D1. Diffusion-weighted MRI suggested cord ischemia, possibly due to anterior spinal artery thrombosis from septic emboli, or demyelination secondary to dengue infection. Neuromedicine consultation was obtained and anti-dengue immunoglobulin was given. Re-exploration was performed the next day with C6–7 discectomy, drainage of abscess, and anterior interbody fusion with iliac crest graft without instrumentation. MRSA sensitive to teicoplanin was identified. He was treated with teicoplanin 600 mg IV once daily plus rifampicin 450 mg once daily, and physiotherapy. He improved gradually; at 3 months he could walk independently but not completely. Blood parameters normalized.

### Case 5
A 65-year-old man was diagnosed with dengue fever (positive NS1 antigen) and treated symptomatically elsewhere. Two weeks later, he developed acute tachypnea and desaturation and was diagnosed with secondary lung infection and right pleural effusion, requiring intubation and ICU care. Blood cultures yielded MRSA and he was diagnosed with MRSA septicemia. He was treated with meropenem 1 g IV three times daily and teicoplanin 600 mg IV. He developed acute kidney injury, metabolic acidosis, hemodynamic instability, and uremic encephalopathy, requiring hemodialysis. MRI spine showed spondylodiscitis at L2–3 with prevertebral, paravertebral, epidural, and bilateral psoas abscess; an enhancing epidural lesion extended from D12 to L2. He underwent hemilaminectomy at L2–3 with epidural abscess drainage and L2–3 discectomy. Culture yielded MRSA; repeat blood cultures also grew MRSA. He later developed cellulitis of the right hand/forearm and Fournier gangrene requiring debridement; he received linezolid 600 mg IV twice daily. After prolonged ICU stay and rehabilitation, he could walk with support at discharge and blood cultures were negative. Teicoplanin 600 mg IV once daily and rifampicin 600 mg once daily were continued for 8 weeks, extended to 10 weeks due to occasional fever spikes. On follow-up, he was walking normally.

### Case 6
A 49-year-old woman was diagnosed with dengue fever (positive NS1 antigen) and treated symptomatically elsewhere. Two weeks later, she developed acute low backache radiating to the lower limbs and fever. MRI showed spondylodiscitis at L5–S1 with epidural and paravertebral components. She underwent left flavotomy at L5–S1 with drainage of abscess. Pus culture yielded MSSA; blood culture was negative. Two days postoperatively, she developed fever and breathing difficulty. CT thorax showed empyema, aspirated under CT guidance; culture yielded MSSA, and blood culture also showed MSSA. She was treated with cefoperazone-sulbactam, linezolid, and rifampicin per culture sensitivity and microbiology consultation. At 6 weeks, she recovered completely clinically, radiologically, and hematologically.

### Case 7
A 54-year-old woman was diagnosed with dengue fever (positive NS1 antigen) and treated symptomatically elsewhere. One week later, she developed acute low backache radiating to the lower limbs and fever. She had extensor hallucis longus weakness on the left side. MRI showed L4–5 discitis. Blood culture was negative. She underwent flavotomy and L4–5 discectomy. Culture yielded Pseudomonas species. She received cefoperazone-sulbactam and ciprofloxacin for 4 weeks, then oral ciprofloxacin plus trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for another 3 weeks. At 4 weeks, she was asymptomatic.

### Case 8
A 51-year-old man was diagnosed with dengue fever (positive NS1 antigen) and treated symptomatically elsewhere. One month later, he presented with low backache and low-grade fever. MRI showed spondylodiscitis at L3–4 with prevertebral, paravertebral, and epidural soft tissue components. Neurologically, knee extension was grade 4 (right) and ankle dorsiflexion/toe extension were grade 4 (right). He underwent L3 hemilaminotomy with abscess drainage and L3–4 discectomy. Culture yielded Elizabethkingia meningoseptica and Staphylococcus aureus. AFB stain was negative. He was treated with cefoperazone-sulbactam and ciprofloxacin based on sensitivity reports. He improved clinically and became normal after 6 weeks of therapy.

## Discussion
A recent upsurge of dengue fever is a major health concern, with increasing complications and deaths. Here, we report an unusual incidence of epidural abscess with or without spondylodiscitis in post-dengue patients within 6 weeks of the primary illness. During a 3-month period (June 2017 to August 2017), eight patients developed epidural abscess with or without spondylodiscitis, septicemia, and other foci of infection.

Seven patients were treated elsewhere for dengue fever symptomatically, and one was treated at our hospital. Presenting symptoms varied, including stiff back, low-grade fever, neurological deficits, and septicemia. All patients were evaluated hematologically, microbiologically, and radiologically, and underwent MRI to confirm epidural abscess and spondylodiscitis.

Only one patient presented with septic shock and multiple abscesses. Among patients with other site involvement, one had empyema with pleural effusion, and one had left sternoclavicular joint abscess with septic arthritis of the knee. Preoperative neurological deficit was seen in five patients; among these, two had positive blood culture and one had septic shock. Of four positive blood cultures, three were MRSA and one was MSSA.

Most patients underwent hemilaminectomy or flavotomy with decompression and abscess drainage. Cervical spine cases were addressed anteriorly with discectomy, abscess drainage, and iliac crest grafting. Cultures yielded MRSA in three patients, Pseudomonas in two, MSSA in one, MSSA with AFB positivity in one, and MSSA with Elizabethkingia meningoseptica in one. Biopsy showed suppurative discitis and infected tissues in all patients.

All patients except one improved immediately after surgery. One patient developed quadriplegia 24 hours postsurgery despite initial improvement; re-exploration and adjacent-level decompression/fusion were performed, and neurological deficit improved progressively. At follow-up, he was walking independently though slowly, with residual weakness in right hand grip.

Overall neurological outcomes were very good: complete recovery in all except one patient with postoperative quadriplegia (Frankel grade B), who recovered to near-normal by six months. All patients had negative blood cultures after treatment. One patient received anti-tuberculous medicines.

All patients were diagnosed after 1 to 6 weeks following dengue fever, and none received blood or blood products. There is no established reason why dengue virus may have predilection for neural tissue; emergence of a neurotropic serotype needs further investigation.

## Conclusion
This article reports a high incidence of epidural abscess with or without spondylodiscitis in dengue-infected patients after an asymptomatic period of 1 to 6 weeks. Experimental studies suggest suppression of immunity through multiple mechanisms, including inhibition of leukocyte migration and lymphocyte proliferation. Splenic T lymphocytes in infected mice produce cytotoxic factors that kill immune cells but are not effective against bacterial cells, and can mediate antigen-specific suppression of IgM antibody-forming cells.

Early diagnosis and treatment can prevent neurological deficits, septicemia, and death. The neurotropic nature of dengue virus and its effects on immunity require further study. Similar series are not reported in the literature. The predilection of dengue virus to neural tissue and disc space, potential serotype changes, and the presence of multiple foci of infection suggest immune effects that warrant further investigation. Rapid and excellent neurological recovery may relate to early diagnosis and immediate intervention, though whether viral peculiarities contribute remains uncertain.

From our study, there is evidence of immunosuppression manifestations in other tissues/organs: one patient had septic shock, two had empyema with pleural effusion, one had left sternoclavicular joint abscess and septic arthritis of the knee, and one had concomitant tubercular infection.

## Key Points
- This is a large case series of epidural abscess with or without spondylodiscitis within 6 weeks following dengue virus infection.  
- The predilection of dengue virus for neural tissue should be further investigated.  
- Post-dengue immunosuppression also needs further study.
