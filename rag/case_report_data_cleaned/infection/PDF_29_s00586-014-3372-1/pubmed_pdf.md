# Spontaneous atlantoaxial pyogenic arthritis surgically managed

## Abstract

**Purpose:** Septic cervical facet joints arthritis is a rare pathology, usually revealed by fever and neck pain. As symptoms may be indolent, a high index of suspicion needs to be maintained. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is effective for early diagnostic, looking for local spread and guiding potential preoperative planning. We present a case exhibiting an uncommon pathology with possible significant morbidity if misdiagnosed.  
**Methods:** A 75-year-old woman presented with fever, neck stiffness and torticollis accompanied with altered level of consciousness.  
**Results:** The diagnosis was established by computed tomography and MRI, identifying atlantoaxial facet joint destruction with para spinal muscles and epidural abscesses. A surgical treatment consisting in posterior decompression of the spinal canal and atlantoaxial pars articularis fixation known as Harms technique, associated with a targeted antibiotic therapy, succeeded in obtaining favorable clinical course.  
**Conclusion:** Atlantoaxial septic arthritis is an underreported and severe infection. Early use of MRI should avoid delayed diagnosis and would guide the practitioner in choosing an appropriate therapy. Early surgical treatment for uncontrolled sepsis is also a critical element of the prognosis.

**Keywords:** Atlantoaxial, Septic arthritis, Harms fixation

## Introduction

Septic arthritis of the cervical facet joints is a rare clinical entity; only three cases of atlantoaxial arthritis have been reported to date in the English literature [1, 2, 4].  

The diagnosis can easily be missed, as clinical behavior is not specific. Early use of computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) would be helpful to avoid delayed diagnosis and related local/systemic spread of the infection. As concerns complications, sepsis with potential multiple organ dysfunction and epidural abscess formation with subsequent spinal cord impairment may compromise the prognosis.  

We reported the case of a 75-year-old woman affected with a pyogenic C1-C2 arthritis managed with surgical decompression, drainage, and fixation.

## Case report

### History and examination

A 75-year-old Caucasian woman was admitted to our hospital with a 3-week history of inflammatory neck pain and stiffness. She presented at the Emergency Department with fever (39.7°C) and chills, cervicalgia, exquisite left-sided tenderness, and altered level of consciousness. Neurological examination was normal.  

She had received a single corticosteroid injection from her rheumatologist 7 months ago to relieve pain related to degenerative cervical osteoarthritis. The usual described infectious risk factors were not found in her medical history. We noticed an allergy to penicillin, asthma, knee and hip prostheses.  

Initial plain radiographs were not of great diagnostic value. Laboratory tests showed elevated leukocyte count of 19 (normal <10 Giga/L), C-reactive protein of 498 (normal <5 mg/L), and procalcitonin of 0.2 (normal <0.1 ug/L). Blood cultures revealed Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia, motivating the initiation of intravenous Vancomycin therapy owing to the allergy to penicillin.  

Cervical spine contrast-enhanced CT detected atlantoaxial arthritis with massive destruction of the left articular mass, and erosions of the odontoid process. MRI revealed prevertebral, left erector spinae muscles abscesses and anterior epidural collection displacing the spinal cord to the back without abnormal intramedullary signal. A body CT was performed and did not demonstrate other infectious sites.  

Considering persistent fever and intensive pain despite antibiotic and analgesic therapy and severe local complications, a combined medical/surgical management was decided.

### Operation procedure

The surgical procedure was performed under general anesthesia. An occipito-cervical incision was made on the patient positioned in a prone position, and the head fixed on a Mayfield head holder. The soft tissue abscess was drained and the joint cleaned. A C1-C2 laminectomy was performed to the pars articularis on the left side.  

All the screws were inserted under lateral C-arm control, according to Harms technique: two screws in the articular masses of C1 (length = 30 mm), one in the right pars articularis of C2 (length = 16 mm), and the last one in the left mass of C3 (length = 14 mm) because of the articular destruction on this side. Fixation was achieved with a crosslink.

The analysis of the evacuated paravertebral abscess confirmed a methicillin-susceptible germ, so the initial antibiotic therapy was replaced by Linezolid (Zyvox).

## Post operative course

Thereafter, sepsis resolved with a decrease in inflammatory markers and recovery of a normal level of consciousness. The post-operative clinical course was uneventful. The patient did not present any neurologic impairment and the wound was clean.  

The control CT confirmed the quality of the decompression and atlantoaxial stabilization. All the screws were positioned correctly.

The patient was discharged to a rehabilitation center 9 days after surgery.  

At the 2-month follow-up visit, she was able to walk without any support. Pain had disappeared, and physical examination was unchanged. Inflammatory markers were negative, and the wound healed well.

One year later, her neck remained pain-free. She did not need any medication.

## Discussion

Pyogenic spine infections more often involve lumbar and thoracic vertebrae; cervical spine infections, especially atlantoaxial arthritis, are less common, with sometimes unclear clinical presentation.

MRI is a reliable tool to confirm the diagnosis at an early stage, with changes being noticeable from the first week of infection, whereas CT may require at least two weeks. Within 48 hours, MRI usually shows joint effusion, enhancement of facet joints, and soft tissue inflammation. Local spread can be assessed precisely: it detects epidural abscesses, paravertebral spinal muscle abscesses, or pyomyositis and spondylitis [5, 6].

Staphylococcus aureus is the main causative agent for facet joint arthritis in the adult population. Infective organisms can reach the spine via the blood supply or by direct inoculation (e.g., from corticosteroid injection) or by extension from an adjacent infectious nidus.  

Several risk factors are described for pyogenic spine infection: intravenous drug abuse, AIDS, diabetes, end-stage renal disease, long-term steroid therapy, and other immunocompromised states.  

In this case, the only predisposing factor was the patient's age. Although she had received a corticosteroid injection 7 months ago, it is likely that this was a spontaneous pyogenic bacteremia with inoculation of the spine, as a previous cervical spine MRI did not reveal any abnormalities.  

The patient presented with a severe septic syndrome, altered consciousness, local complications on imaging (paravertebral and epidural abscesses with spinal cord compression), despite antibiotic therapy, and spinal instability related to massive destruction of the left articular mass.

Indeed, a surgical treatment was decided to decompress the spinal cord and provide bacteriologic information by debridement of the epidural collection, followed by stabilization of the atlantoaxial complex through posterior instrumental fusion. We preferred a posterior approach over transoral decompression due to continuity between the anterior epidural abscess and the soft tissue abscess. Fixation of C1-C2 pars articularis, known as Harms procedure, was preferred as it is a lighter procedure than occipitocervical fixation. It allows effective posterior decompression of the spinal canal and provides stable fixation without any implant in the spinal canal [7].

The patient was successfully treated with rapid improvement of neck pain and avoidance of any neurological impairment. The infectious course was favorable as well. A good consolidation with bone fusion was observed on the CT performed 5 months after surgery.  

Conservative management, based on external immobilization by halo fixation together with long-term appropriate antibiotic therapy (6-8 weeks), should be reserved for controlled sepsis without any neurologic impairment related to spinal epidural abscess [2–4]. In such cases, the infectious agent should be identified through blood cultures or from CT-guided joint aspiration [4].

## Conclusion

This clinical case emphasizes that spinal facet joint arthritis is a severe infection with possible significant morbidity if not treated promptly. Early use of MRI should avoid delayed diagnosis, guide the selection of appropriate management (medical or combined treatment depending on the presence of an abscess), and ensure favorable outcomes. Early surgical treatment for uncontrolled sepsis is also a critical element of prognosis.
