# Minimally Manipulative Extraction of Polycystic Cervical Neurocysticercosis

## Abstract

Intradural, extramedullary cervical spinal involvement is an uncommon manifestation of neurocysticercosis.

A case is presented of a middle-aged man with neurocysticercosis involving the intradural extramedullary cervical spine and brain who originally presented with bilateral paresthesias of his extremities and a progressively unsteady gait. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed cystic enhancing lesions in the brain and cervical spine region, with the largest cyst extending from the posterior fossa through C2, causing spinal cord compression. The patient underwent surgical resection of the intradural extramedullary cervical spinal lesions and has continued to improve clinically, with no recurrence of cystic lesions.

When examining patients with clinical signs of a spinal mass lesion, the differential diagnosis should include neurocysticercosis of the spine.

## Introduction

Neurocysticercosis is a parasitic disease of the central nervous system acquired through fecal–oral contamination with the cestode *Taenia solium*. Humans act as an intermediate host. The eggs of *Taenia solium* are ingested, typically through infected pork. After digestion, the eggs develop into larvae that can migrate to any part of the body, including the central nervous system. Cysticercosis is prevalent in less industrialized nations, particularly in Latin America, where there are approximately 400,000 symptomatic patients and an additional 75 million people at risk for infection [1,2].

Neurocysticercosis is classified based on location, in order of decreasing frequency: subarachnoid cisternal, parenchymal, intraventricular, and spinal involvement [3,4]. Based on radiologic findings from CT and MR imaging, neurocysticercosis has five stages: non-cystic, vesicular, colloidal vesicular, granular nodular, and calcified nodular [2,5].

Intradural, extramedullary cervical spinal involvement with large cystic or racemose structures and spinal cord tethering is an uncommon manifestation of neurocysticercosis. This report describes the diagnosis and operative technique used in treating this unique presentation.

## Clinical Presentation

### History and Examination

A 49-year-old man originally from Guatemala, who had been living in the United States for 3 years, with a history of pulmonary tuberculosis and seizures, presented to the emergency department with paresthesias predominantly in his fingers bilaterally. These symptoms had slowly progressed to his arms over the previous 2 months and had spread to his lower extremities during the preceding week. He also complained of an increasingly unsteady gait. Evaluation during this admission confirmed that he was clear of active tuberculosis.

On physical examination, the patient had patchy loss of sensation in the upper extremities bilaterally and decreased sensation in the lower extremities below the L1–L2 dermatomes. He demonstrated bilateral proximal muscle weakness in both the upper and lower extremities, graded at 4 to 4+/5. Deep tendon reflexes were brisk at 3+ and symmetric. Bilateral Babinski signs were present. He exhibited dysmetria on finger-to-nose testing and decreased amplitude of rapid alternating movements. His gait was wide-based and unsteady. Bowel, bladder, and sexual function were intact, and ophthalmologic examination was normal.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the cervical spine demonstrated large, enhancing polycystic lesions located dorsally, compressing the spinal cord, most prominent from the posterior fossa through C2. An additional area of stenosis was noted at C6–C7. Computed tomography of the brain demonstrated multiple calcified nodules throughout the parenchyma, and MR imaging showed multiple small enhancing lesions throughout the brain. MR imaging of the thoracolumbar spine demonstrated diffuse enhancement along the cord surface with large nodules in the lower lumbar region. Laboratory evaluation included Western blot analysis, which was positive for antibodies to *Taenia solium*. Antibodies for coccidioidomycosis and histoplasmosis were negative.

## Operation

The patient elected to undergo surgical treatment consisting of a suboccipital craniectomy, C1 laminectomy, resection of the intradural extramedullary lesions, and untethering of the cervical spinal cord. Upon opening the dura at the level of C1, a markedly thickened and opacified arachnoid was encountered. Large cystic lesions spontaneously expressed through the dural opening and were extracted with forceps. Several cysts were submitted for microbiologic analysis as well as frozen section and permanent pathology.

The dura was then opened more widely, allowing extraction of several smaller cysts. Just below the level of C1, a large abnormal pedicle of tissue adherent to the arachnoid of the spinal cord was identified. The arachnoid was excised from this tether, and the tethering pedicle was resected to relieve spinal cord tethering. Intraoperatively, the patient received dexamethasone, albendazole, and cefazolin.

## Pathological Findings

Pathologic examination demonstrated cysts of *Taenia solium* with a tegument containing fine basophilic microvilli on its surface. Basophilic calcareous bodies were scattered throughout the stroma, along with enlarged cystic spaces. No scolex was identified in the submitted specimens. A small focus of cyst necrosis with associated inflammation, including scattered eosinophils, was present. The pedicle and adjacent arachnoid tissue showed fibrosis with collagen deposition and chronic inflammation, including foreign body giant cells.

## Postoperative Course

Postoperatively, the patient continued treatment with albendazole and dexamethasone. His paresthesias, dysmetria, and gait instability improved. Cervical MR imaging obtained the day after surgery demonstrated complete removal of the cervical spinal cystic lesions. Imaging performed 5 months postoperatively demonstrated no recurrence of cystic lesions, persistent tethering at C4, and evidence of myelomalacia.

The patient improved during a prolonged 5-month hospital course, which was extended partly due to placement issues. His paresthesias continued to improve, and he gradually regained strength with daily physical and occupational therapy. Therapy focused on gait training, use of assistive devices to address poor proprioception, and strengthening exercises for deconditioning and proximal muscle weakness. He was discharged on gabapentin and baclofen for neuropathic pain and spasticity.

The patient did not attend scheduled neurosurgical follow-up visits but did have several follow-up neurology visits, including electromyography of the upper extremities approximately 1 year postoperatively to evaluate persistent neuropathic hand pain and to rule out ulnar neuropathy versus lower cervical radiculopathy. This study was negative. After discharge, he reportedly began the process of renewing his passport to return to his home country.

## Discussion

Intradural, extramedullary cervical spinal neurocysticercosis is a rare condition. Large polycystic lesions in the cervical spine on MRI are not typical of the usual presentation. While intramedullary neurocysticercosis is thought to result from hematogenous dissemination [6], intradural extramedullary disease likely results from direct cerebrospinal fluid dissemination from the cerebrum into the subarachnoid space [4].

Although CT is advantageous for detecting calcifications, MR imaging is superior for identifying spinal lesions [7]. In addition to neuroimaging, cerebrospinal fluid analysis, antibody detection, and histologic examination aid in the diagnosis of neurocysticercosis [8]. The racemose form consists of cysts without a scolex that enlarge by contiguous growth. These cysts are often large in the posterior fossa and may extend downward into the spinal subarachnoid space, as observed in this case [9].

Viable cysts generally do not provoke significant inflammation unless the organism dies, which was focally present in this patient, as evidenced by eosinophilic inflammation. Older lesions demonstrated fibrosis and foreign body giant cell reaction within the tethered region. Parenchymal neurocysticercosis of the brain is typically managed medically with albendazole and, when indicated, corticosteroids to control inflammation [10].

More aggressive surgical intervention may be warranted in cases of spinal neurocysticercosis, particularly when spinal cord compression and large cystic lesions are present [10,11]. Severe inflammatory reactions, especially those associated with cyst rupture, make gentle manipulation of intrathecal contents essential. Avoidance of cyst spillage, careful handling of the dura and arachnoid, inspection for residual tethering, administration of steroids, and extensive irrigation of the spinal canal following decompression are important measures to minimize postoperative inflammation.

## Conclusion

Although intradural extramedullary cervical spinal neurocysticercosis is rare, it should be included in the differential diagnosis, particularly in patients from endemic regions who present with MRI findings of polycystic spinal lesions. Surgical intervention should be considered a viable treatment option, especially in cases involving large, complex cystic structures that can be evacuated with minimal manipulation of surrounding neural elements.
