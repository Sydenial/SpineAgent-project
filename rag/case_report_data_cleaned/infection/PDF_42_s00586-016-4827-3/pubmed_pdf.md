# Management of Destructive Candida Albicans Spondylodiscitis of the Cervical Spine: A Systematic Analysis of Literature Illustrated by an Unusual Case

## Abstract

**Purpose**: Candida-induced spondylodiscitis of the cervical spine in immunocompetent patients is an extremely rare infectious complication. Since clinical symptoms might be nonspecific, therapeutic latency can lead to permanent spinal cord damage, sepsis, and fatal complications. Surgical debridement is strongly recommended, but there is no standard antimycotic regime for postsurgical treatment. This paper summarizes available data and demonstrates another successfully treated case.  
**Methods**: The systematic analysis was performed according to the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. PubMed and Web of Science were scanned to identify English language articles. Additionally, the authors describe the case of a 60-year-old male patient who presented with a Candida albicans induced cervical spondylodiscitis after an edematous pancreatitis and C. albicans sepsis. Anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion of C4-C6, additional anterior plating, as well as posterior stabilization C3-Th1 was followed by a 6-month antimycotic therapy.  
**Results**: A systematic literature analysis was conducted and 4599 articles on spondylodiscitis were scanned. Only four cases were found reporting a C. albicans spondylodiscitis in a non-immunocompromised patient. Our patient was followed up for 2 years, and until now, he shows no symptoms or infection parameters.  
**Conclusion**: Candida albicans spondylodiscitis of the cervical spine presents a potentially life-threatening disease. To our knowledge, this is the fifth case in literature that describes the treatment of C. albicans spondylodiscitis in an immunocompetent patient. Surgical debridement should be considered, with antimycotic regimes varying in pharmaceutical agents and treatment duration.

## Introduction

With only about 100 cases having been reported worldwide, spondylodiscitis of the cervical spine caused by Candida species is a rare disease. The thoracic and lumbar spine are affected in 95%, and it is usually found in immunocompromised patients. Fungal spondylodiscitis is uncommon even in large series, usually accounting for 0.5–1.6% of all spondylodiscitis cases. Candida albicans is responsible for 62% of Candida species spondylodiscitis, but cases of spondylodiscitis caused by other Candida species are becoming increasingly prevalent. Candida species spondylodiscitis can arise from catheter placement, drug abuse, parenteral nutrition, and opportunistic infections in immunocompromised patients. However, the increasing use of invasive procedures has also led to rising cases of Candida species spondylodiscitis following hospital-acquired septicemia. Early diagnosis is crucial to prevent infection spread, abscess formation, sepsis, and death. The mortality rate is reported to be 15%. Prolonged antimycotic treatment is recommended, and surgical debridement should be considered.

## Case Report

A 60-year-old man presented with chronic neck pain following a cholecystectomy for acute edematous pancreatitis. He had been treated with systemic fluconazole for 3 weeks due to septicemia. After discharge, he sought treatment for muscle tension in the neck at a local Thai massage, which worsened his symptoms, prompting him to visit our clinic.

## Clinical Course

The patient exhibited pressure pain in the middle and lower cervical spine, but without signs of inflammation or neurological deficits. CT and MRI scans showed spinal stenosis and destruction of C4-C6. Blood tests showed marginally elevated infection markers (CRP 4.2 mg/dl, leucocytes 9.74 K/μl). The patient did not receive preoperative antibiotics, as tissue samples were to be taken for microbiological analysis.

## Surgery and Postsurgical Course

The patient underwent anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion (ACCF) followed by CT-guided dorsal stabilization. Intraoperative tissue analysis confirmed C. albicans spondylodiscitis. Antimycotic therapy was initiated with liposomal amphotericin B but was discontinued due to renal deterioration. Therapy was switched to caspofungin, and after 2 months, oral fluconazole was administered for an additional 5 months, completing a 7-month antimycotic regimen. The patient showed no symptoms or signs of infection at 2 years follow-up.

## Methods

A systematic review following PRISMA guidelines was conducted to identify cases of Candida spondylodiscitis of the cervical spine in immunocompetent patients. PubMed and Web of Science databases were searched on December 3, 2015. No restrictions were placed on publication dates, but only articles with English abstracts were included.

## Results

From the initial 4599 retrieved titles and abstracts, 1461 were excluded due to duplication, and those involving immunocompromised patients or non-cervical spine cases were eliminated. Four reports met the inclusion criteria.

## Discussion

### Symptoms

Early Candida spondylodiscitis can present with nonspecific symptoms, and due to its rare occurrence, diagnosis may be delayed, risking severe outcomes. Chronic neck and back pain, fever, and neurological symptoms such as radicular pain, weakness, and sensory loss may develop later.

### Diagnosis

Erythrocyte sedimentation rate, leucocyte count, and CRP are sensitive but not specific. Blood cultures often yield false negatives, and radiological imaging may not reveal the infection in early stages. MRI is considered the gold standard for diagnosis, with contrast agents enhancing sensitivity, especially for early infections.

### Surgical Treatment

There are no standard treatment guidelines for Candida-induced spondylodiscitis in immunocompetent patients. In cases with progressive infection, surgical intervention, including debridement and stabilization, is recommended, especially when neurological deficits, spinal instability, or abscess formation are present.

### Antimycotic Treatment

There is no established treatment regimen for Candida spondylodiscitis in immunocompetent patients. Resistance testing is crucial, as different Candida species show varying antifungal susceptibilities. Amphotericin B has been recommended as first-line therapy, but its nephrotoxic effects have led to alternatives such as fluconazole or caspofungin being used, often for a duration of 6–12 months.

## Conclusion

Candida albicans-induced spondylodiscitis is a rare but serious disease, particularly in immunocompetent patients. Early suspicion, MRI, and needle biopsy can help avoid surgical intervention and ensure early diagnosis. A combination of surgery and prolonged antimycotic treatment is necessary to reduce mortality rates.
