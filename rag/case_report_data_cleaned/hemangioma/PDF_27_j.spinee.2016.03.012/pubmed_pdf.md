# Intradural Cavernous Lymphangioma of the Thoracic Spine: Case Report, Technical Considerations, and Review of the Literature

## Abstract

### Background Context
Cavernous lymphangioma is a rare, slow-growing tumor that can cause neurologic compromise when it involves the central nervous system. Involvement of the spinal column is rare but may affect the osseous structures or the epidural space.

### Purpose
We report the first case of an intradural, extramedullary cavernous lymphangioma involving the thoracic spinal cord.

## Methods
An eighty-three-year-old female presented with progressive gait ataxia, bilateral lower extremity weakness, and a band-like sensation in the middle and lower thoracic dermatomes. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thoracic spinal cord revealed hyperintensity on T2 and enhancement of an intradural cystic lesion at the T5-T8 levels with significant compression of the spinal cord.

## Results
Complete surgical resection was difficult due to the adherence of the tumor to the pial surface and microvasculature of the thoracic spinal cord. Recurrence of the mass was ultimately treated with cystic fluid diversion into the peritoneum. At her twenty-eight month follow-up visit, the patient was able to ambulate with minimal assistance. A comparative literature review is presented. There are no reports in the literature of intradural thoracic spinal cord involvement.

## Conclusions
Intradural cavernous lymphangioma of the spine poses a unique surgical challenge for complete resection. Cystic fluid diversion appears to be a viable treatment option with lasting benefit if complete resection is not achieved.

## Clinical Case Report

An eighty-three-year-old female presented with an eight-month history of an abnormal band-like sensation in the middle and lower thoracic dermatomes and progressive gait ataxia requiring a walker for ambulation. Physical examination revealed 5/5 bilateral lower extremity strength, decreased proprioception in the left lower extremity, positive Romberg sign, decreased sensation over the mid-thoracic dermatomes, and 3+ bilateral patellar and Achilles tendon reflexes.

Initial MRI of the thoracic spine revealed an intradural, extramedullary cystic lesion along the dorsal aspect of the T5-T8 levels with significant compression of the thoracic cord. Systemic imaging, including a PET scan and CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, revealed no other contributing disease processes.

The patient underwent a T5-T8 laminectomy and dural opening for lesion resection. Intraoperative neuro-monitoring included somatosensory evoked potentials, motor evoked potentials, and electromyographic modalities. During the surgical exploration, the cyst was found to be dark in color with thick, rubbery consistency. The capsule contained a viscous fluid, and no septations were found. Sharp dissection was required to separate the tumor from the dural surface. However, the tumor's adherence to the microvasculature of the pia and capsule posed challenges. Residual capsule was left attached to the pial surface. Final pathological review confirmed cavernous lymphangioma.

At her four-week follow-up, the patient was able to walk independently but continued to experience decreased proprioception in the left lower extremity and hyperactive reflexes in both her patellar and Achilles tendons. Four months later, she presented with new onset weakness and progressive gait ataxia requiring a walker. Repeat MRI revealed fluid accumulation within the cystic mass, now extending between T5-T9 with compression of the thecal sac. The patient returned to surgery for another incomplete resection. Post-surgical recovery showed improvement in dorsiflexion strength and gait, although she continued to require a walker.

Three months later, the patient experienced further neurological decline, with bilateral lower extremity strength of 4/5 and severe ataxia, requiring a wheelchair. MRI revealed tumor recurrence with cord compression. Due to the large cystic component of the mass, a cysto-peritoneal shunt was placed. Post-operative recovery showed slow neurological improvement. At her nine-month follow-up, the patient had 5/5 lower extremity strength and was walking with a walker. At her twenty-eight-month follow-up, the patient's neurological examination remained stable with 5/5 strength in her lower extremities.

## Discussion

Lymphangiomas are characterized by abnormal dilated lymphatic channels and cystic spaces lined with endothelium, containing lymphocytes. These benign hamartomatous tumors are most commonly found within the first two years of life and often involve the head and neck. Lymphangiomas are believed to be congenital malformations of large ectatic and sequestered lymphatic channels that fail to connect with venous blood vessels or normal lymph tissue. Histologically, lymphangiomas are classified into three variants: cystic, cavernous, or capillary lymphangiomas. Cavernous lymphangiomas are composed of thin walls and opaque fluid-filled lumens, as seen in this case.

Lymphangioma involvement of the spinal column is extremely rare. When it occurs, it can present as a primary soft tissue tumor in the epidural space, a primary osseous tumor, or a combination of both. The first case of primary lymphangioma involving the vertebral body was reported in 1956. Epidural spinal cord involvement may result from a mediastinal mass extending into the intervertebral foramen and subsequently involving the nerve root and epidural space.

Radiographic diagnosis of spinal lymphangiomas remains challenging. MRI is preferred over CT due to its ability to define boundaries, size, and loculations of the tumor. MRI findings in cavernous lymphangiomas typically show low to isointense signals on T1, high intensity on T2, and homogeneous enhancement with intravenous gadolinium.

Intradural lymphangiomas are thought to be rare because the central nervous system lacks lymphatic tissue. Several hypotheses have been proposed for their occurrence, including remnant lymphatic tissue within the thecal sac, or the involvement of lymphatic channels traveling along neurovascular bundles that penetrate the dura.

Surgical resection remains the primary treatment for symptomatic spinal lymphangiomas. However, as seen in this case, complete resection may not be possible due to the tumor's adherence to critical structures, such as the pia and microvasculature. Recurrence is common when complete resection is not achieved.

## Conclusion

We describe the first case of an intradural, extramedullary cavernous lymphangioma of the thoracic spine with intradural involvement. Given the adherence of the tumor capsule to the microvasculature and pia, caution is advised with attempts at complete resection. If complete removal is not possible, cystic fluid diversion procedures, such as cysto-peritoneal shunting, may be considered for symptomatic treatment.
