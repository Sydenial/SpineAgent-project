# Spontaneous epidural spinal haematoma in children caused by vascular malformations

## Abstract

**Purpose**  
The occurrence of spinal epidural haematoma of spontaneous origin in adults is a well-documented entity, though it is rare in children. In the literature to date, there are few cases of this kind of spontaneous haematoma proven to be due to an underlying vascular abnormality.

**Method**  
Retrospective review of two cases of children under 15 years of age with spontaneous epidural spinal haematoma.

**Results**  
Underlying arteriovenous malformations were identified in both cases. Intra-operative findings and histological features of these anomalies are described.

**Conclusion**  
These are the first two such cases described with clinico-pathological correlation.

## Introduction

Spontaneous epidural spinal haematoma (SESH) is a rare cause of spinal cord compression in children [1]. Although well recognised in adults, reports of this entity in the paediatric population are few and are often ascribed to idiopathic causation. We present two children with spontaneous epidural spinal haematoma, outlining their surgical management, outcomes, and pathological features.

## Case 1

A previously healthy 8-year-old girl developed severe neck pain while dancing and reported feeling a sudden “pop” at onset. Over a 6-week period, she became increasingly lethargic with progressively worsening mobility. After a further week, she attended the casualty department unable to walk.

Clinical examination on admission revealed MRC grade 3/5 spastic tetraparesis and urinary retention. Spinal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed an epidural lesion compressing the thecal sac on the right from C4/5 to C7/T1 levels. The lesion was centrally hyperintense on T1-weighted imaging with a peripheral lower signal intensity rim, consistent with an acute or subacute epidural haematoma. No other abnormalities were identified within the neuroaxis. All haematological investigations were within normal limits.

The patient underwent urgent C5–C7 laminoplasty and evacuation of the haematoma. Significant bleeding was encountered from abnormally dilated arterialised epidural veins, which was rapidly controlled using diathermy. These veins extended caudally beyond the laminotomy. As no vascular imaging was available to define the architecture of the suspected arteriovenous malformation (AVM), further exposure was avoided due to the immature spine. The abnormal vessels were carefully dissected from the dural sac, achieving excellent haemostasis. Once the veins at the level of exposure had been excised, bleeding from remaining veins was no longer arterial.

Post-operatively, the child made a rapid neurological recovery over 3 weeks. At discharge, she was independently ambulant with normal bladder function. A time-resolved imaging of contrast kinetics (TRICKS) MRI performed post-operatively demonstrated no residual haematoma or abnormal vasculature.

Histological examination revealed blood clot and a complex arrangement of vessels with varying wall thickness, including venous-like structures with fibrous thickening and arterialised vessels with thinner walls. These findings were consistent with a vascular malformation, specifically an arteriovenous malformation.

## Case 2

An otherwise healthy 13-year-old boy developed sudden-onset interscapular pain with no obvious precipitating factor. The following day, he experienced progressive lower limb weakness and urinary retention. On admission, neurological examination revealed paraplegia (MRC grade 0/5), areflexia, loss of anal tone, and a T4 sensory level.

MRI of the thoracic spine demonstrated a mixed-signal epidural lesion located posteriorly and to the left, extending from C7/T1 to T4/5. The lesion displaced the spinal cord anteriorly and its signal characteristics were most consistent with an acute or subacute epidural haematoma.

He underwent emergency T1–T5 laminoplasty with evacuation of the epidural haematoma and resection of an AVM. Histological analysis revealed haemorrhagic tissue containing vascular channels with abnormally thick, irregular walls, with focal areas of thinning. These features were consistent with an arteriovenous malformation.

Post-operatively, neurological function improved to MRC grade 4/5. At discharge, he was able to walk independently but experienced intermittent urinary incontinence. At 3-month follow-up, he remained independently ambulant with only very mild right lower limb weakness evident on close gait assessment, though intermittent urinary incontinence persisted. Follow-up MRI demonstrated no flow voids or abnormal contrast enhancement to suggest residual vascular malformation.

## Discussion

Spontaneous epidural spinal haematoma is an uncommon cause of spinal cord compression in children, with 37 cases reported in the English literature to date. Clinical presentation may be variable or non-specific, complicating diagnosis. The most common presenting symptom is spinal pain at the level of the haematoma, with or without associated radicular pain [2, 3]. In some cases, irritability may be the only early symptom. Delayed diagnosis has been reported, including cases initially misdiagnosed as Guillain–Barré syndrome [4], highlighting the importance of timely imaging.

Magnetic resonance imaging is considered the diagnostic gold standard for spinal epidural haematomas [3, 5]. MRI not only confirms the presence of haematoma but may help estimate its age and suggest an underlying vascular abnormality. TRICKS MRI provides high-resolution vascular imaging and may obviate the need for digital subtraction angiography, which is more invasive and technically demanding.

In the first case presented, a prolonged interval of several weeks occurred between symptom onset and neurological deterioration. Similar delays have been reported in other cases [2, 6], with some patients experiencing recurrent episodes over years before definitive diagnosis. Recurrent spontaneous epidural haematomas have also been described, occasionally without identification of an underlying vascular lesion despite extensive imaging and surgical exploration [7].

Epidural haematomas in children are most commonly dorsal in location and frequently occur between the C5 and T1 vertebral levels [8]. An underlying cause is identified in approximately 50–60% of cases [9], with bleeding traditionally thought to be venous in origin. Activities causing abrupt changes in intra-abdominal or intrathoracic pressure, such as coughing or sneezing, may precipitate bleeding within the valveless epidural venous plexus [2, 8]. Other potential aetiologies include coagulopathies, vascular malformations, and neoplasms, though in many cases no definitive cause is identified [10].

Abnormally dilated epidural veins were identified intra-operatively in the first case, a finding also reported in adult patients [11]. Histological examination in such cases often demonstrates vessels with features intermediate between veins and arteries. True AVMs as an underlying cause of SESH are particularly rare, accounting for approximately 2% of spinal lesions in children under 10 years of age [12]. Although AVMs have been reported in isolated paediatric cases, they frequently remain undetected despite advanced imaging [9].

Spinal digital subtraction angiography is generally reserved for cases in which MRI suggests a vascular malformation [1, 13]. However, angiographic findings may be negative even when a vascular abnormality is later suspected or confirmed, and delayed angiography may reveal abnormalities not evident on initial studies [7].

The possibility of an underlying vascular malformation should be considered in children presenting with spontaneous epidural spinal haematoma, even in the absence of clear MRI evidence. Given the potential for significant arterial bleeding, surgical evacuation should be undertaken by experienced surgeons. These two cases represent the first reported instances of spontaneous epidural spinal haematoma in children with confirmed clinico-pathological correlation. The optimal follow-up strategy and natural history of asymptomatic vascular lesions remain uncertain.
