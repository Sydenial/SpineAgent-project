# VERTEBRAL BODY LIPOMA: AN UNUSUAL PATHOLOGY TREATED WITH KYPHOPLASTY

## Abstract
Study Design: Case report.

Objective: Report of vertebral body lipoma treated by kyphoplasty alone.

Summary of Background Data: Vertebral lipoma is extremely rare; our search of the English literature revealed 20 patients in 16 reports.

Methods: A 32-year-old female presented with low back pain for nearly one year. Lumbar MRI suggested a hemangioma, and the patient underwent surgery.

Results: On microscopic examination, the lesion showed a widely infiltrating appearance of mature fat tissue between bone trabeculae; the diagnosis was intraosseous lipoma.

Conclusions: We believe that the management should be surgical total removal of the lesion even in incidentally found cases in order to obtain histologic diagnosis and pain relief.

Key Words: Back pain, vertebral lipoma, kyphoplasty.

Level of Evidence: 5.

## Introduction
Vertebral tumors are basically primary or metastatic. Primary tumors are either benign or malignant. The frequent primary benign tumors of vertebra include hemangioma, osteoid osteoma, osteoblastoma, giant cell bone tumor, osteochondroma, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, and aneurysmal bone cyst. The primary malignant tumors of vertebra are chordoma, Ewing sarcoma, osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, lymphoma, and multiple myeloma. Fibrosarcomas are rare among malignant tumors.

Vertebral metastatic tumors compose nearly 40% of all bone metastases. Lung, breast, and prostate constitute half of spinal metastases. Metastases to the spine frequently involve the lower thoracic and lumbar segments. In the pediatric age group, neuroblastoma and Ewing sarcoma are likely to metastasize to the spine. Osseous lipomas are a rare benign pathology.

Vertebral lipoma is extremely rare, and our search of the English literature revealed 20 patients in 16 reports [1-16]. This case report is unique since it is the first vertebral lipoma case treated by kyphoplasty alone.

## Case Report
A 32-year-old female presented with low back pain lasting nearly one year, refractory to conservative treatment. Rheumatologic evaluation was unremarkable. Neurological examination was normal except for tenderness over the lower lumbar region on palpation.

Lumbar MRI revealed features in the L4 vertebral body suggestive of a hemangioma (Fig. 1A, 1B, 1C). The patient underwent surgery for pain relief. Biopsies were taken from the L4 vertebral body, and bilateral 5 cc of cement was inserted. There were no perioperative complications. The patient’s pain resolved; she was mobilized the next day and discharged. Postoperative X-ray confirmed appropriate filling of the cement within the vertebra (Fig. 1D). On microscopic examination, the lesion showed a widely infiltrating appearance of mature fat tissue between bone trabeculae, consistent with intraosseous lipoma (Fig. 2).

## Discussion
Lipomatous lesions of the musculoskeletal system are grouped into nine entities: lipoma, lipomatosis, lipomatosis of nerve, lipoblastoma or lipomatosis, angiolipoma, myolipoma of soft tissue, chondroid lipoma, spindle cell lipoma, pleomorphic lipoma, and hibernoma. Only 16 patients have been reported harboring hibernoma occurring outside a soft tissue location [2].

Milgram [3] subdivided intraosseous lipomas according to the degree of involution:
- Stage 1: solid tumors of viable lipocytes
- Stage 2: partial fat necrosis and focal calcification with regions of viable lipocytes
- Stage 3: late cases in which fat cells have died, with variable cyst formation

Intraosseous lipoma is benign. The major clinical symptom of vertebral lipoma is nearly always pain, with distribution depending on segmental level. Some vertebral lipomas may be asymptomatic and incidentally found. Ramos et al. reviewed radiographic and CT characteristics of osseous lipoma, noting that an osteolytic lesion with a well-defined thin sclerotic border is characteristic on X-ray. The presence of fat-equivalent density on CT is highly suggestive of osseous lipoma, though comparable attenuation can be seen in chronic osteomyelitis and post-necrotic subchondral excavation [4].

MRI and CT are sensitive in detecting fatty tissue. The T1 and T2 relaxation times and spin density of benign lipomatous tumors are similar to those of normal subcutaneous fat [5]. Fat generally produces high signal intensity on MRI, enabling diagnosis of primarily fatty lesions; lipomas do not cause trabeculation and show no enhancement. Blacksin et al. reported that lesions composed of variable fat tissue had high signal intensity on both T1-weighted and T2-weighted images; extensive fat necrosis yielded low T1 and high T2 signal, which can create diagnostic difficulty [6].

On MRI, isointense areas on T1-weighted and T2-weighted images correspond to subcutaneous fat [7]. Some bony lesions may undergo fatty necrosis, but they can be differentiated from lipoma because bony expansion, cystic change, and resorption of pre-existing bone occur only in lipomata [8]. The radiologic differential diagnosis includes hemangioma, aneurysmal bone cyst, chondrosarcoma, chordoma, and bone infarct.

Our review of the literature revealed 20 patients reported in 16 papers. The anatomic localization was lumbar in seven patients, sacral in four, cervical in three, thoracic in two, and one each in the ilium, acetabulum, and coccyx; one patient had multiple involvement. All but three had histopathologic confirmation. In patients without histopathologic verification, diagnosis was based on radiologic findings [9-11].

Surgical treatments reported in 17 operated cases included percutaneous or open biopsy alone in five cases; biopsy with curettage in four cases (with bone grafting in two); tumor excision in two; partial removal in one; laminectomy in two; coccygectomy in one; and radiofrequency ablation in one. One report did not specify surgical details. Conservative management has been recommended for asymptomatic lesions that do not cause instability.

We believe that management should be surgical total removal of the lesion, even in incidentally found cases, to obtain histologic diagnosis and pain relief. Our case is the first example in the literature in which vertebral cementing via kyphoplasty alone was performed successfully for pain relief in a patient with vertebral lipoma.
