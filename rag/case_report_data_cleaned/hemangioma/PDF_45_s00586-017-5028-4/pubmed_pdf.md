# A Rare Invasive Hemangioma in Cauda Equina

## Abstract

**Purpose**: Typical hemangioma of cauda equina with a relatively clear margin has been described in some case reports, but atypical hemangioma with invasion, infiltration, and augmentation of all nerve roots in the cauda equina area has never been reported. In this paper, we report a rare case of invasive hemangioma in cauda equina, and analyze its radiological appearance and treatment.  
**Methods**: We describe an atypical case of hemangioma, revealed by MR and intraoperative photographs without a clear margin and confirmed by histopathologic diagnosis.  
**Results**: The patient was treated with radiotherapy (5400 cGy/25f/5w) and continued to be clinically in good condition with follow-up MR after 24 months without further surgery.  
**Conclusions**: Surgery may be the first management modality for hemangiomas with severe or progressive neurologic deficits, and radiotherapy may be an alternative therapy to treat hemangiomas according to accurate histopathologic diagnosis.

**Keywords**: Cauda equina, Hemangioma, Radiotherapy

## Introduction

Tumors of the cauda equina are rare conditions that can lead to major permanent neurological deficits. The most common tumors in the cauda equina are myxopapillary ependymomas (90%) and schwannomas. Although typical hemangioma of the cauda equina has been described in some case reports, atypical hemangiomas are rarer, often involving vascular histogenesis and infiltration of all nerve roots in the cauda equina. To our knowledge, no such cases have been reported until now, and we present a rare case of invasive hemangioma in the cauda equina.

## Materials and Methods

A 51-year-old man presented with a 5-month history of progressively worsening pain in his low back and legs. Conservative treatment for pain control failed.

Upon examination, he experienced radicular pain and sensory disturbances in both legs, especially in the perineal region, without muscle weakness. Knee and ankle jerk reflexes were absent, and there were no positive pathological reflexes. The straight leg raising test was positive in both legs, with leg movement during physical evaluation exacerbating the pain.

MRI and dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI indicated an atypical tumor with intermediate signal intensity on T1 and T2-weighted images. The tumor showed relatively homogeneous enhancement after contrast. It revealed signal abnormalities in all nerve roots of cauda equina (L1-L5) without a clear margin, demonstrating tumor ingrowth in all nerve roots. There was no significant finding on X-ray or CT.

Given the patient's clinical history and negative laboratory findings, the preoperative diagnosis was an intraspinal tumor invading and infiltrating all cauda equina roots, a situation we had not encountered before, and we could not identify the specific type of tumor. The patient was treated with posterior decompression of the lumbar spinal canal with dissection of the dura due to his severe neurological symptoms.

Intraoperative findings revealed that the tumor had disseminated within all cauda equina roots without forming a separate mass. The enlarged nerve roots were firm, with tortuous vessels surrounding them. We attempted to remove the tumor but could not as it had invaded and infiltrated all the nerve roots inseparably. A small sample of the vessels and nerve roots infiltrated by the tumor was obtained for histopathologic diagnosis under SEP/MEP monitoring.

## Results

The patient experienced immediate relief of pain and numbness, recovering well over a 1-week period after surgery with intravenous treatment (methylprednisolone and mannitol). The histopathologic diagnosis confirmed cauda equina hemangioma, and the patient received radiotherapy (5400 cGy/25f/5w). Follow-up MR after 24 months showed no further surgery was needed, and the patient remained in good clinical condition.

## Discussion

The most common tumors in cauda equina are myxopapillary ependymomas (90%) and schwannomas, while other less common tumors include paragangliomas, intradural metastases, hemangioblastomas, meningiomas, astrocytomas, spinal PNETs, and gangliogliomas. Benign masses that mimic tumors include lipomas of the filum terminale, dermoid cysts, and epidermoid cysts. In this case, atypical hemangioma is extremely rare, with invasion, infiltration, and augmentation of all nerve roots in the cauda equina, a phenomenon we did not find in any previous publications.

Tumors in the cauda equina are generally easy to diagnose through MRI and dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI. These tumors or benign masses are usually solitary and sporadic, often showing intense enhancement, intrinsic vascular changes (e.g., thrombosis, sinusoidal dilatation), cyst formation, hemorrhage, calcification, and fatty degeneration. MRI is considered the gold standard for diagnosing these types of tumors.

Typical hemangiomas are mainly located in the vertebral bone, accounting for about 5% of all vascular lesions in the spine, with only about 3% found inside the spinal canal sporadically, and with a clear margin. Histopathologically, typical hemangiomas are vascular malformations consisting of vascular spaces lined by a single endothelial layer without neural or glial elements. Cavernous hemangiomas consist of large dilated vascular channels without interposed neural tissue. Capillary hemangiomas are characterized by lobular architecture, with each lobule separated by fibrous connective tissue and consisting of numerous small capillaries lined by endothelial cells. 

In our case, the lesion was larger and poorly circumscribed, infiltrating the nerve roots. The irregularly dilated blood-filled vessels arranged haphazardly, surrounded by nerve fibers and inflammatory cells, distinguishing it from typical hemangiomas.

## Conclusion

Hemangiomas of the cauda equina are a rare condition that can lead to major permanent neurological deficits. MRI may be the gold standard for diagnosing typical cases, but in this atypical case, histopathologic diagnosis played a crucial role in guiding further treatment. Surgery may be the first management option for patients with severe or progressive neurologic deficits, even without histopathologic confirmation or accurate preoperative diagnosis. In our case, radiotherapy proved to be an effective alternative treatment based on the histopathologic diagnosis.
