# Worsening Cervical Epidural Hematoma After Tissue Plasminogen Activator Administration for Stroke Like Symptoms

## Abstract
Hemiparesis caused by cervical spontaneous epidural hematoma (SEH) is a relatively uncommon symptom in comparison with tetraparesis in cervical SEH. In patients presenting with hemiparesis, it may be difficult to distinguish cerebral infarction from cervical SEH, especially at the acute stage [1–11]. We present an extremely rare case of stroke-mimicking cervical SEH with hemiparesis worsened after tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) therapy for hemiparesis followed by emergency cervical decompression.

## Case Report
A 71-year-old male presented to the emergency department with left-sided incomplete hemiparesis (manual muscle test [right/left]: deltoid 5/4, elbow flexors 5/3, wrist extensors 5/3, elbow extensors 5/3, finger flexors 5/2, finger abductors 5/2, and lower extremities 5/2) after the sudden onset of neck pain and was thought to have had a stroke. The results of preoperative laboratory blood tests were all within the normal limits. The patient was not taking any antiplatelet or anticoagulant medications.

As brain computed tomography showed no signs of hemorrhage, tPA was administered 2 hours after symptom onset. However, the patient's neurological condition worsened 1 hour after the administration of tPA, resulting in incomplete tetraplegia (manual muscle test [right/left]: deltoid 5/4, elbow flexors 5/3, wrist extensors 5/3, elbow extensors 5/3, finger flexors 3/2, finger abductors 3/2, and lower extremities 3/2).

Twenty-four hours after onset, cervical magnetic resonance imaging revealed a left posterolateral SEH at C4–C6, and the patient underwent evacuation of the epidural hematoma and C4–6 laminoplasty. The amount of intraoperative blood loss was 62 mL, and no complications of hemorrhage were observed postoperatively.

The patient's clinical status in both extremities improved 1 day after the surgery, although he was found to have a neurogenic bladder. Magnetic resonance imaging performed 5 days after onset showed no hematomas. On the final follow-up examination performed 1 year after the surgery, the neurological deficits had resolved without a bladder disorder requiring intermittent catheterization.

The patient and his family were informed that the data for this case would be submitted for publication and provided their consent.

## Discussion

### Cervical SEH Presenting With Hemiparesis
Spinal epidural hematoma compresses the spinal cord and occasionally presents with Brown-Séquard syndrome [7,8,12] or hemiparesis [1–6,9–11], mimicking acute cerebral infarction. Kreppel et al. found only 17 patients presenting with Brown-Séquard syndrome among reported cases. Matsumoto et al. reviewed 13 patients with hemiparesis caused by SEH. Moreover, hemiparesis was reported in 6 of 35 patients with SEH by Liao et al. and in 2 of 4 patients by Lonjon et al. Collectively, hemiparesis may not be uncommon in cases of cervical SEH.

### Diagnostic Procedure for Distinguishing Cervical SEH From Stroke
Severe neck or back pain followed by hemiparesis is an important sign when diagnosing cervical SEH with hemiparesis [1–5,6–14]. Moreover, Lhermitte’s sign, the absence of facial weakness or cranial nerve signs [5,10], Horner’s syndrome, and the development of Brown-Séquard syndrome during the clinical course [7,8,12] have been reported as symptoms that may help differentiate cervical SEH from stroke. Therefore, physicians should consider that these symptoms may indicate cervical SEH. The most reliable diagnostic tool in such cases is magnetic resonance imaging, although plain computed tomography may also yield acceptable results. However, the possibility of misdiagnosis with computed tomography still remains [10,12].

### Worsening Cervical SEH Erroneously Treated With Antiplatelet Therapy, Anticoagulants, and tPA
It is well known that antiplatelet, thrombolytic, and anticoagulant therapies are risk factors for SEH, including tPA. Several patients with cervical SEH presenting with hemiparesis are misdiagnosed as having cerebral infarction at the acute stage and are erroneously treated. Although tPA treatment is a first-line therapy for acute ischemic stroke, the development of spinal epidural hematomas after tPA treatment for acute myocardial infarction and pulmonary embolism has been reported. Therefore, the administration of tPA to patients with SEH is risky and may result in progression of the hematoma. Treatment with tPA can be characterized as a double-edged sword: remarkably effective, but carrying a potential risk of causing or worsening SEH.

To our knowledge, only three cases of cervical SEH erroneously treated with tPA, including the current case, have been reported in the English literature [5,11]. Fortunately, none of the cases involved excessive intraoperative bleeding or postoperative hemorrhage. However, patients should be carefully monitored for bleeding and hematoma formation during and after surgery.

To properly administer tPA treatment for acute cerebral ischemia, a correct diagnosis must be made within 3 hours of symptom onset. However, this time frame is challenging because a wide variety of conditions can mimic stroke, including seizures, migraines, spinal masses, and hematomas. Although magnetic resonance imaging is considered the most reliable diagnostic tool for detecting ischemic lesions, it can be time-consuming and may not be readily available in all settings. These pitfalls are particularly important in emergent stroke cases in which tPA treatment is being considered. In addition to cranial imaging, the spine should also be evaluated in cases of possible spinal involvement or in patients presenting with neck pain before tPA administration.

## Conclusion
It is important for physicians, especially those who administer tPA, to include cervical SEH in the differential diagnosis of stroke in patients presenting with the sudden onset of neck pain followed by neurological deficits, including hemiparesis.

## Key Points
- An extremely rare case of cervical SEH treated with tPA for hemiparesis followed by emergency posterior cervical decompression was presented.
- Although cervical SEH is typically characterized by sudden neck pain followed by tetraparesis, it can occasionally present with hemiparesis mimicking stroke.
- Cervical spinal lesions should be considered in the differential diagnosis of acute hemiparesis associated with neck pain.
- Physicians must be aware that administration of tPA may worsen cervical SEH in patients presenting with hemiparesis.
