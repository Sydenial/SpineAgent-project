# Management of a posterior gunshot injury with a floating pedicle and cerebrospinal fluid leak

## Abstract

Purpose: Gunshot injuries to the spine remain controversial regarding optimal management.

Methods: We present the case of a 30-year-old male who received a bullet in his back after an exchange of fire with the police. Initial assessment revealed paraparesis with cerebrospinal fluid leak (CSF) from the bullet entry hole. CT scan showed metal debris with two bullet fragments in the right L5/S1 foramen and lateral recess, and another fragment in the central canal posteriorly. CT also revealed two fracture lines creating a right L5 “floating pedicle.”

Results: The patient was taken to the operating room and underwent an L5/S1 posterior approach with right L5 pedicle stabilization using a pedicle screw, removal of bullet fragments, dural repair with a patch, L5/S1 posterolateral fusion, and insertion of a lumbar CSF drain. The patient could walk with a walking frame on day 7 with a satisfactory radiological result at 1 year, but with a remaining partial motor deficit of both ankles, mainly on the right side.

Conclusion: The literature contains many controversies regarding the management of spinal gunshot injuries. This case shows that early surgical management, when partial neurological deficit with a CSF leak is noted, could improve clinical outcome and prevent related complications.

Keywords: Gunshot wound; Floating pedicle; Cerebrospinal fluid leak; Spine injury

## Case presentation

A 30-year-old male, in the context of drug dealing, was trying to escape the police in a high-speed car chase with an exchange of fire. He suddenly felt a sharp pain in his low back area and the vehicle stopped. The patient was found with back bleeding and inability to stand up or walk.

The initial medical assessment made by the emergency team found a man with a normal conscious level associated with severe low lumbar pain and paraparesis mainly concerning ankle motion bilaterally. The patient was transported immediately to the hospital for further evaluation and investigations.

Upon arrival to the emergency room, the patient was alert and oriented. Exposure of the back revealed a left paravertebral wound at the level of L5 corresponding to the entry hole of a bullet, with minimal bleeding, but with leak of a transparent fluid corresponding to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Sensory and motor exam revealed saddle anesthesia, weak anal tone, urine retention, with partial deficit of ankle plantar flexors on both sides (2/5), and complete ankle dorsal flexors deficit on the right side (0/5), with sparing of the tibialis anterior muscle. Lumbar X-ray and full body CT scan confirmed the gunshot injury with multiple bullet fragments at the L5/S1 level posteriorly, along with two fracture lines around the right L5 pedicle. The patient was taken to the operating room for exploration, bullet fragment removal, dural repair, and L5/S1 posterior instrumented fusion.

## Diagnostic imaging section

Lumbar X-ray showed multiple bullet fragments at the L5/S1 level posteriorly with metal debris. CT scan revealed three main fragments: one in the right L5/S1 foramen, one in the right lateral recess, and a third in the central canal posteriorly. No vertebral body fracture was noted, but a fracture of the right L5 isthmus was seen, associated with another fracture line at the junction of the right pedicle and vertebral body, which created a right L5 “floating pedicle.” The L5 spinous process was also fractured.

## Historical review, epidemiology, diagnosis, pathology, and differential diagnosis

Gunshot wound to the spine is the third most common cause of spine injuries after fall from height and road traffic accident. It accounts for 13–17% of all gunshot injuries and occurs predominantly in the thoracic region in civilian practice, and the rate of incomplete injury in the lumbosacral area is about 70% [1]. The damage caused by a bullet depends on its kinetic energy (KE) that is directly related to its velocity via the kinetic energy formula $(\mathrm{KE}= \tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2})$. Low velocity weapons account for most civilian spinal gunshot injuries and are usually caused by direct trauma from bullet fragments or vertebral fractured segments, whereas high velocity weapons (mostly encountered in military practice) cause spinal cord injury secondary to shock wave and cavitations generated by very high-speed bullets [2]. Therefore, the extent of injury to the spinal cord and neural elements depends on ballistics (especially rotational effect like yaw), degree of contusion and transection, degree of concussive blast injury, compression by displaced bone fragments, and mechanical stability of the spinal segments [3].

Gunshot victims should be examined thoroughly, looking for entrance and exit wounds, and detailed neurological examination must be performed, as for other types of spinal trauma, including motor, sensory, reflexes, and anal sphincter tone. Two plain radiographic views of the spine (anteroposterior and lateral) can help locate bullet fragments and detect associated fractures. CT scan allows more accurate localization of any foreign body in the spinal canal or foramen, with some limitation due to artifact in the presence of metallic debris [4]. MRI in patients with retained bullet fragments has been reported to be safe [5]; nevertheless, because of the risk of migration of any ferromagnetic metal, it should be made on a patient-specific case-by-case basis.

Acute infectious complications due to contamination, especially with visceral perforation, include empyema, intraabdominal sepsis, spinal abscess, psoas muscle abscess, subcutaneous abscess, and bullet tract infection [6]. Lumbar spine is the most common site of infection followed by the thoracic and cervical spines; this is due to the fact that at the lumbar level bullets often pass through the gastrointestinal tract [7].

Retained canal fragments located below T12 have a risk of secondary migration [8], and late lead poisoning is a possible complication at a later stage [9]. Rarely, disc material can cause neural defects by compressing the canal when the bullet damages the annulus [2].

## Rationale for treatment

Ideal management of gunshot wound to the spine remains a matter of controversy, with studies supporting conservative management to avoid further harm to the spine and associated complications [10, 11], and others recommending surgical intervention, as removal of a foreign body may enable higher potential for regeneration of the axons of injured nerve roots, therefore a better functional outcome [12].

The presence of a bullet is not by itself an indication for surgery and frequently does not require removal (thecal sac often heals spontaneously in a closed injury). The decision to perform surgery depends on different main variables: neurologic status, spinal stability, bullet location, injury level, and the existence of a CSF leak.

If a patient is neurologically intact, there are few indications for surgery: evidence of acute lead intoxication, the presence of an intracanal copper bullet, or new onset neurologic deficit. Wide laminectomy may destabilize the spine and lead to late postoperative deformity [4]. Partial spinal cord or nerve root injury due to compression by bone, metal fragment, or hematoma may benefit from surgical decompression in the lumbosacral spine, which may result in motor and sensory improvement [1]. Poor benefit was noted after surgery in complete and partial neurological deficits at the cervical and thoracic levels [4].

Associated fractures are usually stable and rarely require stabilization, but if a bullet in the lumbar or cervical area breaks the pedicle or facet while traversing, it can cause acute or chronic instability that may require stabilization [13]. Patients may undergo a classical open posterior approach, or minimally invasive surgery may be used in selected cases [14]. Surgery is also indicated for complications such as infection, or persistent external CSF fistulae.

Regarding antibiotic prophylaxis in penetrating spine injuries, there is scant evidence in the literature on its type and duration, and recommendations vary from 2 to 14 days of coverage, with the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics in case of viscus perforation [4]. Steroids are not indicated and may increase the risk of non-spinal complications [4, 15].

In our patient, given the CSF leak, the fact that the right L5 pedicle was unstable, and the partial neurological deficit, the decision was made to perform a posterior instrumented L5/S1 fusion with wide laminectomy for exploration, fragment removal and dural repair. The right foramen was completely opened to remove foraminal fragments. For the high L5/S1 disc (increased risk of non-union), no interbody cage was placed during the procedure because of important intraoperative bleeding, surgery duration, and risk of infection in the given context. The patient was proposed a complementary anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) after complete posterior wound healing 2 weeks later, but he refused.

## Operative procedure

Under general anesthesia, with the patient in a prone position on four cushions and the field including the bullet entry hole, incision was made. The spine was exposed subperiosteally from L4 to S1, in a fashion similar to other posterior instrumented surgeries, going laterally to the transverse processes and sacral ala. The right L5 transverse process was completely mobile, confirming the CT scan aspect.

After exposure of the spinous process and laminae, clear CSF could be seen coming from the left L5/S1 interlaminar space, which was temporarily packed with gelfoam. Two pedicle screws were placed on the left side in L5 and S1 and on the right side in S1 with a free-hand technique. For the right L5 pedicle, a reduction forceps was used between the lower edge of the L5 lamina and the upper edge of the L4 lamina. This maneuver put compression on the isthmic area, right pedicle, and the pedicle-body fracture line, which allowed stabilization of the right L5 pedicle and enabled insertion of a pedicle screw under lateral C-arm assistance.

Afterwards, the right foramen was opened, with removal of the right superior articular process of S1 and the right inferior articular process of L5, similar to a classical transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) approach. This revealed two bullet fragments in the foramen and lateral recess that were removed, and the complete absence of the right L5 nerve root in its usual pathway; it was cut and burnt at its origin where it departs from the dura.

A wide L5 laminectomy was then performed and revealed one bullet fragment in contact with neurological elements posteriorly, with an important laceration of the dura and a large defect. Rootlets below the L5 level were exposed with many small debris within the cauda equina. The fragment was removed and debris stuck to rootlets was removed carefully. The dura was suspended bilaterally to clearly visualize its edges and the departing S1 and S2 roots. Partial inferior and central laminectomy of L4 was done to enable good proximal control of the dura suture.

Thorough washing of the neurological elements was performed with a small catheter and serum to evacuate remaining debris. A dural patch was cut to fit the defect and progressively sutured using 4-0 silk sutures. The anesthesiologist was asked to perform a Valsalva maneuver to test the suturing, which revealed minimal and acceptable CSF leakage. The remaining patch was placed on top of the dura and sealed with a dural sealant.

Two titanium rods were inserted and the setscrews tightened. Posterolateral autologous graft was placed after meticulous freshening of the transverse process and ala bilaterally. Intrawound application of 1 g of vancomycin powder was performed at the end of the procedure. A lumbar CSF drain was inserted at L3/L4 to decrease intrathecal pressure at the level of injury. A regular drain without suction was left in the surgical wound.

Post-operatively, the course was uncomplicated. The patient received antibiotic prophylaxis for 3 days with vancomycin, and he was assisted on the first post-surgery day by a physiotherapist with a walking frame for verticalization and gait aiding. The surgical drain was removed on day 3. The spinal drain was removed on day 7.

## Clinical outcome

The patient showed significant improvement of neurological symptoms mainly on the ankle plantar flexors bilaterally, walking independently with the walking frame at the end of his 2-week hospital stay. Anal tone improved and Foley catheter was removed at 4 weeks. A postoperative CT scan confirmed removal of all large bullet fragments and verified implant position, especially in the right L5 pedicle. Surgical wound, bullet entry hole wound, and drain wounds were completely healed at 3 months. At 1 year, he showed a globally satisfactory clinical and radiological result, walking with a cane, but right ankle dorsal extension weakness related to right L5 root damage remained.
