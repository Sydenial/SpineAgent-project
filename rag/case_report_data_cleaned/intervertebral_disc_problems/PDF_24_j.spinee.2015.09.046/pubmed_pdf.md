# Pars/Pedicle Fracture And Screw Loosening Associated With Cortical Bone Trajectory: A Case Series And Proposed Mechanism Through A Cadaveric Study

Wayne K. Cheng, MD, Yusuf T. Akpolat, MD, Serkan Inceoğlu, PhD, Shalin Patel, MD, Olumide A. Danisa, MD

## Background

Cortical bone trajectory (CBT) technique for pedicle screw placement in the lumbar spine has become more popular since its introduction in 2009. The distinct advantages of utilizing CBT technique involve increased screw purchase within cortical bone and reduced surgical dissection. However, contrary to several favorable biomechanical results, there have been anecdotal reports of clinical complications associated with CBT.

## Purpose

First, to report on two unique pars/pedicle fracture cases involving the use of CBT technique; secondly, to perform a cadaveric pilot study to determine the possible mechanism for this fracture pattern.

## Study Design/Setting

Case report and cadaveric study.

## Methods

After presenting two clinical cases, 19 fresh frozen lumbar vertebrae were obtained from eight cadavers. Pedicle screws were instrumented on each level using CBT under video recording. After the instrumentation, X-rays were taken and anatomical dissections were performed.

## Results

To achieve the medial to lateral CBT trajectory, 13 out of 19 (68%) spinous processes had to be removed. A total of 7 complications were observed: one pars/pedicle fracture (2.7%) and six out of 37 trajectory deviations (16.2%) resulted in gross loosening.

The fracture observed during the cadaveric experiment was identical to the fractures previously described in the clinical cases. The fracture started at the screw insertion point on the lateral border of the pars, extended in a semi-sagittal oblique plane through the medial aspect of the superior facet, continued through the pedicle, and exited out the lateral aspect of the pedicle.

Through video analysis, it was clear that both screw loosening and fracture development were caused by the same mechanism. There was no observable abnormality during the initial drill and tap process. However, during final screw placement, the head of the screw impinged medially against the base of the spinous process and lamina, causing a subtle but sudden lateral deviation to the initial tapped trajectory. This deviation resulted in either loss of purchase or, in extreme cases, the fracture of the pars/pedicle.

## Case Reports

### Case 1

A 77-year-old female with a BMI of 27 presented with symptoms of neurogenic claudication and back pain. The patient had failed to respond to conservative therapy. Imaging studies showed central canal stenosis involving L3-L4 and L4-L5 along with grade 1 spondylolisthesis at both levels.

**Surgery**: Decompression was performed at both L3-4 and L4-5 levels. Pedicle screws (5.0 x 30 mm) using the CBT technique were placed on the left side without complication. However, when placing screws using the CBT technique on the right, a fracture through the pars of L3 was encountered. Given the significant pars fracture that had occurred intraoperatively, the use of CBT was aborted and traditional pedicle screw placement was employed on the right side at L3, L4, and L5. Postoperatively, the patient's pain improved, and her ODI and VAS scores were 6 and 0, respectively. The most recent follow-up was 15 months after the initial surgery.

### Case 2

A 56-year-old male with a BMI of 27.8 and a history of hypertension and a 42-year smoking history presented with severe progressive low back and right leg pain. The patient had failed conservative treatments, including medication, therapy, and epidural steroid injections.

**Surgery**: The patient underwent decompression at L5-S1 and posterior instrumentation at L4 and L5 using the CBT technique. Sacral alar screws (7.5 x 30 mm) were placed at S1. Postoperatively, the patient reported modest improvements initially, but after 4 weeks, he began to have increasing low back pain. A CT scan revealed a right pars/pedicle fracture at L5, as well as loosening of both sacral alar and right L5 pedicle screws. The patient required two additional revision surgeries.

## Cadaveric Study

Given the clinical complications, a cadaveric study was performed to determine the mechanism of these fractures. 

19 fresh frozen lumbar vertebrae were obtained from eight cadavers. Bone mineral density (BMD) was quantified using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA). All screws were inserted using the cortical bone trajectory technique. X-rays were obtained after instrumentation, and anatomical dissections were performed.

## Discussion

To our knowledge, there has only been one study in the literature mentioning complications of CBT technique. Glennie et al. retrospectively reviewed records for a 2-year period at their institution and found 5 of 8 patients experienced screw loosening. They suggested that a possible mechanism for this early failure involved the trapezoidal shape of the final construct. 

In a biomechanical study by Paik et al., the effect of the pedicle screw head hubbing against the lamina with a traditional trajectory was examined. Their findings indicated that hubbing led to significantly lower pullout strength and caused fractures in 11 out of 22 screws.

There were multiple limitations to our study, including the small sample size and the fact that the cadaveric study did not replicate in-vivo conditions. Additionally, more upper lumbar vertebrae than lower lumbar vertebrae were tested, which might have falsely increased our complication rate.

## Conclusions

The study observed screw head impingement on the base of the spinous process and lamina in a cadaveric model. This mechanism could potentially explain both screw loosening and fracture formation associated with CBT technique. Possible surgical techniques were proposed based on the cadaveric study to avoid potential complications.
