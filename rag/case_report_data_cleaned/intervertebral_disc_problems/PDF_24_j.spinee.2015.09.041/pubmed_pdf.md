# Extrinsic Thoracic Spinal Cord Compression Related to Supine Position: From Diagnosis to the Creation of a Spinal Protection Shield

## Abstract

**Background:** Rapidly progressing extrinsic spinal cord compression syndromes are rare, especially when the compression is associated with the supine position.  

**Purpose:** This work presents a case of extrinsic thoracic spinal cord compression related to the supine position and the creation of a spinal protection shield.  

**Study Design:** Case report.  

**Patient Sample:** We report the case of a Coptic priest who, as a result of Pott disease sequelae, underwent several decompressive and stabilizing surgeries for major kyphoscoliosis. Consequently, he developed extrinsic thoracic spinal cord compression caused by the supine position.  

**Outcome Measures:** After each instrumentation device removal, progressive severe paraparesis was noted when the patient was supine. Imaging assessment confirmed dynamic and intermittent spinal cord compressions triggered by the supine position, facilitated by exposure and vulnerability of the thoracic spinal cord.  

**Methods:** A tailored titanium mesh spinal protection shield combined with a trapezius flap for spinal coverage was implanted. Diagnostic aspects and surgical technique options are presented.  

**Results:** At 6-year follow-up, the patient’s neurologic condition was significantly improved. Neurologic improvement was observed with no sphincter disorder, persistent spasticity, and lower-limb weakness not affecting full ambulation.  

**Conclusions:** To our knowledge, no other case of spinal protection shield implantation for compression caused by the supine position has been reported. The surgical and technical management remains innovative.

## Introduction

Acute or rapidly progressing spinal cord compression syndromes related to posture are rare and can be difficult to diagnose, especially when neurologic deficits appear only in the supine position. The case discussed here concerns a patient presenting major thoracic post–Pott disease kyphoscoliosis sequelae.

The patient had biologically confirmed pulmonary tuberculosis revealed by extrapulmonary symptoms and complications caused by thoracic Pott disease. Iterative surgery on a vulnerable and deformed spine led to extrinsic spinal cord compression at the T5–T7 apex of a kyphoscoliosis. The diagnosis was challenging, as was the uncommon treatment, which consisted of implantation of a spinal cord protection shield associated with a musculocutaneous flap.

To our knowledge, no similar cases have been reported.

## Clinical and Technical Presentation

A 27-year-old Coptic priest consulted for a spinal disorder caused by T5–T7 thoracic deformation following Pott disease diagnosed at the age of 13. Progressive paraparesis and neurologic deterioration were observed during the preceding months. Computed tomography and plain radiographs demonstrated a 95° kyphotic deformity with an apex at T5–T7.

Corrective surgery was performed through a wide posterolateral T5–T8 laminoarthrectomy. The posterior wedge of the deformity was resected, allowing anterior spinal translocation. Posterolateral fusion associated with T2–T10 transpedicular plate instrumentation was performed to stabilize, rather than correct, the kyphosis. Postoperative evolution was uneventful, and the patient recovered full neurologic function.

Eight years later, instrumentation was removed because of pain. Fusion was confirmed intraoperatively. Six months postoperatively, intermittent paraparesis occurred, identified as position-related. Supine and standing positions triggered symptoms, while lateral decubitus relieved them. Imaging showed a small T5–T7 range of motion and, in the supine position, a spontaneous reduction of the kyphotic apex with severe spinal cord compression at T5–T6.

Following device removal, recovered flexibility of the kyphotic spine induced recurrent compression at the deformity apex. Extensive posterolateral laminoarthrectomy directly exposed the cord to compression when supine. Revision surgery with anterior strut grafts and new posterior instrumentation was performed, with additional cross-links and fusion. The patient recovered ambulation but had persistent spasticity and weakness.

In 2005, after posterior instrumentation removal due to infection, a new progressive spinal cord compression syndrome developed. Scar palpation or the supine position triggered neurologic deficits, whereas lateral decubitus reduced them. Imaging confirmed thoracic spinal cord compression at the laminoarthrectomy level, with thin scar tissue directly covering the cord. The kyphoscoliosis apex was completely fused.

In February 2008, a spinal cord protection shield was implanted via a posterior approach with wide scar excision. A cylindrical titanium mesh device was shaped to fit the exposed thoracic spine and fixed with short cortical screws into the posterolateral fusion mass. The shield overhung the delaminated cord from T5 to T8. Soft-tissue coverage was achieved using a trapezius flap.

Within one year, neurologic condition significantly improved, with no sphincter disorder, persistent spasticity, and mild lower-limb weakness not affecting ambulation. No delayed healing or sepsis occurred. At last follow-up in 2014, the spinal shield remained intact with satisfactory esthetic results.

## Discussion

This report describes a rare case of dynamic extrinsic spinal cord compression triggered by the supine position, associated with multiple surgeries following Pott disease. The context of major thoracic deformity was decisive in the onset of neurologic deficits but not sufficient alone.

Spinal cord compression in Pott disease is usually progressive and related to abscesses or kyphotic deformities [1–3]. In this case, intermittent neurologic deficits were multifactorial, involving kyphoscoliosis, local instability, and significant spinal cord exposure. Supine positioning caused unexpected extrinsic and intermittent compression, with the cord compressed between the kyphotic apex and dorsal soft tissues.

Diagnostic tools were limited. Only MRI and myelography in the supine position successfully revealed compression. Dynamic MRI or electrophysiological studies might be useful in atypical cases [5,7]. Standard surgical strategies were inadequate given the mechanism. Posterior spinal cord shielding was the most appropriate option.

Alternative strategies, such as cross-links, risked recreating posterior compression. Experimental studies have shown higher spinal cord integrity with titanium mesh shielding compared with controls [10]. Similar concepts have been applied in posterior column reconstruction after total en bloc spondylectomy, without neurologic deterioration [11].

Biomechanical complications of spinal cord shields remain unknown due to limited experience. Skin coverage is also challenging, particularly in septic and undernourished patients. In this case, a trapezius musculocutaneous flap provided reliable and reproducible coverage, consistent with previous reports [12–15].

## Conclusions

This case confirms that spinal cord compression related to the supine position exists. Imaging investigations reproducing the triggering position are essential to reveal the compression mechanism. Spinal cord shielding combined with stabilization should be considered. Iterative dorsal approaches may require trapezius flap coverage planning. Prompt recovery and favorable long-term outcomes can be achieved with this strategy.
