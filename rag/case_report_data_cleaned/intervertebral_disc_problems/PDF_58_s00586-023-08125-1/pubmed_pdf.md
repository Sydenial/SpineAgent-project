# Anterior approach for a stab wound with penetrating rebar injury causing incomplete cauda equina syndrome in lumbosacral spine lesion: a case report and literature review

## Abstract

Purpose To report a unique case of incomplete CES following a rebar penetrating injury in the perineal region with a retropulsed fragment, which was treated with an anterior approach, and discuss a suitable surgical approach.

Methods Incomplete cauda equina syndrome caused by non-missile penetrating injury is extremely rare. A 26-year-old male patient presented with incomplete cauda equina syndrome due to a penetrating rebar wound from his perineal region to the lumbosacral spine. Computed tomography demonstrated a bony fragment broken from the S1 body compressing into the spinal canal.

Results By an anterior approach, we performed partial corpectomy of L5, decompression by retrieving the bony fragment, and L5–S1 interbody fusion. The patient had a significant recovery, and no clinical complication was found after over 2-year follow-up.

Conclusion It is challenging to determine the optimal strategy of surgical treatment for penetrating spinal injuries with retained foreign bodies. Here we suggest an anterior approach in situations that has the advantage of being able to effectively perform decompression and prevent iatrogenic damages of the thecal sac and nerve rootlets.

Keywords Penetrating injury · Lumbosacral spine trauma · Stab wound · Incomplete cauda equina syndrome · Anterior approach

Abbreviations CES Cauda equina syndrome; CSF Cerebrospinal fluid; CT Computed tomography; MRI Magnetic resonance imaging

## Introduction

The cauda equina syndrome (CES) manifests as a triad of symptoms including saddle anesthesia, urine and/or bowel dysfunction, and motor paralysis combined with back discomfort. CES is distinguished as complete or incomplete syndrome according to the severity of urination and symptoms. Complete CES presents loss of bladder control, which is not only difficult to micturate but also urinary leaking because of loss of detrusor function. Incomplete CES is characterized by reduced urinary sensation, lack of the urge to urinate, a weak urine stream, and a need to strain of urination [1–3].

Herniation of the central lumbar disc is the most common cause of CES. Less common causes include postoperative hematoma, neoplasm, trauma, complication after chemonucleolysis, and penetrating injuries [1, 3].

Penetrating injuries are less commonly reported as the cause of CES. Gunshot or stab wound is the main type of penetrating spinal injury. Spinal gunshot injuries are more frequently reported and have a higher prevalence of neurologic impairment. On the other hand, neurologic damage following stab wound has been reported extremely rare in the clinical circumstance, but prognosis after a surgical approach has been reported with better neurologic recovery [4, 5].

CES requires urgent surgical decompression, and central lumbar disc herniation, which is the most common cause of CES, is most sufficiently decompressed with posterior laminectomy. However, if the structure located in front of the dura mater is solid and large, isolated posterior decompression does not allow for complete neurologic recovery, and the operator should consider additional iatrogenic dural tear and complications because of dural compression [2, 6]. Especially, if traumatic spinal injuries with retropulsed fragments and incomplete neurologic deficits have been decompressed with posterior laminectomy only, unrelieved tension on nerve roots may progress neurologic deficit by the bowstring effect of a tethered spinal cord [7, 9, 10].

However, there is no definitive study comparing anterior and posterior surgery, whether one approach is superior to the other, and sometimes combined surgery should be considered. Optimal treatment strategy should consider the patient's circumstance including injury mechanism and stability of the posterior osteoligamentous structure.

Here we report a unique case of incomplete CES following a rebar penetrating injury in the perineal region with a retropulsed fragment, which was treated with an anterior approach, and discuss a suitable surgical approach for the bony fragment with a literature review.

## Case report

A 26-year-old male patient presented to our hospital after falling from a height of one meter onto a rebar which vertically penetrated his perineal region. The emergency rescue team found him impaled on the steel ground bar and could not remove the rebar because it was firmly fixed to the ground. Because there was no fluid leakage or active bloody discharge, they rescued him by standing him up from the rebar and transported him to our emergency department as soon as possible.

On neurologic examination, voluntary anal contraction was absent and deep anal sense was markedly decreased. Voluntary urination function was also lost. Other motor and sensory functions were preserved as normal. The patient had no significant accompanying injury such as abdominal or urethral lesion according to relevant specialists' assessment at that time.

Computed tomography (CT) showed that the rebar penetrated the posterior margin of the S1 body, broke a cylindrical bony fragment (20×15×13 mm), and this fragment anteriorly compressed the thecal sac at the level of the fifth lumbar vertebral body on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Finally, we diagnosed the patient as incomplete CES and performed decompression surgery within 8 h after injury. The surgery took 3 h 40 min including autogenous fibular strut bone harvest surgery, with an estimated blood loss of 1.902 L.

We performed an anterior (trans-abdominal) approach with microscopic magnification and partial corpectomy of L5 for a retrieval window $(21\times16\mathrm{x}14\ \mathrm{mm})$ to remove the bony fragment of S1 (20×15×13 mm), followed by autogenous fibular strut bone graft.

The rebar penetrated the posterior longitudinal ligament and outer layer of ligamentum flavum through the interlaminar space of L5 and S1 to the right side of the spinous process and the tip of the L4 spinous process. A bony fragment from the tip of the L4 spinous process was translated to the L3 spinous process level. However, we found almost no damage to the dura and no cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage. After decompression, we performed interbody fusion between L5 and S1 using an autogenous fibular strut bone graft with a fragmentary screw and bone graft at the L5 partial corpectomy site.

Voluntary anal contraction markedly improved immediately postoperatively and voluntary urination was possible at 4 weeks after surgery. We recommended absolute bed rest for 2 weeks and prescribed a low back brace for 6 weeks. The patient started walking with a manual wheelchair after absolute bed rest and walked independently after 3 days of wheelchair ambulation without subsidence of the autogenous fibular bone graft. Immediate postoperative CT and follow-up plain radiographs showed little subsidence of the autogenous fibular strut bone graft. We obtained MRI to confirm expansion and decompression of the cauda equina; however, we did not obtain follow-up CT for bony healing and fusion. We followed the patient for over 2 years and identified no clinical complication. Regular radiographs showed little subsidence of the autogenous fibular strut bone graft, but stability and lumbar lordosis remained well aligned.

## Discussion

The most frequent causes of spinal injuries include falls, motor vehicle accidents, and sports-related mishaps. Compared to injuries caused by physical trauma, penetrating spinal injuries are uncommon [5], and the most typical causes are bullets and knives. However, penetrating rebar injuries to the spine are extremely rare, with only one similar case reported in the literature review. Zhou, Z. S. reported a similar mechanism treated with a posterior approach. In contrast, we report a case of CES following a rebar penetrating injury in the perineal region with a retropulsed fragment decompressed with an anterior approach [10].

CES is a complex neurologic disorder and is managed as a surgical emergency. Traumatic cauda equina injuries are a frequent cause of abrupt and acute neurologic deterioration. Assessment should identify lower extremity sensory and motor impairments during the initial emergency evaluation [5].

On radiographic evaluation, anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the spine should be taken and evaluated immediately. In emergency situations, CT is the ideal investigative modality. If the composition of the object is uncertain, especially when it is close to important structures, or when the patient has a neurologic deficit without detection of canal compression or a trajectory across the canal, MRI is helpful [5, 11].

Immediate radiologic evaluation provides insight into the cause of CES including fractured bony anatomy, foreign material, and tissue material such as disc herniation. These should be clearly defined to allow objective judgments about stability and the three-dimensional relationship between canal and cauda equina before surgical decision-making.

After evaluation and diagnosis of CES, emergency surgical decompression is recommended, especially for incomplete syndrome. However, timing of surgery remains controversial [2, 3, 6, 12–14].

According to a meta-analysis published in 2000, Ahn et al. analyzed 332 CES cases using logistic regression analysis and suggested there was no evidence of neurologic improvement between patients treated before 24 h and those treated within 24–48 h, but there was significant improvement in CES symptoms among patients treated within 48 h [6].

However, Kohles et al. raised concerns about weak methodology, misinterpretation, and quantitative comparison in Ahn's meta-analysis, which could overestimate risk of delayed surgery (24 to 48 h). They emphasized prompt surgical intervention for injured nerve tissue, which can slowly deteriorate due to swelling and tightening of nerves [13].

In 2005, Todd N.V. studied decompression time with internal comparison and suggested that patients treated within 24 h may have more sufficient recovery from bladder dysfunction [7]. Limitations of Todd's meta-analysis were noted, including inappropriate summary statistics and comparisons across clinically heterogeneous studies and fixed-effects models [14].

Chau, A.M. et al. performed a systematic critical review in 2014, including 374 animal and 2802 human clinical studies, and concluded there was no evidence supporting a strict 48 h threshold, but early and delayed surgery may improve neurologic deficits. Given the continuous biologic progression of nerve injury, earlier intervention may result in greater clinical improvement [14].

Compared with published literature, we performed anterior decompression within 24 h after onset of CES. Our patient underwent surgical decompression within 8 h after injury, and we consider that earlier decompression contributed to earlier neurologic recovery from bladder dysfunction.

This patient presented incomplete CES with a retropulsed fracture fragment of S1 compressing the cauda equina anteriorly, and we also considered a risk of infection due to penetrating injury in the perineal region. Therefore, we decided to perform anterior decompression with autogenous fibular strut bone graft. Nevertheless, it remains challenging to determine the optimal surgical strategy for CES patients.

According to current literature, CES is commonly caused by central lumbar disc herniation and requires immediate surgical treatment typically via posterior decompression. Patients with penetrating injury in the lumbosacral region are commonly decompressed with posterior laminectomy and fusion [1–6, 10, 15–19]. Posterior approach is widely recommended for degenerative diseases, and advantages include familiarity to spinal surgeons and good access to neural elements through a single incision. However, surgeons should consider iatrogenic paraspinal muscle injury with prolonged retraction, and hard structures compressing the dura anteriorly may not be adequately decompressed with isolated posterior laminectomy, which can be associated with bowstring effects of neural tethering. Incomplete reduction and neurologic recovery may necessitate additional anterior decompression [7, 9, 19].

On the other hand, anterior approach is recommended for infection and tumor removal, neural decompression, deformity reconstruction, and spine fusion. Advantages include excellent exposure of disc space permitting rapid endplate preparation and facilitating insertion of large implants for correction of lordosis and foraminal height, which may lead to high fusion rate [19].

Kirkpatrick et al. reported that patients with incomplete deficit, especially those with large retropulsed fragments and marked canal compromise, were recommended for anterior decompression to provide adequate visualization of the anterior aspect of the dura mater in traumatic thoracolumbar fracture. Kingwell, S.P. reported that anterior decompression may offer potential benefits in bladder recovery in traumatic cauda equina injury [7, 20, 21].

However, surgeons should consider a patient's history of abdominal surgery, which can cause adhesions and increase risk of vascular injury, the most devastating complication of anterior approach. Mobbs, R. J. reported that spondylolisthesis greater than grade 2 is a contraindication of anterior approach if there is no posterior fusion [19, 20].

In our case, the patient had trans-perineal penetrating trauma with a large retropulsed fragment of S1, nearly completely compressing the dura at L5–S1. First, if we performed posterior laminectomy, the large retropulsed fragment might worsen neurologic deficit due to the bowstring effect and might not be removable completely without iatrogenic dural tear or additional facetectomy. Second, the rebar penetrated the outer layer of ligamentum flavum between the interlaminar space of L5 and S1 to the right side of the L5 spinous process and tip of the L4 spinous process without injury of the facet joint and posterior ligament complex, so we considered that additional posterior fusion or instrumentation was unnecessary. Finally, cage insertion could be a risk factor for infection after penetrating injury.

Thus, we performed partial isolated corpectomy of the L5 vertebral body for removal of the fracture fragment of S1 through an anterior approach. After removal, the compressed dura completely expanded. We confirmed stability by reinforcing the L5 partial corpectomy site with autogenous fibular strut bone graft.

## Conclusion

Incomplete CES caused by non-missile penetrating injury is extremely rare, and to our knowledge this is the first case of CES following a rebar penetrating injury in the perineal region decompressed by an anterior approach in a literature review. Although anterior approach carries a risk of vascular injury, posterior decompression may not allow complete neurologic recovery in the presence of a retropulsed fragment due to bowstring effect of neural tethering. We suggest an anterior approach for sacral lesions with retropulsed fragments to effectively perform decompression and prevent iatrogenic injuries of the thecal sac and nerve rootlets.
