# Posterior lumbar fixation in a kidney transplant recipient: logistics and perioperative challenges

## Abstract
Background Kidney transplantation has become the ideal treatment for medically suitable patients with established kidney disease. This results in increased likelihood of these patients developing unrelated conditions requiring surgery, including spinal surgery. There are only a few publications regarding spinal patients with renal transplants.

Case report A 67-year-old patient presented with recurrent sciatica. Four years prior, he received a living donor kidney transplant. He was diagnosed with right L4 radiculopathy due to recurrent foraminal stenosis as a result of grade I L4/5 spondylolisthesis. He underwent reoperation including microdecompression and posterolateral fixation and fusion. The renal transplant necessitated specific pre- and intraoperative considerations. The knee-chest position with extra padding was used to maintain the region of the renal transplant free from pressure. Renal care was planned in detail by transplant surgeons and nephrologists and shared with ward doctors and on-call teams. The procedure was uneventful; there were no signs of intraoperative or postoperative acute renal injury. The patient was discharged 5 days postoperatively; renal parameters remained within normal ranges and postoperative plain films demonstrated satisfactory surgical results.

Conclusions The key to success was a multidisciplinary approach and detailed planning regarding pre-, intra-, and postoperative care. The presented scheme of care may be useful when considering the posterior approach and prone positioning in kidney transplant recipients with spinal pathologies requiring surgical treatment.

## Introduction
The first successful kidney transplant was performed in 1954 between identical twins. Kidney transplantation has become the ideal treatment for medically suitable patients with established kidney disease due to advances in immunosuppression, histocompatibility, and medical care of renal failure patients. Outcomes have improved and 10-year graft and patient survival following a deceased donor kidney transplant are 72% and 73%, respectively; and following a living donor transplant, 77% and 89% [1]. This has increased the likelihood of these patients developing unrelated conditions requiring surgery, including spinal surgery. There are few reports describing spinal surgery on renal transplant recipients, and none describe related technical and organizational challenges [2-4].

The usual site of a kidney transplant is the retroperitoneal space of the iliac fossa, most commonly the right, although the kidney can be more prominent depending on the relative size of the kidney and the recipient.

Although there is only one paper describing prone positioning of kidney transplant recipients undergoing spinal operations [2], it is reasonable to use that position in the majority of cases requiring instrumentation and/or fusion. However, compression related to suboptimal positioning may result in acute renal injury. Hypertension and loss of function related to extrinsic compression is well described for kidney transplants and is called the Page kidney phenomenon [5,6]. Classically, Page kidney-related renal failure results from compression by a subcapsular hematoma caused by blunt trauma [7,8] or renal biopsy [9]. Although preservation of the compressed renal graft is possible in some cases [10,11], affected recipients often need surgical treatment and may require subsequent retransplantation [8].

A plan for safe intraoperative positioning as well as for pre-, intra-, and postoperative care was developed by a multidisciplinary team (spinal surgery, anesthesia, general surgery and renal transplantation) based on experience, literature, and clinical judgment.

## Case Report
A 67-year-old patient presented with recurrent right-sided sciatica. Four years prior, he received a living donor kidney transplant; apart from an early episode of treated acute cellular rejection, his post-transplant course was unremarkable. He had no other relevant past medical history and no problems with previous anesthetics.

Lumbar spine MRI demonstrated L4/5 stenosis. He underwent L4/5 microdecompression and experienced intermittent improvement in pain and mobility. Several months later, symptoms recurred, particularly on the right side, and he was diagnosed with right L4 radiculopathy due to recurrent foraminal stenosis from grade I L4/5 spondylolisthesis. Reoperation with microdecompression and posterolateral fixation and fusion was offered. The renal transplant necessitated specific pre- and intraoperative considerations.

### Diagnostic Imaging (Fig. 1)
Preoperative MRI showed L4/5 foraminal stenosis with facet joint hypertrophy and degeneration, prior L5/S1 fusion, and the renal transplant in the right iliac fossa (Fig. 1).

## Methods
### Surgical Procedure
The patient underwent posterolateral L4-5 fixation and fusion.

### Patient Positioning
Safe intraoperative positioning was a major concern given that the transplanted right-sided pelvic kidney was palpable above the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS). The lateral position (used during the first operation) was considered safest from a renal perspective but was abandoned due to expected technical difficulties with instrumentation. Standard bolster positioning, Jackson table, and Wilson frame were not appropriate because lower supports localize over the ASISs.

Following advice from the renal and transplant team, the knee-chest position with extra padding was used. The area to remain pressure-free was marked with adhesive plaster as a visible reminder. This position allowed the ASIS region to remain free from pressure due to favorable support point localization. During positioning, care was taken to avoid abdominal compression, especially over the transplanted kidney, with attention to prevent compression related to sliding. The patient was rolled over the contralateral (safe) side during transfer to prone. Operative duration was 159 minutes.

### Perioperative Care
- Prior to hospital admission: A surgical high-dependency unit bed was arranged for postoperative care. The transplant/nephrology team was informed of the medical background and proposed admission date. The anesthesia department was involved in planning.
- Preoperative: The patient was admitted preoperatively and received intravenous fluids (Hartmann's solution 100 ml/h) overnight. Blood tests included full blood count, group and screen for possible cross-match, urea and electrolytes, liver function tests, calcium, and magnesium. Four days prior to the operation, a 7-day course of flucloxacillin (250 mg i.v. every 6 h) was started.
- Perioperative: Extra corticosteroid cover was administered on the morning of surgery, continued for a week postoperatively, then tapered back to baseline immunosuppression. Mannitol bolus (0.5 ml/kg of 10% mannitol) was given at induction, followed by infusion of 0.5 ml/kg/h peri- and postoperatively. The patient was catheterized; urine output was closely monitored with a target of 0.5 ml/kg/h. Routine cefuroxime and gentamicin for spinal instrumentation were modified: gentamicin was omitted due to nephrotoxicity risk. Cefuroxime alone (1.5 g i.v.) was administered on induction, with repeat doses at 8 and 16 hours postoperatively, as an add-on to the flucloxacillin course.
- Postoperative: The patient was kept well hydrated and received regular immunosuppression with extra corticosteroid cover as above. Postoperative fluid intake target: previous hour urine output + 40 ml/h for the first 24-36 hours. The nephrologist adjusted immunosuppressive dosing and fluid/electrolyte replacement on daily rounds.

## Results
The procedure was uneventful with no signs of intraoperative or postoperative acute renal injury. Postoperatively, care followed renal team guidance and all renal parameters remained within normal ranges (Fig. 2). The patient mobilized on postoperative day 2 with physiotherapy and was discharged 5 days after the procedure. Postoperative radiographs demonstrated satisfactory surgical results (Fig. 3).

## Discussion
This case illustrates that posterior lumbar fixation can be performed safely in a kidney transplant recipient when meticulous multidisciplinary planning is applied, with special emphasis on positioning to avoid graft compression and on renal-focused perioperative management. Key milestones of care include:
1. Early multidisciplinary discussions involving transplant surgeons, nephrologists, anesthetists, and intensive care/high-dependency teams.
2. Transplant/nephrology team leadership on immunosuppression and fluid administration.
3. Clear communication so all team members (anesthetists, ward doctors, nurses) recognize the patient as a kidney transplant recipient.
4. Thorough planning of the operative steps and perioperative care, with special attention to positioning and kidney protection; simulate positioning if needed.

## Conclusion
A standardized, multidisciplinary scheme of care with detailed pre-, intra-, and postoperative planning enables safe posterior approaches and prone positioning for kidney transplant recipients requiring spinal surgery.
