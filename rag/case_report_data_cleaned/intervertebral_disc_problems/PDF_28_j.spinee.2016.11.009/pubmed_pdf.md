# Variability in Diagnostic Error Rates of Ten MRI Centers Performing Lumbar Spine MRI Exams on the Same Patient Within a Three Week Period

## Abstract

### Background Context
In today’s healthcare climate, Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is often perceived as a commodity—a service where there are no meaningful differences in quality and thus an area in which patients can be advised to select a provider based on price and convenience alone. If this prevailing view is correct, then a patient should expect to receive the same radiological diagnosis regardless of which imaging center he or she visits or which radiologist reviews the examination. Based on their extensive clinical experience, the authors believe that this assumption is not correct and that it can negatively impact patient care, outcomes, and costs.

### Purpose
This study evaluates whether imaging centers performing a lumbar MRI exam on the same patient over a short period of time will have (1) marked variability in interpretive findings and (2) a broad range of interpretive errors.

### Study Design
A prospective observational study comparing the interpretive findings reported for one patient scanned at 10 different MRI centers over a period of three weeks to each other and to reference MRI exams performed immediately preceding and following the 10 MRI exams.

### Patient Sample
A single patient.

### Methods
The complete set of interpretive findings from the 10 study MRI exams were tabulated and compared for variability and errors. Two of the authors, both subspecialist spine radiologists from different institutions, independently reviewed the reference exams and then came to a final diagnosis by consensus. Errors of interpretation in the study exams were considered present if a finding present or not present in the study exam’s report was not present in the reference exams.

### Outcome Measures
Variability was quantified using percent agreement rates and Fleiss’ Kappa statistic. Interpretive errors were quantified using true positive counts, false positive counts, false negative counts, true positive rate (sensitivity), and false negative rate (miss rate).

### Results
Across all 10 study exams there were 49 distinct interpretive findings reported. No finding was reported in all 10 study exams, and only one finding was reported in nine out of 10 study exams. Overall, 32.7% of the interpretive findings appeared only once across all 10 study exam reports. A global Fleiss’ Kappa statistic, computed across all reported findings, was $0.20\pm0.06$, indicating poor overall agreement on interpretive findings.

The average interpretive error count in the study exams was $12.5{\scriptstyle\pm}3.2$ (both false positives and false negatives). The average false negative count per exam was $10.9\pm2.9$ out of 25 and the average false positive count was $1.6\pm0.9$, corresponding to an average sensitivity of $56.4\%{\scriptstyle\pm11.7}$ and a miss rate of $43.6\%{\scriptstyle\pm11.7}$.

The authors conclude that where a patient obtains their MRI exam and which radiologist interprets the exam may have a direct impact on radiologic diagnosis, subsequent choice of treatment, and clinical outcome.

Keywords: diagnostic accuracy, MRI quality

## Introduction
In the initial evaluation of low back pain despite conservative measures, clinicians may order an MRI exam to assist in explaining the patient’s symptoms in order to determine whether or not modification of the patient’s therapy is required, including referral for interventional pain management and/or surgical evaluation. Moreover, the results of MRI exams play a central role when payers are reviewing whether or not to approve a recommended treatment. Therefore, an accurate diagnosis is paramount to timely and correct treatment.

Several studies provide information as to the variability of interpretation of radiological exams, including MRI exams of the lumbar spine, and the importance of nomenclature when communicating radiologic findings [1–11]. However, these studies provide no information on the variability and quality of interpretation of an MRI diagnosis for a single patient imaged at different imaging centers. The authors believe that this study is the first of its kind and provides critically important and novel insights into the variability and diagnostic performance between MRI exams.

## Methods

### Study Subject and Imaging
The study subject was a 63-year-old female with a history of low back pain and right L5 radicular symptoms. Her pain radiated down to the anterolateral side of her right leg. On examination, she had mild weakness in right ankle dorsiflexion (4+/5) and right great toe extension (4+/5). Reflexes were diminished but symmetrical bilaterally (1/4), and she had a positive straight leg raise.

The subject underwent 12 MRI exams of the lumbar spine. Ten exams were performed at 10 different regional imaging centers over a period of three weeks, along with two reference MRI exams performed immediately preceding and following the 10 study MRI exams.

The reference exams were performed on a closed 1.5T MRI system and included the following sequences: spin-echo T1, spin-echo T2, and STIR sagittal sequences (slice thickness 3.5 mm/no gap; minimum of 24 slices); three stacked overlapping spin-echo T2 axial sequences acquired perpendicular to the central canal and parallel to a disc space (T12 to L3 parallel to the L2–3 disc space; L3 to S1 parallel to the L4–5 disc space; top of L5 to bottom of S1 parallel to the L5–S1 disc space); and a spin-echo T2 coronal sequence acquired parallel to the posterior aspect of the lumbar spine. The study centers performed their routine lumbar spine MRI exam.

### Study Center Selection and Blinding
Study centers were selected based on their location within or close proximity to New York City (for patient convenience) and to include a range of MRI equipment. The 10 MRI centers were blinded to their participation in the study and evaluated the subject as a routine patient. At intake, the subject provided the same history of back pain and leg symptoms to each MRI center and did not reveal participation in a clinical study.

Following completion of the 10 study MRI exams, the MRI reports were stripped of all information identifying the center, radiologist, and type of equipment used. The reports were then reviewed by one author (a subspecialized spine radiologist), and all reported findings (appearing in either the Body or Impression sections) were inserted into a single “Study Exam Data Sheet” for cross-exam comparisons. Interpretive variability was quantified using percent agreement rates and Fleiss’ Kappa statistic.

### Reference Standard and Error Definitions
Two authors, both subspecialist spine radiologists from different institutions with over 25 years of clinical experience, independently reviewed the two reference MRI exams. The only discussion prior to independent interpretation was to confirm the grading system for stenosis [9]. Three minor disagreements related to severity of neural foraminal stenosis were resolved by consensus, and the final set of findings was used as the reference standard.

For evaluation of interpretive errors, the authors limited reference findings to a subset reported in the Spinal Patient Outcomes Research Trial (SPORT) [9,10]. Specifically, reference findings were limited to: disc degeneration, disc herniation, spinal stenosis, nerve root involvement, facet degeneration, and anterior spondylolisthesis. Disc bulge (as distinct from herniation) was not captured since many study exam reports did not make this differentiation.

Central canal stenosis was based on visual assessment of the thecal sac cross-sectional area [9]. The thecal sac area at the disc space level (or most severe stenosis) was compared to the thecal sac area at the pedicle level cephalad to the stenosis. Stenosis was considered mild if reduced by one-third or less, moderate if reduced between one- and two-thirds, and severe if reduced by more than two-thirds. Neural foraminal stenosis was graded similarly by visual assessment of foraminal area reduction: mild (≤ one-third), moderate (between one- and two-thirds), severe (> two-thirds) [9]. Nerve root involvement was diagnosed based on the presence of any pathologic process that abutted, impinged, displaced, or compressed the nerve root, or the presence of an anomalous nerve root.

An error in interpretation in a study exam was considered present if there was no mention in the report of a reference finding (false negative). Any positive finding reported in a study exam that was not present in the reference findings was considered a false positive.

To reduce reporting errors resulting from variation in nomenclature for degenerative disc disease, the authors accepted all of the following to indicate disc degeneration: disc degeneration, disc bulge with reduced T2 nuclear signal intensity, disc desiccation, spondylosis, and decreased disc height.

### Error Metrics
Interpretive errors were quantified using true positive counts, false positive counts, false negative counts, true positive rate (sensitivity), and false negative rate (miss rate). Accuracy was not used because many findings were not reported as negative findings, making quantification of true negatives problematic.

## Results

### Interpretive Variability
There was marked variability in reported findings across the 10 study exams. Across all 10 exams there were 49 distinct findings reported (in either the Body or the Impression section of MRI reports) related to the presence of a distinct pathology at a specific motion segment. The findings included vertebral alignment, disc bulge, disc degeneration/desiccation/spondylosis/decreased disc height, disc herniation, stenosis of the central canal, lateral recesses and neural foramina, nerve root involvement, endplate degeneration, and facet degeneration.

None of the 49 reported findings were unanimously reported in all 10 study exams, and only one finding (anterior spondylolisthesis at L5–S1) was reported in nine out of 10 exams. Overall, 32.7% of interpretive findings only appeared once across all 10 reports.

Agreement across the 10 exam reports was summarized using Fleiss’ Kappa statistic [13]. The overall Fleiss’ Kappa statistic across all reported interpretive findings was $0.20\pm0.03$, indicating poor overall agreement.

Variation was also observed for disc herniation reporting. The number of exams reporting disc herniation at a given motion segment ranged from 70% at L3–4 to 20% at L5–S1; two exams reported disc herniation at all five motion segments, and one exam did not report disc herniation at any motion segment. The number of exams reporting thecal sac compression due to disc herniation ranged from 60% at L1–2 to only one exam reporting thecal sac compression at L4–5. The Fleiss’ Kappa score for agreement on the presence of a disc herniation was $-0.02{\scriptstyle\pm}0.23$ across the five motion segments.

Similar variation existed for reporting stenosis. Central canal stenosis at a given motion segment ranged from 80% at L3–4 to only one exam reporting central canal stenosis at L1–2. Central canal stenosis was reported at four motion segments in two exams and was not reported at any motion segment in two exams. The Fleiss’ Kappa score for agreement on the presence of central canal stenosis was $0.17\pm0.32$ across the five lumbar motion segments.

Only five out of the 10 exam reports included descriptions of any effect of spinal pathology on nerve roots. Nerve root involvement at a given motion segment ranged from 50% at L3–4 to only one exam reporting nerve root involvement at L5–S1. In four study exams, nerve root involvement was reported at three motion segments; in five study exams, nerve root involvement was not reported as present at any motion segment.

### Interpretive Diagnostic Errors
In addition to variability, there was a high rate of interpretive errors across the study exams based on comparison with reference exams. The interpretive miss rates for pathologies ranged from 10% to 47.5%.

An example used for grading central canal stenosis: at the level of the L2 pedicles the thecal sac area measured approximately 241 sq. millimeters, and at the level of the L2–3 disc space the thecal sac area measured approximately 67 sq. millimeters. The reduction was greater than 2/3 and graded as severe stenosis. At L2–3, central canal stenosis was not reported in four study MRI exams, reported as moderate in five, and severe in one (no error was assessed for reporting moderate versus severe since errors were assessed only if stenosis was misgraded by two grades).

Across all study exams, the average interpretive error count was $12.5{\scriptstyle\pm}3.2$ per exam (false positives plus false negatives). The average false negative count was $10.9{\scriptstyle\pm}2.9$, and the average false positive count was $1.6\pm0.9$, corresponding to an average sensitivity of $56.4\%{\pm}11.7$ and miss rate of $43.6\%{\scriptstyle\pm}11.7$.

## Discussion
This study compared lumbar MRI exams for the same patient conducted at 10 unaffiliated imaging centers within a three week period and interpreted by radiologists who were blinded to their participation in the study. The study identified marked variability in reported interpretive findings and an alarmingly high number of interpretive errors in lumbar MRI reports. With respect to variability, no interpretive findings were reported in all 10 study exams and only one finding was reported in nine out of 10 study exams. Overall, 32.7% of interpretive findings appeared only once across all 10 reports. A global Fleiss’ Kappa statistic computed across all reported findings was $0.20\pm0.06$, indicating poor overall agreement on interpretive findings.
