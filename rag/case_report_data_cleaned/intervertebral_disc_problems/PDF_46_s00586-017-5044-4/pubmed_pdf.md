# Spontaneous cervical intradural disc herniation presenting with Brown-Séquard and Horner's syndrome: lesson learned from a very unique case

## Abstract
Purpose Cervical spontaneous intradural disc herniation (IDH) is an extremely rare condition. We describe a unique case of a patient presenting with Brown-Séquard syndrome (BSS) and Horner's syndrome (HS). This study aimed to report an unusual case of spontaneous cervical IDH that presented with HS and BSS and to discuss difficulties in preoperative diagnosis and treatment of intradural cervical disc herniation.

Methods Notes and imaging review, and analysis of the relevant literature.

Results A 45-year-old female presented with acute HS and BSS. Cervical spine magnetic resonance imaging revealed C4-5 disc extrusion with cord compression. The patient underwent urgent decompression through an anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion. The patient fully recovered 6 months after disease onset.

Conclusion Prompt anterior cervical decompression is the treatment of choice, as it directly addresses the problem and allows dural repair in spontaneous cervical disc herniation.

## Introduction
Intradural herniation of intervertebral disc is an unusual manifestation and has most commonly been reported in the lumbar spine [1]. Cervical intradural disc herniation (IDH) is an extremely rare condition, and trauma is the most common aetiology [2]. We describe a case of spontaneous cervical IDH associated with ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) and presenting with HS and BSS (Table 1).

## Case Report

### History and Neurological Examination
A 45-year-old woman presented with an acute onset of severe neck pain radiating to the right upper limb, right-sided hemiparesis, right eye ptosis, miosis, and anhidrosis. Neurological examination was consistent with BSS and HS.

### Imaging
Cervical MRI showed a central disc-osteophyte bar with focal acute right paracentral disc extrusion that had migrated cranially, compressing the right hemicord at the C4-5 level.

### Surgical Procedure
The patient underwent urgent spinal cord decompression through an anterior C4 corpectomy. A tear was noted in the posterior longitudinal ligament (PLL) on the right side posterior to the OPLL mass. A dural defect was clearly visualized with a herniated disc fragment within it. The dura was closed. The cervical spine was reconstructed with a Bengal interbody fusion cage filled with autogenous bone and a Skyline cervical plate (DePuy Synthes, MA, USA).

### Outcome
The postoperative course was marked by immediate pain relief and gradual recovery of right-sided hemiparesis. HS was still present after surgery. Clinical examination 6 months after surgery revealed complete resolution of BSS and HS.

## Discussion
Cervical IDH is an extremely rare clinical entity and, due to its rarity, it remains difficult to definitively diagnose before surgery. The first report of lumbar intradural disc herniation was in 1942 by Dandy [3], and cervical IDH was reported in 1959 by Marega [4]. To the best of our knowledge, there have been around thirty cases published so far and only one case was associated with OPLL [2].

The true cause of IDH is unknown, but most commonly it is precipitated by trauma on the background of degenerative disc disease. Chronic inflammation in OPLL causes scarring and adhesion of the adjacent dura mater to the PLL [5, 6]. The adherent and fragile dura can be easily perforated by an extruded disc. In our case, disc extrusion was not associated with previous trauma, and intraoperatively we found that the calcified PLL was firmly attached to friable dura.

The classical presentation of cervical disc herniation is radiculopathy or myelopathy. Other neurological syndromes such as BSS and HS have been described as first presenting symptoms in patients with IDH [3–11]. Patients with sudden onset hemiplegia are initially assessed to rule out stroke, intracranial space-occupying lesions, or other neurological conditions. In our case, the diagnosis of severe cervical spinal cord compression was made incidentally during radiological assessment for a brain stroke. Being HS consistent with posterior circulation stroke [12], the cervical spine was also imaged.

BSS is rarely caused by herniated disc, but in cases of severe unilateral cord compression due to herniated disc it can be the first clinical presentation of cervical disc herniation [3, 11]. According to Kobayashi et al., the degree of compression, together with the individual distribution of the anterior spinal artery, may account for the level of severity of the sensory deficit [8]. The prognosis of neurological recovery following BSS is slightly worse in cervical IDH compared to extradural cases [13], but good recovery is still achievable in the majority of cases.

The presence of HS in spontaneous cervical IDH is exceptionally rare, and it has been reported in only two traumatic cases [1, 14]. The oculosympathetic pathway connecting the hypothalamus and the orbit passes through the ciliospinal center at the level of C8–T2. Direct pressure of the herniated disc on the spinal cord produces compression of the first-order neuron of the sympathetic pathway at the C4-5 level, interrupting it between the hypothalamus and the orbit [14], as occurred in our case.

Different surgical approaches have been used to address cervical IDH, and variability in outcomes has been reported [1, 7]. In our case, we performed corpectomy that allowed direct visualization of the intradural disc prolapse with complete decompression of the spinal cord. Primary dural closure was also easily done through the anterior approach. Our patient fully recovered 6 months after the procedure (Fig. 1).

## Conclusions
Spontaneous cervical IDH should be considered as part of the differential diagnosis in patients presenting with acute onset of BSS and HS. Prompt surgical intervention allows a good clinical outcome and complete recovery. An anterior approach is the treatment of choice, as it directly addresses the problem.
