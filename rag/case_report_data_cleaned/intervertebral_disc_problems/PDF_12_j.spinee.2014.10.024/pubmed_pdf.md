# Ankylosing Spondylitis With Idiopathic Spinal Cord Herniation

## Introduction
Idiopathic spinal cord herniation, unlike spinal cord herniation with a known traumatic or postoperative origin, is a relatively rare condition. It was first reported by Wortzman et al. [1] in 1974. Such herniation most often occurs between the T4 and T7 vertebrae, a spinal segment that includes the area of physiologic kyphosis [2]. Brown-Sequard syndrome is the most frequently reported clinical feature [3].

## Case Report
A 56-year-old man with a 20-year history of ankylosing spondylitis presented with progressive numbness and weakness in the right lower limb for 2 months.

### Imaging Findings
This exemplar case of idiopathic spinal cord herniation shows, on magnetic resonance imaging, an acute, anterior kink of the spinal cord at the L1 level, the apex vertebra of kyphosis, and enlargement of the dorsal subarachnoid space without any apparent filling defect (Figure A). Cerebrospinal fluid flows in the subarachnoid space posterior to the herniated spinal cord (Figure B). This feature may allow differentiation of spinal cord herniation from an arachnoid cyst [4]. The herniated spinal cord produces an arch calcific lesion in the posterior L1 vertebra on computed tomography images (Figure C and D).
