# Extremely high preoperative C7 slope limits compensatory cervical lordosis after muscle-preserving selective laminectomy

## Abstract
Purpose To determine the effect of an extremely high C7 slope on C2–C7 lordotic compensation following selective laminectomy (SL).

Methods We enrolled 151 cervical compressive myelopathy (CCM) patients who underwent SL. Lateral cervical spine radiographs were taken before surgery and at final follow-up. Patients were divided into extremely high C7 slope (EH; ≥30°) and non-high C7 slope (NH; <30°) groups, and the influence of a high C7 slope on radiological and surgical outcomes was examined.

Results Mean age was higher in group EH (p < 0.001). Preoperatively, group EH had a larger C2–C7 sagittal vertical axis (SVA) (p = 0.001) and greater cervical lordosis (p < 0.001). Although C2–C7 SVA increased after surgery, mean C2–C7 angle in group EH decreased. Mismatch between C7 slope and C2–C7 angle increased postoperatively in group EH (p = 0.015). Postoperative Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA) score and recovery rate (RR) were slightly lower in group EH (p = 0.001 and p = 0.006, respectively). Multiple linear regression showed that an extremely high C7 slope, not age, affected RR of JOA score (p = 0.006).

Conclusion Patients in group EH were older and had highly compensated cervical sagittal alignment preoperatively. They demonstrated postoperative increases in cervical sagittal balance mismatch and slightly worse functional recovery. An extremely high C7 slope limited compensatory cervical lordosis following SL.

## Introduction
Cervical sagittal alignment is a determining factor of surgical and radiological outcomes of cervical spine surgery [1–3]. The T1 slope contributes to cervical and global sagittal alignment [4]. Likewise, the C7 slope is a marker of overall spinal sagittal alignment by linking the occipitocervical and thoracolumbar spine [5]. Few studies have examined the relationship between preoperative T1 (or C7) slope and radiological or functional outcomes [6–8]. Two studies identified greater postoperative kyphotic changes in laminoplasty patients with higher preoperative T1 slope [6, 8]. Among patients undergoing muscle-preserving selective laminectomy (SL), more postoperative lordotic changes and comparable functional recovery were observed in patients with a higher C7 slope [7]. SL, in which decompression laminae are selected without disturbing the deep extensor muscles (DEMs) or facet joints, has been used at our institution to decompress CCM for over 12 years, minimizing posterior structural invasiveness and maintaining cervical alignment [7, 9].

As C7 slope increases, the C2–C7 angle compensates into lordosis to maintain cervical sagittal balance and horizontal gaze [7, 10, 11]. We speculated that patients with an “extremely” high C7 slope (≥ average + 1 SD, 30°) whose cervical sagittal alignments were highly compensated by preoperative C2–C7 hyper-lordosis would reveal limits of postoperative cervical lordosis as a compensatory mechanism. We divided patients into EH and NH groups and examined the impact of an extremely high C7 slope on radiological and functional outcomes.

## Methods

### Subjects
Between April 2010 and December 2013, 278 consecutive patients underwent SL for CCM at our institution. Inclusion criteria: age ≥ 18 years, symptomatic CCM (≥1 clinical sign of myelopathy), and evidence of CCM on cervical myelography–computed tomography (CM–CT). Exclusion criteria: radiculopathy without myelopathy; prior foraminotomy or cervical surgery; ankylosing spondylitis, rheumatoid arthritis, infection, tumor, or trauma. Surgical indications for SL were: (1) local kyphosis < 20° [7]; (2) spondylolisthesis < 3.5 mm [12]; (3) occupying ratio of ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) < 60% [13]. Preoperative CM–CT was performed to determine OPLL presence and type. Because C6 was the most common surgically treated level, patients whose SL included C6 were selected. The study included 151 CCM patients who underwent C6 SL (12 cases), C5–C6 SL (38 cases), C4–C6 SL (74 cases), and C3–C6 SL (27 cases).

### Operative Procedure
SL was performed as previously described [7, 9, 14]. Selective monolaminectomy was used to decompress two adjacent intervertebral levels when indicated [7, 9, 14]. For C3–C6 SL, the C2–C3 to C6–C7 interlaminar spaces were exposed through the bilateral interspinalis muscles without detaching DEMs [9]. The C3–C6 spinous processes were split longitudinally and divided at the base without damaging bilateral DEMs. The C3–C6 laminae, the upper half of the C7 lamina, and the yellow ligament on the ventral aspect of the C2 lamina were removed, achieving adjacent five-level decompression (C2–C7). Split spinous process fragments (C3–C6) were sutured together.

Decompression levels were determined preoperatively by complete obstruction of the subarachnoid space on CM–CT with the neck in neutral and extended positions. Laminectomy width was set to 2–3 mm wider than the spinal cord width measured at the upper edge of each lamina on CM–CT [7, 14]. Mean laminectomy width was 15–19 mm; bilateral facet joints were never exposed.

### Patient Characteristics and Outcome Measures
Clinical characteristics (age, sex, diagnosis, operative level, perioperative complications) were recorded. The JOA score system for cervical myelopathy was used preoperatively and at final follow-up (> 1 year after surgery). Hirabayashi’s method was used to calculate recovery rate (RR) of the JOA score [15].

### Evaluation of Radiological Findings
Standing lateral, flexion, and extension radiographs of the cervical spine were obtained preoperatively and at final follow-up. Neutral lateral radiographs were taken with patients in a comfortable standing position, facing forward with horizontal gaze.

- C7 slope: angle between the superior endplate of C7 and a horizontal line. C7 slope was used instead of T1 slope because T1 was difficult to measure in many patients [7, 16, 17].
- C2–C5, C5–C7, and C2–C7 angles: measured by tangential lines along the posterior borders of the respective vertebral bodies.
- C2–C7 SVA: horizontal distance between the C2 plumb line and the posterior superior corner of C7.
- Cervical range of motion (ROM): difference in C2–C7 angle between flexion and extension.

Two independent spine surgeons analyzed the images using a DICOM viewer. Interobserver reliability assessed by intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC[2,1]) showed strong correlations (> 0.8) for each radiological parameter.

### Statistical Analysis
SPSS (version 22.0; IBM) was used. Continuous variables are mean ± SD. Pre- vs postoperative radiological parameters were compared using unpaired t-tests. Correlations between C7 slope and age or radiological factors were analyzed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Variables were compared between EH (preoperative C7 slope ≥ 30°) and NH (< 30°) using unpaired t-tests for continuous variables and Chi-square or Mann–Whitney U tests for discrete variables. Factors with p < 0.05 in univariate analyses between EH and NH were examined by multiple linear regression for RR of JOA score. Significance was set at p < 0.05.

## Results

### Patient Demographics and Radiological Parameters
A total of 151 patients (96 males, 55 females) were included. Mean follow-up was 27.2 ± 11.5 months. Perioperative complications included two cases of C5 palsy (1.3%), one superficial infection (0.7%), and one spinal epidural hematoma (0.7%). Postoperative C2–C5 lordotic change (p = 0.001) and C5–C7 kyphotic change (p = 0.004) were observed. Mean cervical ROM decreased slightly postoperatively (p < 0.001).

## Case Presentation
A 74-year-old man underwent C4–C6 SL. Preoperative C2–C7 angle, C7 slope, C7 slope minus C2–C7 angle, and C2–C7 SVA were 23.6°, 35.4°, 11.8°, and 28.8 mm, respectively (EH group). At 2 years and 1 month postoperatively, C2–C7 angle, C7 slope, C7 slope minus C2–C7 angle, and C2–C7 SVA were 11.2°, 35.3°, 24.1°, and 37.6 mm, respectively. Preoperative JOA, postoperative JOA, and RR were 10, 10.5, and 7.1%. A kyphotic alignment change, postoperative increase in C2–C7 SVA, and increased C7 slope minus C2–C7 angle were observed.

## Discussion
Patients with an extremely high C7 slope were older, more likely to have lordotic cervical alignment, and had larger preoperative C2–C7 SVA. After surgery, C2–C7 SVA increased and C2–C7 angle decreased in EH patients. A postoperative increase in the mismatch between C7 slope and C2–C7 angle was also observed. Surgical outcome was slightly worse among EH patients. Multiple linear regression indicated that only an extremely high C7 slope affected RR of JOA score, suggesting that a very high preoperative C7 slope limits compensatory cervical lordosis after SL.

C7 slope is an important marker of overall spinal sagittal alignment and links the occipitocervical and thoracolumbar spine [5]. It is easier to determine the superior endplate of C7 than T1 in many patients [5]. C7 slope correlates with C2–C7 angle, occiput–C2 angle, sacral slope, and C7 SVA [5], consistent with our findings of significant correlations with segmental angles, SVA, and ROM. Prior work showed T1 slope increases with age in asymptomatic individuals [11]; we observed a similar age correlation for C7 slope.

Unlike prior analyses using “relatively” high (≥ median) C7 slope [7], we separated patients with “extremely” high (≥ average + 1 SD) C7 slope (EH) from those with lower slopes (NH). NH patients demonstrated postoperative lordotic change in C2–C7 angle with increases in SVA, whereas EH patients had kyphotic change in C2–C7 angle with SVA increases. These findings indicate that the compensatory C2–C7 lordotic change seen in NH patients was absent in EH patients, suggesting that an extremely high preoperative C7 slope limits postoperative cervical lordotic compensation. Previously, SL including lower cervical laminae often caused slight postoperative C5–C7 kyphosis along with compensatory C2–C5 lordosis. In this study, EH patients, who had higher preoperative C2–C5 lordosis, showed smaller postoperative C2–C5 lordotic gains than NH patients, contributing to postoperative C2–C7 kyphotic change.

T1 slope minus C2–C7 angle has been considered a cervical analog to pelvic incidence minus lumbar lordosis mismatch [8, 18]. A higher value suggests uncompensated or kyphotic cervical alignment [8, 18]. Here, NH patients had postoperative decreases in C7 slope minus C2–C7 angle, indicating better compensation after surgery. In contrast, EH patients had postoperative increases in this mismatch, reflecting limited lordotic compensation.

Slightly worse functional recovery was observed in EH. Because EH patients were older, we performed multiple linear regression; the lower RR in EH was attributable to the extremely high C7 slope, not age. C7 slope should be assessed when evaluating SL outcomes, especially in elderly patients. Whether cervical fixation surgery improves outcomes for EH patients remains unclear, and must be weighed against greater invasiveness [23–25]. Surgical strategy should be individualized based on age, comorbidities, and social factors.

Limitations include the retrospective design and limited sample size, potential confounders, and lack of whole-spine radiography and HRQOL assessment. Prospective studies with larger cohorts, whole-spine imaging, and HRQOL are needed to clarify the influence of a high C7 slope on outcomes after SL.

## Conclusion
Older patients with an extremely high preoperative C7 slope (≥ 30°) and highly compensated preoperative cervical sagittal alignment demonstrated postoperative increases in cervical sagittal balance mismatch. An extremely high preoperative C7 slope limited compensatory cervical lordosis after SL.
