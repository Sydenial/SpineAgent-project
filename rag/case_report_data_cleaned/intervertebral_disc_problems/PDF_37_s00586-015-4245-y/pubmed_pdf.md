# Traumatic L4-5 Bilateral Locked Facet Joints

## Abstract
Traumatic bilateral locked facet joints occur with extreme rarity in the lumbar spine; only three case reports were identified in the literature. We report a 36-year-old male with bilateral L4-5 facet fracture-dislocations after a head-on motor vehicle collision. Imaging showed disruption of the posterior elements, grade II anterolisthesis of L4 on L5, and an epidural hematoma causing severe canal stenosis; the patient was neurologically intact at presentation. He underwent open reduction with L3 to S1 pedicle screw fixation and posterolateral arthrodesis for this highly unstable injury. We review the existing literature and lumbar biomechanics and propose a mechanism for these dislocations.

Keywords: bilateral locked facet joints; pedicle screw fixation; spinal biomechanics

## Introduction
Traumatic jumped or locked facets in the lower lumbar spine are rare. To date, three cases of bilateral locked facets at the L4-5 level have been reported [1-3]. We present a young male with bilateral L4-5 jumped facets after a motor vehicle collision, detailing the history, radiologic findings, treatment course, and outcomes. We also compare prior reports and discuss the biomechanical features of the lower lumbar spine relevant to these injuries.

## Methods
PubMed was searched using: "(L4-5 OR L4-L5) AND dislocation"; "(L4-5 OR L4-L5) AND locked facets"; "(L4-5 OR L4-L5) AND jumped facets"; "(L4-5 OR L4-L5) AND traumatic spondylolisthesis". Three English-language cases of bilateral locked facets at L4-5 were identified [1-3].

## Clinical Presentation
A 36-year-old restrained passenger in a head-on collision arrived neurologically intact, with full lower-extremity strength, intact pinprick and light touch sensation, normal rectal tone, and normal deep tendon reflexes.

Computed tomography of the lumbar spine revealed bilateral jumped facets at L4-5 with a small fracture of the right L5 superior articular process, grade II anterolisthesis of L4 on L5, and severe canal narrowing. Additional injuries included fractures of the right transverse processes from L1 through L5, a fracture of the anterior superior L5 vertebral body, and a displaced fracture of the L4 spinous process. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated rupture of the L4-5 disc; disruption of the anterior and posterior longitudinal ligaments, ligamentum flavum, interspinous, and supraspinous ligaments; and an epidural hematoma causing severe canal stenosis at L4-5.

The patient underwent open reduction with evacuation of the epidural hematoma, followed by L3 to S1 pedicle screw fixation and posterolateral arthrodesis using autograft and allograft. After treatment of concomitant fractures and a pneumothorax, he was discharged home without neurological deficit. No intra-abdominal organ injuries were sustained.

## Discussion
Bilateral facet dislocations are well described in the cervical spine but are rare in the lower lumbar region. Most previously reported bilateral lower lumbar jumped facets involve the lumbosacral junction [4-7]. Only three reports describe traumatic bilateral locked facets at L4-5 [1-3].

### Biomechanical Considerations
Lumbar sagittal plane range of motion increases from L1-2 to a maximum at L4-5 (average approximately 16 degrees) and then decreases at L5-S1 [8]. The L5-S1 facets are more coronally oriented, whereas the L4-5 facets are more sagittally oriented [8]. Consequently, hyperflexion or flexion-distraction forces may more readily produce L5-S1 dislocations, while the L4-5 joints tolerate these forces within their physiologic range. This may explain the higher frequency of L5-S1 dislocations despite stronger ligamentous support at the lumbosacral junction.

Three of four patients (including our index case) with L4-5 locked facets were restrained occupants in head-on collisions and had extensive adjacent injuries such as transverse or spinous process fractures, implying high-energy forces and failure of supporting muscles and ligaments [2, 3, 9-12]. One case involved a thin (44 kg) 32-year-old woman who wore her shoulder harness under the axilla; the authors suggested that low muscle mass and incorrect belt use facilitated the dislocation [2]. In the other reports, including ours, patients did not have low body mass and used seatbelts correctly [1, 3].

During head-on collisions, the waist harness can splint the pelvis and L5-S1 segment, while the shoulder harness acts as a thoracic fulcrum. Forward momentum can translate into anterior and rostral forces on the lower lumbar spine. With the pelvis stabilized, extension-distraction forces on the L4-5 facet complex may pull the L4 inferior facets over the L5 superior facets, producing dislocation. The stronger ligamentous support and greater extension capacity at L5-S1 may confer relative protection [8]. The larger lever arm from a thoracic fulcrum and rapid transition to the robust lumbosacral ligaments may render L4-5 more susceptible. The presence of multiple transverse process fractures and extensive posterior ligamentous injury supports the high-energy nature of these forces. The minimal bony injury to the L5 superior facet in our case, despite profound posterior ligamentous disruption, suggests unusual and extreme loading patterns.

## Treatment Options
Two reports achieved canal decompression via complete L4 and superior L5 laminectomies and reduced the dislocation by resecting the L5 superior articular processes [1-3]. Instrumentation was placed at L4-5 in two cases [2, 3], with one construct extended rostrally to L3; interbody arthrodesis was performed in all [1-3]. Interbody fusion or construct extension may be selected based on the extent of discectomy for disc fragmentation/herniation, posterior column compromise, and bone quality or vertebral body fractures affecting screw purchase.

In our case, extensive posterior tension band injury (large paraspinal muscle hematoma from L3 to the sacrum and gross ligamentous incompetence) prompted extension of instrumentation from L3 to S1. Interbody fusion was not performed because L4-5 disc disruption was limited and the posterior annulus was relatively preserved, with no canal compromise from the disc or fragments necessitating radical discectomy. Large pedicles and good bone quality provided excellent screw purchase, and the longer construct was deemed sufficient for stabilization.

## Outcomes
Given the wide lower lumbar canal, extensive neurological deficits were uncommon at presentation [1-3]. One patient exhibited bilateral lower-extremity numbness and weakness, though distribution was not specified [1]. After treatment, all patients returned to their neurologic baselines and were symptom-free on follow-up.

## Conclusion
Isolated bilateral traumatic L4-5 jumped facets are extremely rare, high-energy injuries. Seatbelt use in head-on collisions may alter lumbar biomechanics, producing extension-distraction forces at L4-5 that drive dislocation. Reported cases had no permanent neurologic deficits and were safely reduced and instrumented with varying constructs. The extent of instrumentation and arthrodesis should be guided by posterior tension band damage. Reduction can be safely achieved by removing the superior L5 articular facet with limited lateral osteotomies. Interbody arthrodesis has yielded good results but is not always necessary when stabilization is adequate and the posterior annulus is preserved.
