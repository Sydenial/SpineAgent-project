# Management of fracture dorso-lumbar spine in a pregnant female by surgery in prone position  

## Abstract
Surgical management of thoraco-lumbar spine fracture in pregnancy has specific concerns during surgery like positioning, approach, and radiation exposure to the fetus. We report a case of burst fracture of the 12th thoracic vertebra with paraplegia in a young female with 26 weeks of gestation. Surgery was performed in a prone position on a Toronto frame. This report also discusses the management of thoraco-lumbar spine fracture in pregnancy with a review of literature.

**Keywords**: Dorso-lumbar fracture, Pregnancy, Toronto frame  

## Introduction
Fracture spine in pregnancy is uncommon. They occur following traumatic events; however, relative osteoporosis of pregnancy is found to be associated. There are only a few reports which discuss the management of thoracolumbar spine fractures in pregnancy. We report a case of a 26-week pregnancy with a traumatic fracture of the twelfth thoracic vertebra (D12) with paraplegia, which was operated in a prone position on a Toronto frame.

## Case History
A 28-year-old female with 26 weeks of gestation (G3P2L2) had a history of falling from a ladder. She was referred to the casualty from another primary care center and presented after 36 hours with severe back pain and inability to move both lower limbs since the fall. She had no history of vaginal bleeding, abdominal pain, or loss of consciousness. Pulse was 88/min, blood pressure was 128/76 mmHg. Her fundal height on examination was approximately 26 weeks, fetal heart rate was 156/min. Per vaginal examination was normal. Spine examination revealed tenderness at the dorso-lumbar junction. Neurologically, the patient had total spinal cord injury below the L1 segment level, which was American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) grade A neurological impairment. Knee and ankle reflexes were exaggerated. Urgent ultrasonography (USG) of the abdomen showed a normal fetus with no other organ injury. After taking consent from the patient, a plain radiograph of the thoraco-lumbar spine was taken. X-ray showed anterior wedging of the D12 vertebra. MRI showed a burst fracture of the D12 vertebra with cord compression. A decision for posterior surgical decompression was made, after explaining the risk of maternal and fetal complications. The Toronto frame was assembled and fixed to the radiolucent operating table. The patient was placed in a prone position on the Toronto frame, and the height of the frame was adjusted so that the abdomen remained free. The major advantage of this frame was minimal pressure on the abdomen or pelvis, while the patient was supported in the prone position by her shoulder and iliac crest. Before the procedure, Fetal Heart Sounds (FHS) were assessed with handheld Doppler, and intraoperative monitoring of fetal heart sounds was done by cardiotocogram with FHS belt. Care was taken to avoid blood loss and hypovolemia, which could cause fetal bradycardia. Fetal bradycardia, if occurred, was managed with rapid intravenous Ringer lactate infusion, high-flow oxygen inhalation, and, if required, left lateral positioning. The procedure was completed rapidly to achieve hemostasis and prevent hemorrhage. A D12 laminectomy with posterior decompression and short-segment pedicular screw fixation was done. Postoperative monitoring did not show any evidence of fetal distress. The patient's neurological status remained the same during her hospital stay. At discharge, paraplegia care instructions were provided, and the patient was mobilized in a wheelchair with a Taylor's brace. At 39 weeks of pregnancy, the patient delivered a healthy 2.8-kg baby by cesarean section under general anesthesia. At the most recent follow-up (2 years), the patient remained paraplegic with partial recovery of bowel and bladder function.

## Discussion
The management of spine fractures in pregnancy is poorly described in orthopedic literature. Most of the reported cases focus on the obstetric aspects. Surgical indications involve comparing the relative risks and benefits of two different treatment methods. Surgical management of spinal fractures in pregnancy carries a risk to both the mother and fetus due to positioning, blood loss, and the inherent risk of anesthesia. Conservative management, on the other hand, precludes early mobilization, thus exposing the patient to hazards of immobilization, such as deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary complications. An individualized approach is recommended in managing spinal surgery in pregnancy. It is always prudent to postpone surgery until after delivery if the patient is near term. However, in earlier weeks of pregnancy, unstable spinal column injury and incomplete neurological deficit are definite indications for surgery.

The surgical approach and positioning for these thoracolumbar fractures in pregnancy is also a debated issue. An anterior approach has the advantage of avoiding pressure on the gravid uterus. However, in later weeks of gestation, where the gravid uterus reaches up to the xiphisternum, approaching the thoraco-lumbar spine via an anterior approach becomes difficult. The posterior approach has the disadvantage of pressure on the gravid uterus due to positioning and the need for radiation exposure for pedicular screw fixation. Relton et al. described a specialized frame (Toronto frame) for reducing blood loss in scoliosis surgery in a prone position by relieving the extrinsic pressure on the inferior vena cava. The frame consists of four supports with a 45° inward tilt, arranged in two V-shaped pairs, supporting the lateral aspects of the upper thoracic cage and antero-lateral aspects of the pelvic girdle. With suitable adjustment, this frame provides adequate support and prevents extrinsic pressure on the abdomen. The use of this frame in our patient worked to our advantage, allowing us to position her prone without causing external pressure on the gravid uterus.

The hazardous effects of radiation exposure are a concern in stabilizing spinal fractures via the posterior approach. The potential biological effects of in utero radiation exposure to a developing fetus include prenatal death, intrauterine growth restriction, small head size, mental retardation, organ malformation, and childhood cancer. The gestational age and level of absorbed dose are important factors in determining the occurrence of these effects. Most teratogenic effects of radiation occur due to exposure during the first trimester. After the 25th week, major fetal malformations and functional anomalies are highly unlikely. In our patient, because of the 26 weeks of gestation, we could use fluoroscopy for pedicular screw fixation, taking care to keep radiation exposure to a minimum.

Pregnancy complicated by traumatic paraplegia carries a risk to the fetus. Goller et al. performed a retrospective investigation on pregnancy in paraplegic patients. He concluded that pregnancy complicated by paraplegia is more threatened than in normal circumstances due to:
1. The trauma itself.
2. The immediate posttraumatic situation of the patient.
3. Chronic infections and anemia in pregnancies following spinal cord injury.

He also found that malformations and disabilities occurred in that group of patients who became paraplegic during the course of pregnancy.
