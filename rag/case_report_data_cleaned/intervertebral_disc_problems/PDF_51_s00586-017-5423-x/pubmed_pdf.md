# A technical case report on use of tubular retractors for anterior cervical spine surgery

## Abstract
Aim To report an anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion (ACCF) and a two-level anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) using a minimally invasive approach with tubular retractors.

Methods First case: cervical spondylotic myelopathy secondary to a large postero-inferiorly migrated disc treated with corpectomy and reconstruction with a mesh cage and locking plate. Second case: cervical disc herniation with radiculopathy treated with a two-level ACDF. Both cases were operated using a minimally invasive approach with a tubular retractor via a single incision. Technical aspects and clinical outcomes are reported.

Results No intra- or post-operative complications were encountered. Intra-operative blood loss was negligible. The patients had a cosmetic scar on healing. Standard placement of tubular retractors provided adequate surgical exposure with minimal invasiveness.

Conclusion Minimally invasive anterior cervical spine surgery with tubular retractors is feasible. This is the first report on the use of a minimally invasive approach for ACCF and two-level ACDF.

Keywords Tubular retractors · Cervical spine · Minimal invasive spine surgery · Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion · Spinal fusion

## Introduction
The anterior approach to the cervical spine is used to treat numerous disorders including degenerative [1,2], traumatic, oncological, inflammatory, congenital [3,4], vascular, and infective etiologies [2]. Cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) is a common cause of neurologic morbidity and can substantially decrease quality of life [5]. Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) and anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion (ACCF) are well-accepted procedures for symptomatic cervical myelopathy, with excellent fusion rates [5-7]. Despite their success, complications such as dysphagia, hematoma formation, recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy, and esophageal perforation have been reported [8,9]. In anterior cervical surgery, prevertebral muscle dissection for visualization can induce neck discomfort and slow recovery [10]. Minimally invasive spine surgery aims at faster recovery with earlier return to normal activities. Tubular retractor systems such as METRx and MAST QUADRANT (Medtronic Sofamor Danek) are extensively utilized to accomplish surgical goals with minimal access in posterior-based MIS procedures of the lumbar, thoracic, and cervical spine.

There is limited literature on the use of minimal-access tubular retractors to decompress and reconstruct the anterior cervical spine [11-13]. Here, we report two cases in which a tubular retractor system was used in anterior cervical surgery. Unlike previously published single-level cases, tubular retractors were utilized to perform a corpectomy and a two-level discectomy using minimal access. These cases highlight the feasibility of decompression and fusion for multilevel cervical pathologies via a minimally invasive approach.

## Case Report

### Case 1
A 49-year-old male office worker presented with neck pain and left upper limb radicular pain for 1 year, and 3 months of gait instability, paresthesia, and subjective heaviness in both lower limbs. Conservative measures provided no relief. Examination revealed painful neck range of motion, myelopathic features with hyperreflexia and bilateral extensor plantar response, and mild weakness of left wrist extension. MRI revealed a large C4-5 disc herniation with inferior migration behind the C5 body, with significant cord compression and cord signal changes (Fig. 1a, b).

### Surgical Technique
Under general anesthesia in the supine position with slight neck extension, the target segment was confirmed by intra-operative lateral C-arm radiography using a radio-opaque marker placed on the anterior neck. A right-sided approach through a 3-cm transverse skin-crease incision was used. The platysma was incised in line with the skin incision, and subplatysmal structures were bluntly dissected with the index finger. The trachea and larynx were gently retracted contralaterally while the carotid sheath was protected laterally. The initial dilator was introduced to palpate bony anatomy and fluoroscopy confirmed position. Sequential dilators were placed, followed by an expandable tubular retractor (22 mm diameter, 60 mm blade length) locked to a table-mounted flexible arm. Once introduced, the blades were opened vertically. The longus colli were elevated with a long-handled cautery; the blades were collapsed and reopened vertically to retract the longus colli laterally on both sides. Final fluoroscopic confirmation of docking was obtained before proceeding.

Fixation of the tubular retractor to the table maintained a correct working trajectory without excessive soft-tissue tension typical of manual retraction. Under microscopic assistance and through the tube, the retractor was docked initially on the C5 body and angulated cranially and caudally to access the C4-5 and C5-6 discs. C4-5 and C5-6 discectomy followed by C5 corpectomy was performed to remove the retrovertebral disc fragment and decompress the spinal cord along its length. A mesh cage with autograft was used to bridge the corpectomy defect. A locking plate was inserted through the tube with screws in C4 and C6. An alternative strategy would be C4-5 discectomy with removal of the retrovertebral disc using long nerve hooks; if unsuccessful, combine with C5-6 discectomy and remove the retrovertebral disc piecemeal from beneath.

### Results
No surgery-related complications occurred. Operative time was 145 min; intra-operative blood loss was 80 ml; no post-operative bleeding occurred. The patient had relief of left upper limb pain and improvement in subjective heaviness. The wound healed without complications, leaving a cosmetic scar. Postoperative radiographs showed appropriate positioning of the cage and plate (Fig. 4b). No postoperative dysphagia was reported.

### Case 2
A 34-year-old male presented with 3 months of neck pain and bilateral upper limb radicular pain with paresthesia radiating to the interscapular region, shoulders, and upper extremities. Conservative management failed. Examination revealed painful neck movements and a positive Spurling test bilaterally. MRI showed severe compression at C4-5 and C5-6 (Fig. 5a-c). A two-level ACDF was planned.

Using the technique described above, the initial dilator was docked at C4-5 and a tubular retractor was attached, retracting the longus colli vertically through a 3-cm skin-crease incision. After discectomy, decompression, and disc space preparation under microscopy, a stand-alone cage was inserted and confirmed fluoroscopically. The retractor was removed, and the initial dilator was reintroduced under C-arm guidance at C5-6 through the same incision. After sequential dilation, the retractor system was placed and ACDF with a stand-alone cage was performed at this level (Fig. 6a, b).

### Results
Intra-operative blood loss was 60 ml with no post-operative bleeding; operative time was 110 min. The patient had significant clinical improvement with no postoperative dysphagia. Postoperative radiographs confirmed accurate implant placement (Fig. 7a, b).

## Discussion
ACDF and ACCF are effective strategies for cervical degenerative disorders, providing direct decompression of the spinal cord and nerve roots, immediate stability, restoration of cervical lordosis, and anterior column reconstruction [7,14]. However, complications such as dysphagia, hematoma, recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy, and esophageal perforation are documented [9,10,16,18,19]. Minimal-access surgery with reduced blood loss, limited tissue dissection, improved cosmesis, and reduced postoperative pain is increasingly used in spine surgery [15].

Tubular retractors are commonly used for posterior approaches to the lumbar, thoracic, and cervical spine. Prior work includes use of tubular retractors for excision of osteoid osteoma of the C2 vertebra via a posterior approach [17]. Reports of tubular retractors in anterior cervical surgery are limited. Yao et al. [11] described a full endoscopic single-level ACDF technique and concluded that endoscopic ACDF with a 2-cm port appears safe and potentially advantageous. In the first case here, a C5 corpectomy with cage and plate reconstruction was completed through a tubular retractor. The expandability of the retractor allowed the procedure through a narrow corridor with minimal soft-tissue injury, reflected in reduced postoperative pain, lack of dysphagia, and a smaller scar (Fig. 4). Dysphagia is the most common complaint after ACDF, with reported early postoperative incidence up to 69% [18,19]. Proposed causes include recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy, esophageal ischemia-reperfusion injury, and local soft-tissue swelling. Ratnaraj et al. [20] concluded that minimizing endotracheal cuff pressure and the duration and pressure of intra-operative retraction may decrease postoperative dysphagia.

In this technique, the tubular retractor is fixed to the operating table, maintaining a stable working trajectory and minimizing tension on surrounding soft tissues compared with manual retraction during open surgery. As can be seen in Figs. 4 and 5, the blades were opened in the cranio-caudal plane to provide the working trajectory. This contrasts with traditional sideways (medio-lateral) retraction, which can make the esophagus susceptible to impingement by the retractor blade if not placed properly. Minimizing medio-lateral retraction helps keep the trachea and esophagus from obstructing the field and may reduce postoperative dysphagia, which was absent in both patients described.

Given the steep learning curve for MIS techniques, the narrow working corridor of tubular retractors, and difficulties distracting severely degenerated intervertebral spaces, these procedures should be performed by experienced surgeons familiar with the system. Further validation requires comparison with a control patient group.

## Conclusion
Complex anterior-based minimally invasive cervical procedures can be performed successfully using tubular retractors. A minimally invasive anterior cervical approach with tubular retractors minimizes soft-tissue trauma and allows uneventful recovery in the postoperative period.
