# Percutaneous Endoscopic Cervical Discectomy

## Abstract

**Purpose**: Percutaneous endoscopic cervical discectomy (PECD) is an emerging surgical treatment for cervical disc herniation. We report two cases of total spinal anesthesia (TSA), an unusual complication of PECD.

**Methods and Results**: One patient became unconscious with unstable vital signs, and another patient experienced numbness in both arms and legs during the PECD operation.

**Conclusions**: Based on these two cases, we concluded that the operation of anchoring during PECD should be performed cautiously and standardized. Intravenous anesthesia should be considered to enhance intraoperative anesthesia to prevent intraoperative total spinal anesthesia.

**Keywords**: Total spinal anesthesia, Complication, Endoscopic, Cervical discectomy

## Introduction

In recent decades, endoscopic techniques have been widely applied in spinal surgeries. The minimally invasive approach offers less trauma and faster rehabilitation for patients. However, complications such as hematomas and sensory or motor issues have occurred during the development of these techniques. To minimize nerve and spinal cord injuries, both local and intravenous anesthesia are feasible anesthesia protocols. However, local anesthesia carries the risk of total spinal anesthesia. This case report aims to describe an accidental case of total spinal anesthesia, an unusual complication of PECD.

## Case 1

A 60-year-old male patient presented with left arm numbness for six months, unresponsive to conservative treatments. Cervical MRI revealed C3/4–C7/T1 cervical disc herniation and ischemic changes in the spinal cord at the C4–5 level. JOA score was 14, and the Eaton test for the left arm was positive. He was diagnosed with cervical spondylosis and scheduled for C4/5 and C5/6 posterior percutaneous endoscopic cervical discectomy (p-PECD).

The patient had a history of third-stage hypertension for 30 years, but no regular medication. Previous surgeries included tibial fracture fixation and abdominal laparotomy. He had a 20-year smoking history (20 cigarettes per day) and a 20-year history of drinking 100 cc of alcohol daily. The patient had no previous anesthesia complications or respiratory diseases. Chest X-ray showed no significant findings. Preoperative lab tests showed normal results.

The p-PECD procedure was performed in the prone position with cervical flexion. The initial blood pressure was 160/100 mmHg, heart rate 80, and oxygen saturation 97%. After sterilization and localization of the C6 left pedicle, local infiltration anesthesia was administered with 10 mL mixed solution of 0.5% lidocaine and 0.19% ropivacaine. 

An 8-mm incision was made to access the C6 lamina, where a paracentesis needle was used to puncture the lamina. Fluoroscopy confirmed placement of the Kirschner wire on the lateral mass of C6. The patient experienced neck pain during anchoring of the wire, leading to cessation of the procedure. A subperiosteal block was performed using 4 mL mixed anesthesia.

Despite the procedure, the patient's blood pressure and oxygen saturation decreased to SpO2 84% and BP 90–70/61–45 mmHg. The patient lost consciousness, and immediate resuscitation was initiated, including intravenous dopamine, metaraminol, doxapram hydrochloride, nalmefene, and dexamethasone. Tracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation were provided. 

After stabilizing the patient, an emergency contrast-enhanced CT scan of the neck and brain revealed no brain abnormality but showed a perforation of the C6 lamina. The patient regained consciousness after approximately 165 minutes, with gradual recovery of sensory and motor functions. Postoperative blood tests showed normal results after 3 days.

## Case 2

A 64-year-old male patient presented with right arm pain and weakness for two years. He had undergone anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion (ACCF) for C3–5 disc herniation 8 months prior, but symptoms had relapsed despite conservative treatments. Cervical myelography showed osteoproliferation at the C3/4 Luschka joint with nerve root compression. Preoperative tests were normal, and the patient had no significant medical history.

The p-PECD procedure was planned with the patient in the prone and cervical flexion position. The initial blood pressure was 130/90 mmHg, heart rate 85, and oxygen saturation 98%. Local anesthesia was administered with a similar anesthetic mix as in Case 1.

An 8-mm incision was made on the C4 pedicle. Kirschner wire placement was performed under fluoroscopy. The patient experienced neck pain when the wire was anchored. Following anesthesia injection around the wire, the patient felt numbness in both arms and legs, along with chest pressure. Despite normal motor functions, the patient's blood pressure decreased to 90/50 mmHg, and heart rate dropped to 40. Oxygen saturation remained stable at 98%.

The procedure was terminated, and the patient was repositioned and administered oxygen. Dopamine and atropine were injected to stabilize vital signs. The patient's symptoms gradually subsided, and after 30 minutes, he was returned to the ward with no further complications.

## Discussion

Percutaneous endoscopic cervical discectomy (PECD) is an emerging technique for treating cervical disc herniation. It includes posterior percutaneous endoscopic cervical foraminotomy (p-PECF) or discectomy (p-PECD), as well as anterior approaches like anterior percutaneous endoscopic cervical discectomy (a-PECD). While some complications such as hematoma and neurological deterioration have been reported, total spinal anesthesia (TSA) is a rare but serious complication. TSA typically occurs due to accidental injection of anesthetics into the wrong site, causing hypotension, motor block, loss of consciousness, and respiratory failure. 

In PECD, TSA may result from leakage of anesthetic into the intradural or subarachnoid space. In our cases, CT scans revealed lamina perforations that could have allowed anesthetic leakage, causing TSA. The use of Kirschner wires too close to the central area and aggressive manipulation may have contributed to these complications.

## Conclusion

Total spinal anesthesia during PECD is rare but warrants vigilance. To avoid TSA, the following technical considerations should be observed: Kirschner wire anchoring should be shallow and perpendicular to the lamina, and the procedure should be gentle and guided by fluoroscopy. Additionally, sufficient and well-distributed local anesthesia should be administered, and intravenous anesthesia should be considered if local anesthesia is inadequate.
