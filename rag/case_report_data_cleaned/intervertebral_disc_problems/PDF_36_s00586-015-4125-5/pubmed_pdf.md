# Postdural Disc Herniation at L5/S1 Level Mimicking an Extradural Spinal Tumor

## Abstract

Postdural disc herniation has been documented rarely, and the pathogenesis is still unknown. The average age of postdural disc herniations is between 50 and 60 years, with L3–L4 and L4–L5 being the most affected levels. Less than 10% occur at the L5-S1 level. Although magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a useful diagnostic tool, postdural disc herniation is often misdiagnosed as an extradural spine tumor preoperatively. The definitive diagnosis is made either during surgery or through postoperative pathology.

In this article, we describe a 48-year-old male patient who presented with intermittent low back pain and frequent urination for 4 years, as well as hypesthesia and pain in the left lower extremity for 1 month. A standard total laminectomy was performed, and histopathological diagnosis confirmed a degenerated intervertebral disc. The patient experienced significant relief from pain and neurological symptoms postoperatively, but frequent urination did not improve.

## Introduction

Disc sequestration is defined as a perforation of the fibrous ring and posterior longitudinal ligament, leading to migration of the fragment into the epidural space. According to Brock [1], disc sequestration accounts for 28.6% of all disc herniations. The sequestered disc is completely separated from the parent disc and disc space. Free fragments generally migrate within the spinal canal in superior, inferior, and lateral directions [2, 3]. However, posterior epidural migration is rare. Moreover, this form of lumbar disc herniation (LDH) is often misdiagnosed on MRI, being confused with various posterior epidural lesions. 

In this report, we present a case of postdural migration of lumbar disc herniation at the L5/S1 level and discuss clinical presentations, imaging features, treatments, and outcomes. MRI revealed a tumor-like mass filling almost the entire spinal canal at the L5/S1 level with rim enhancement after gadolinium administration.

## Case Report

A 48-year-old man with a 4-year history of intermittent low back pain, left lower limb pain, and frequent urination was admitted. He had been experiencing increased pain and hypesthesia in the left lower limb for the past month. Four years ago, the patient was treated conservatively with massage, traction, and medication, resulting in a reduction in his symptoms.

Physical examination revealed hypesthesia on the posterolateral aspect of the left lower limb (including the plantar surface), negative Lasegue's sign on both limbs, slight loss of the left knee tendon reflex, and absent left Achilles tendon reflex. No significant decline in muscle strength was noted. Lumbosacral radiographs showed degenerative changes without scoliosis. Computed tomography (CT) scans confirmed a herniated nucleus pulposus at the L5/S1 level. MRI revealed a tumor-like mass filling nearly the entire spinal canal at L5/S1, causing cauda equina compression. The lesion appeared hypodense on T2-weighted and T1-weighted images, with rim enhancement after gadolinium administration.

A standard total laminectomy was performed. Upon excision of the ligamentum flavum, the extruded disc fragment was clearly visible posterolaterally to the thecal sac. The fragment was compressed but not adherent to the thecal sac. The histopathological diagnosis was consistent with a degenerated intervertebral disc.

## Discussion

Although disc fragments are known to migrate in superior, inferior, or lateral directions within the anterior epidural space, posterior epidural migration of lumbar disc fragments is extremely rare [4]. Since the first case of posterior epidural migration of lumbar disc fragments was described by Lombardi in 1973, fewer than 50 cases have been reported in the English-language literature [5-11]. The average age of patients with postdural disc herniations is between 50 and 60 years, and the most commonly affected levels are L3–L4 and L4–L5, with less than 10% occurring at L5–S1 [11–13]. The occurrence of postdural disc herniations in the cervical and thoracic regions is particularly rare [14]. 

Patients with postdural disc fragments typically present with radiculopathy and, less frequently, with cauda equina syndrome. In our case, the patient experienced pain and hypesthesia in the left lower limb, as well as frequent urination, indicative of radiculopathy and cauda equina syndrome. However, postoperative evaluation did not confirm cauda equina syndrome as there was no improvement in the frequent urination. This may be due to prolonged nerve compression that prevented restoration or because the frequent urination was not associated with the posterior epidural migration of the disc fragment.

The pathogenesis of posterior epidural migration of disc fragments remains unclear. Most authors attribute it to the anatomical structure of the region, including the sagittal midline septum, lateral membrane, epidural fat tissue, epidural venous plexus, and the nerve root. These structures may act as impediments to posterior migration [3, 8, 15–17]. Kuzeyli and colleagues [18] suggested that the position of the nerve root in relation to the dura and intervertebral disc space may influence posterior epidural migration. Tatli [19] proposed that the insufficiency of ligaments and other structures may facilitate posterior migration, especially at the upper lumbar levels.

The diagnosis of posterior epidural migration of disc fragments is difficult due to its radiological similarity to other common epidural lesions, such as synovial cysts, ligamentum cysts, cystic neurinomas, tumors, hematomas, and abscesses [15, 20–23]. MRI is the gold standard for evaluating spinal pathologies, as posterior disc fragments generally appear hypointense on T1-weighted images and hyperintense on T2-weighted images [12, 18]. In many cases, the diagnosis is made intraoperatively, as the lesion may mimic a common epidural tumor, such as a neurofibroma or meningioma. However, in this case, the lesion was identified as posterior epidural migration of a disc fragment during surgery, and this diagnosis was confirmed by postoperative pathology.

To avoid severe cauda equina syndrome or radiculopathy, surgical removal of the lesion should be the first choice in patients with posteriorly migrated disc fragments [11, 12]. The decision to perform unilateral hemilaminectomy or total laminectomy depends on the size of the lesion. In this case, total laminectomy was performed due to the large size of the lesion, and the disc fragments were completely removed. A posterior transpedicular fixation system was used to maintain spinal stability, in addition to posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF). The patient experienced significant relief from pain and neurological symptoms postoperatively, but there was no improvement in frequent urination during the 1-year follow-up.
