# Treatment of Cervical Subaxial Injury in the Very Young Child

## Abstract
Infant cervical spine anatomy and biomechanics differ markedly from older pediatric groups and adults; the pediatric spine progressively resembles the adult spine after age 10. Clinical presentation of cervical spinal trauma also varies from birth through adolescence. In young children, cervical spine trauma is predominantly localized to the upper cervical region, whereas subaxial injuries and fracture-dislocations are rare.

Here, we present a 7-month-old infant with a severe subaxial flexion-distraction injury managed surgically. Neurologic examination revealed complete loss of motor function below C5. Whole-body CT demonstrated anterior C5-6 dislocation of approximately one vertebral body with a unilateral locked facet. Intubation was performed, and closed reduction under fluoroscopy with general anesthesia was attempted but unsuccessful. Anterior C5-6 microdiscectomy and fixation with a craniofacial miniplate were performed. Because posterior alignment remained imperfect after anterior stabilization, a posterior approach was added. At 12-month follow-up, the patient improved from quadriparesis to paraparesis with satisfactory radiologic outcome.

Keywords: Infant, Subaxial, Anterior, Posterior, Fusion, Pediatric trauma, Cervical spine injury, Spinal cord injury, Stabilization

## Case Presentation
A 7-month-old female infant was admitted to the emergency department after a traffic accident. One parent died in the crash, indicating high-energy trauma. Neurological examination showed spontaneous eye opening, restlessness, and no limb movements (Glasgow Coma Scale = 9; Frankel grade A). The patient was intubated due to low oxygen saturation and respiratory distress and was admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit.

## Diagnostic Imaging
Whole-body CT revealed a right occipital cephalohematoma and bilateral pulmonary contusions. Cervical CT showed anterior C5-6 dislocation of approximately one vertebral body and a unilateral locked facet on the left (Fig. 1a, b). Cervical MRI demonstrated a spinal cord contusion at C4-5, near-complete transection just below, cord edema below C5, and signal changes suggestive of subarachnoid hemorrhage. The C6 superior endplate showed avulsion of the intervertebral disc, and there were posterior ligamentous and paravertebral muscle avulsions at C5-6 (Fig. 1c).

## Review of the Literature
Cervical trauma in infants differs from older children and adults due to immature neck muscles and bones, relatively larger head size, and ligamentous laxity [1-4]. Injuries typically involve the upper cervical region and more often reflect subluxations and ligamentous injuries rather than fractures. Dislocation, subluxation, facet locking, and distraction injuries are rarely seen in the subaxial region in infants. Reported treatments include non-surgical management (external orthosis) and surgical approaches (posterior decompressive laminectomy and fusion without instrumentation) [4-6].

Illustrative reported cases:
- Holland et al. [4]: 3 weeks; posterior fusion; rib autograft + rhBMP-2; no instrumentation.
- Rooks et al. [6]: 3 months; external immobilization, posterior fusion; fusion material not stated; no instrumentation.
- Rooks et al. [6]: 3 months; posterior laminectomy + fusion; fusion material not stated; no instrumentation.
- Present case: 7 months; combined anterior-posterior surgery with fusion; allograft; anterior craniofacial miniplates.

Note: rhBMP-2 = recombinant human bone morphogenetic protein-2. Progressive kyphosis was reported after external orthosis in one patient [6].

Pediatric cervical stabilization with standard cervical plates and screws is challenging because available implants may not fit small bony anatomy. Craniofacial miniplates and screws have been used for posterior occipitocervical stabilization [7], and anterior stabilization with miniplates has been reported in a 2-year-old with C3-4 dislocation and a 15-month-old with C6-7 dislocation [8, 9]. This case supports the feasibility of anterior stabilization with miniplates in infants.

Fusion material remains debated in very young children. Autograft and allograft are widely used [10]. In this case, an iliac crest autograft harvested for anterior fusion fragmented during shaping due to incomplete ossification and cartilaginous structure; an allograft was used anteriorly, and iliac crest bony fragments were used posteriorly. Bone morphogenetic protein or demineralized bone matrix are synthetic options in this age group [4, 10], but risks include heterotopic ossification, systemic/local toxicity, and potential carcinogenesis via osteoblastic/osteoclastic activation [11].

## Epidemiology
Pediatric spinal injuries constitute 1-10% of all spinal injuries, with the cervical region most commonly affected across pediatric age groups [3, 12]. In children under 2 years, 74% of cervical injuries involve the axial region (C1-2), 22% the subaxial region (C3-7), and 4% are SCIWORA [13]. Motor vehicle accidents are the most common cause under age 2, followed by falls, pedestrian injuries, blunt head/neck trauma, obstetrical complications, and child abuse [6, 9, 12, 13]. Syndromic conditions (Down, Morquio, Grisel, rheumatoid arthritis) may predispose to significant injury from minor trauma [14]. Despite rarity, morbidity is high: 26% mortality during hospitalization, 37% persistent neurologic deficits, and 37% recovery [13].

## Diagnosis
Evaluation in very young children is difficult due to unfused synchondroses, widened atlantodental intervals, pseudosubluxation, horizontal facets, flatter vertebral bodies, ligamentous laxity, epiphyseal variants, incomplete ossification, and absent lordosis [14, 16, 17]. There is no standardized imaging algorithm for very young, non-communicative patients [15, 17]. Common modalities include X-ray (AP, lateral, open-mouth), CT, and MRI.

In pediatric trauma patients who are not alert, cannot respond, or have neurologic deficits, midline cervical tenderness, painful distracting injury, or intoxication, AP and lateral cervical spine radiographs are recommended [15]. Flexion-extension views may be added if instability is suspected; note that a single lateral radiograph may have a 21-26% false-negative rate [17].

CT is recommended to exclude occult fractures, evaluate regions poorly visualized on X-rays, and detect post-traumatic instability [15-18]. Because injuries in this age group are often subluxations and ligamentous disruptions rather than fractures, CT has a limited role in very young children’s cervical trauma [9, 17, 19]. Radiation exposure is a concern; thyroid cancer risk doubles with CT screening in ages 0-4 years [20]. Nevertheless, sensitivity and specificity are high for high-energy, critically unstable injuries [18].

Cervical MRI can reveal spinal cord injury, delineate ligamentous injury and traumatic disc herniation, and inform prognosis [17]. In patients with normal CT scans after cervical trauma, MRI detects acute traumatic findings in 48% (82% ligamentous/soft tissue injury, 12% disc protrusion, 4% hematoma, 3% cord signal changes) [21].

## Pathology
This case represents severe cervical subaxial flexion-distraction injury in a 7-month-old. Pathologic findings include complete tetraplegia (spinal cord injury), a unilaterally locked facet, anterior and posterior ligamentous injuries, hemorrhagic cord contusions, intervertebral disc disruption, C5-6 anterior dislocation, and soft-tissue edema/hematoma.

## Differential Diagnosis
Given the known traffic accident, differential diagnosis is facilitated. Considerations include child abuse, pseudosubluxation, congenital neuromuscular diseases, persistent synchondroses, vertebral artery injury, whiplash-associated disorders, acute torticollis, hanging injuries, and SCIWORA.

## Rationale for Treatment and Evidence-Based Literature
Management challenges in serious infantile cervical trauma begin with neurological assessment. Without a defined algorithm, choosing conservative versus surgical treatment is difficult [15]. Rigid collars may be insufficient and Halo vests poorly tolerated in infants [22]. Surgical challenges include the lack of appropriately sized internal fixation instrumentation and the susceptibility of thin, weak bone to iatrogenic damage during procedures (e.g., decortication, wiring).

In adults with severe subaxial flexion-distraction injuries causing marked instability and neurologic deficit, decompression, stabilization, and realignment are recommended [23]. In pediatrics, evidence is limited and largely comprises case reports in very young children [15, 22]. In the present case, surgical management was guided by adult cervical spine instability and cord compression principles:

- CSISS [24]: Anterior column injury (traumatic disc herniation) isolated; lateral column injury complex (unilateral facet dislocation); posterior column injury complex (posterior ligamentous injuries) — three-column injury indicating instability.
- SLIC [25]: Morphology (rotation/translation; facet dislocation) = 4 points; disco-ligamentous complex disrupted = 2 points; neurologic status complete cord injury = 2 points — total 8 points, supporting surgical management.
- Radiographic features of instability [22]: Unilateral/bilateral facet subluxation, ligamentous injury, and vertebral subluxation >4.5 mm (<8 years old) — consistent with instability.

Postoperatively, early mobilization with a rigid collar and initiation of physical therapy in the ICU helped prevent pulmonary complications, respiratory distress syndrome, tracheostomy requirement, and pressure ulcers.

## Procedure
Closed reduction under fluoroscopy with general anesthesia was attempted post-intubation but failed. Emergency surgery proceeded with an anterior approach: C5-6 microdiscectomy and open reduction restored alignment. No traumatic dural tear or CSF leakage was observed. An allograft (Tutoplast, Tutogen Medical, Germany) was placed into the evacuated disc space. Fixation used a craniofacial miniplate (Medplates, Ramed Medical, Izmir, Turkey; 1 mm thickness, 12 mm length) and 12-mm screws. Fluoroscopy confirmed sagittal alignment; however, posterior interspinous separation persisted (Fig. 2a). A posterior approach was added. Via a C3-7 posterior cervical incision, the avulsed posterior ligamentous complex and persistent instability were confirmed. Bilateral sublaminar polyester fiber sutures (Mersilene, Ethicon) approximated the laminae, and fusion was performed using iliac crest allograft. Fluoroscopy showed excellent alignment improvement (Fig. 2b).

## Outcome and Follow-Up
The patient remained intubated and sedated in the ICU postoperatively. A closed tube thoracostomy was placed for hemothorax on postoperative day 3 and removed on day 10 after resolution. Sedation was discontinued on day 12; extubation occurred on day 19. A rigid cervical collar was applied and physical therapy initiated. After sedation cessation, neurological status improved to Frankel grade C. The patient was discharged on hospital day 31 to a rehabilitation center. Following intensive rehabilitation, she could raise the upper limbs against gravity with marked finger movement difficulty. The lower extremities were completely flaccid and areflexic. Cervical CT at 12 months demonstrated fusion (Fig. 3).
