# Intractable Hiccups Caused by Syringobulbia and Syringomyelia Associated with Intramedullary Spinal Hemangioblastoma

## Abstract

Hiccups caused by a neoplasm in the spinal cord are rare. We report a case of intractable hiccups caused by syringobulbia and syringomyelia associated with a cervical intramedullary spinal hemangioblastoma, which was successfully treated by surgical excision. A 60-year-old man was referred from the neurology department after presenting with hiccups for 1 year. The hiccups were aggravated 3 months prior and were sustained during eating and sleeping. Several doctors administered a muscle relaxant and an anticonvulsant, but these treatments were ineffective. Spinal MRI revealed extensive syringomyelia from C2 to T2 associated with a highly enhancing intramedullary mass lesion at the C5 level. The hiccups ceased after removal of the tumor through a right hemilaminectomy. Histopathological examination confirmed hemangioblastoma. Follow-up MRI performed 5 months after surgery showed a marked decrease in the size of the syringobulbia and syringomyelia. Intractable hiccups can be caused by syringobulbia associated with an intramedullary cervical cord tumor, and possible mechanisms of hiccups are reviewed.

## Introduction

Hiccups are usually associated with disorders in the abdomen, thorax, or neck and are commonly associated with advanced internal organ malignancy [1–3]. Hiccups can also be a presenting symptom of neurodegenerative disorders involving brainstem lesions, such as neuromyelitis optica or multiple sclerosis. They are occasionally caused by syrinx-forming neoplasms, such as hemangioblastoma in the cerebellum, or by developmental anomalies of the central nervous system, including Chiari malformation. However, hiccups caused by a neoplasm in the cervical spinal cord have not previously been reported. We describe a case of intractable hiccups caused by syringobulbia and syringomyelia associated with an intramedullary hemangioblastoma in the cervical cord, successfully treated by surgical excision.

## Case Report

A 60-year-old man was referred from the neurology department with persistent hiccups that had begun approximately 1 year earlier and gradually worsened. Three months prior to presentation, the hiccups became continuous during eating and sleeping, significantly impairing daily life. Neurological examination revealed subtle gait disturbance and urinary incontinence, though these deficits were mild. Medical treatment with baclofen (5 mg three times daily), valproic acid (300 mg three times daily), and diazepam (2.5 mg three times daily) was ineffective.

The patient was sedated with lorazepam to reduce motion artifact during brain MRI, after which his consciousness did not recover for several hours. Brain MRI demonstrated extensive expansion of the central canal in the dorsal medullary region, prompting spinal MRI evaluation. Spinal MRI revealed syringobulbia connected to syringomyelia extending from C2 to T2, along with a highly enhancing intramedullary mass lesion at the C5 level.

The lesion was surgically removed via right hemilaminectomy of the fourth and fifth cervical vertebrae. Cerebrospinal fluid gushed out during midline myelotomy, and a cherry red-colored mass was identified near the center of the spinal cord. The interface between the cord and tumor was well preserved, allowing relatively easy dissection. Continuous cerebrospinal fluid outflow was observed from both poles of the mass during resection.

Postoperative recovery was uneventful, and the hiccups resolved immediately after surgery. Histopathological examination confirmed hemangioblastoma. The patient’s gait disturbance and urinary symptoms gradually improved without sequelae, although mildly decreased proprioception in the right leg was noted. Follow-up MRI 5 months after surgery demonstrated a marked reduction in the size of both the syringobulbia and syringomyelia. During a 2-year follow-up period, the hiccups did not recur.

## Discussion

Hiccups are repeated, synchronous, involuntary myoclonic contractions of the diaphragm, often accompanied by sudden closure of the glottis producing a characteristic sound. Intractable hiccups are defined as those persisting for more than 24 hours, increasing in frequency to 40–100 contractions per minute, or becoming refractory to standard treatments [4, 5].

The etiology of intractable hiccups remains unclear. Potential causes include disorders of the abdomen, thorax, neck, or central nervous system [6–14]. Hysteria, drugs, trauma, biochemical abnormalities, infections, demyelinating diseases, tumors, and vascular diseases have all been reported as causes [15–22]. Familial cases without an identifiable etiology have also been described [4].

The hiccup reflex arc consists of an afferent limb involving the vagus nerve, phrenic nerve, and sympathetic fibers from T6 to T12, and an efferent limb primarily composed of motor fibers of the phrenic nerve [23, 24]. The hiccup center is believed to reside in regions including the hypothalamus, medullary reticular formation, brainstem near the respiratory center, medial and dorsal medullary nuclei, and the cervical spinal cord between C3 and C5 segments [5, 24]. Autopsy studies suggest that the nucleus tractus solitarius plays a critical role in hiccup generation.

Several reports have linked intractable hiccups to lesions of the medulla oblongata, including hemorrhage, syringobulbia associated with Chiari malformation, and cavernous hemangioma [5, 25, 26]. Hiccups caused by cervical spinal cord lesions without medullary involvement have also been reported [27, 28]. These findings support the hypothesis that lesions affecting any part of the hiccup reflex pathway may trigger intractable hiccups.

In cases involving hemangioma or syringobulbia, chronic irritation from repetitive hemorrhage or gradual syrinx expansion may stimulate, rather than destroy, components of the hiccup reflex arc [29]. Lesions are most commonly located in the area postrema of the dorsal medulla oblongata, and individual susceptibility or hyperirritability of the hiccup center may contribute to symptom persistence.

Although hiccups associated with many disease entities can often be managed medically using agents such as baclofen, haloperidol, carbamazepine, chlorpromazine, gabapentin, or methylprednisolone, treatment is frequently challenging [4]. In the present case, intractable hiccups associated with hemangioblastoma and accompanying syringobulbia and syringomyelia were successfully resolved by surgical excision of the tumor, highlighting the importance of identifying and treating the underlying cause.

## Conclusion

Intractable hiccups can be caused by syrinx-forming spinal tumors such as hemangioblastoma in the cervical cord. In such cases, surgical excision of the tumor alone may effectively resolve symptoms, even in the absence of prominent neurological deficits. Persistent intractable hiccups should prompt consideration of brainstem or cervical spinal cord pathology.
