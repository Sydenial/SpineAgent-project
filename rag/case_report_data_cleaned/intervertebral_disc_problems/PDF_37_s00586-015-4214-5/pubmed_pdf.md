# Thoracic intervertebral disc calcification and herniation in adults: a report of two cases

## Abstract

**Purpose** Acute paraplegia due to thoracic intervertebral disc protrusion and calcification is rare. The purpose of this study was to report two cases with acute paraplegia due to a calcified thoracic disc prolapse, and to discuss the clinical diagnosis and surgical treatment with a review of the literature.

**Methods** These two cases were verified by patient history, physical examination, laboratory examination, CT and MRI studies, and pathological findings.

**Results** CT scans revealed disc calcification and protrusion at the T11–12 level in case 1 and at the T10–11 level in case 2. MRI revealed severe spinal cord compression with a hyperintense central core and a surrounding hypointense area in both cases, directly connected to the calcified intervertebral nucleus pulposus. Pathological examination revealed calcium deposition. Both patients underwent discectomy followed by interbody fusion, with satisfactory therapeutic outcomes.

**Conclusions** Decompression surgery should be carried out as early as possible in patients with early spinal myelopathy or paraplegia caused by a calcified protruded disc.

## Introduction

Intervertebral disc calcification (IDC) is a common incidental finding on radiographic examinations. Previous studies have shown that the prevalence of IDC in the general adult population accounts for approximately 5–6% of chest and abdominal radiographs [1, 2]. Calcification occurs predominantly in the cervical spine, followed by the thoracic and lumbar spine.

Thoracic disc herniation is relatively uncommon, with an incidence of approximately one case per million inhabitants per year [3], and usually occurs in the lower third of the thoracic spine. The majority of posterior thoracic disc herniations are small and clinically insignificant [4]. Acute paraplegia due to intervertebral disc protrusion and calcification is even rarer.

In this study, we describe two adult cases of acute paraplegia caused by spinal cord compression from calcified and herniated thoracic discs, and discuss their clinical diagnosis and surgical treatment in the context of the existing literature.

## Case Reports

### Case 1

A 57-year-old man presented with back pain for 40 days, bilateral lower-extremity numbness and weakness for 20 days, and bowel and bladder incontinence for 5 days. Physical examination revealed diminished sensation below the $\mathrm{T}_{11}$ level, bilateral lower-extremity paraplegia (muscle strength grade 0), diffuse hyperreflexia (patellar and Achilles tendon reflexes graded as $+++$), and positive Babinski signs bilaterally.

Laboratory examinations, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate, hemogram, serum sodium, calcium, C-reactive protein, creatinine, and blood urea nitrogen, were all within normal limits. CT confirmed nucleus pulposus calcification with posterior central herniation at the $\mathrm{T}_{11–12}$ level. MRI demonstrated severe anterior spinal cord compression at the same level, characterized by a hyperintense central core surrounded by a hypointense area.

A diagnosis of calcified thoracic intervertebral disc herniation was made. The patient underwent discectomy via a posterior transfacet approach followed by interbody fusion. Intraoperatively, the calcified disc material was semisolid and could be removed piecemeal. Pathological examination confirmed calcium deposition. Postoperatively, neurological function gradually improved, and no significant deterioration was observed during follow-up.

### Case 2

A 53-year-old man presented with back pain for 2 months, progressive bilateral lower-extremity weakness, and bowel and bladder incontinence for 15 days. He had received conservative treatment at a local hospital without improvement before admission. Physical examination revealed bilateral lower-extremity paraplegia, diffuse hyperreflexia, and positive Babinski signs.

Laboratory findings, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate, hemogram, serum electrolytes, calcium, C-reactive protein, creatinine, and blood urea nitrogen, were within normal ranges. CT revealed disc calcification and protrusion at the T10–11 level. MRI showed severe spinal cord compression with a hyperintense central region surrounded by hypointense margins.

The patient underwent discectomy through transversoarthropediculectomy via a bilateral posterolateral approach, followed by interbody fusion with instrumentation. Intraoperative findings were similar to those in case 1: the calcified disc material had a semisolid, toothpaste-like consistency and was easily removed. Pathological examination revealed degenerative fibrocartilage with calcium deposition.

At 6-month follow-up, the patient had completely recovered, with restoration of sensation, muscle strength, and normalization of reflexes.

## Discussion

Imaging findings and intraoperative assessment of disc hardness provided valuable guidance for surgical management. In both cases, CT suggested a hard calcified mass, while MRI revealed severe spinal cord compression with a hyperintense central core and surrounding hypointense area. Surgical intervention through anterior or posterolateral approaches was considered the only viable option.

Posterolateral approaches are generally contraindicated in cases of ossified central disc herniation. Therefore, differentiating between calcified but non-ossified discs and true ossification is critical. In these patients, preoperative imaging suggested calcified herniated discs with clear margins and preserved disc space height, favoring a posterior approach. Intraoperative findings confirmed that the discs were semisolid rather than bone-like, allowing safe removal.

Attention to the hardness of calcified protruded discs is essential for surgical planning. Although calcified discs may show high signal intensity on T1-weighted MRI [5–8], imaging alone cannot accurately determine disc consistency. A sharply contoured calcified mass often indicates a soft or semiliquid lesion rather than true ossification. In our cases, the semisolid, toothpaste-like consistency facilitated decompression and contributed to favorable outcomes [9, 10].

The etiology of thoracic disc calcification remains unclear. It may share similarities with calcifying tendinitis of the rotator cuff, which involves calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate deposition [11, 12]. A prolonged asymptomatic phase of calcium deposition may be followed by spontaneous liquefaction and inflammatory response, weakening the annulus fibrosus and leading to disc herniation and acute neurological symptoms. This hypothesis requires further investigation.

Given the narrow spinal canal in the thoracic region and the limited reserve space for the spinal cord, compression can rapidly lead to severe neurological deficits. Early decompression surgery is therefore strongly recommended for patients with early myelopathy or paraplegia caused by calcified thoracic disc herniation. Although the number of cases in this study is limited, the outcomes support timely surgical intervention, with longer-term follow-up needed to confirm sustained benefits.
