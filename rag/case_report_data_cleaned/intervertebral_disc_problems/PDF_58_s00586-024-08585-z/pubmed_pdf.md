# Osteolysis after Cervical Disc Arthroplasty with Artificial Cervical Disc

## Abstract

**Purpose**  
Cervical total disc arthroplasty (TDA) is a motion-preserving alternative to anterior cervical discectomy and fusion that has gained popularity among spine surgeons. Although generally effective, TDA has been associated with rare cases of progressive osteolysis, a complication whose natural history and impact on clinical outcomes are not well understood. This case report aims to present a case of progressive osteolysis following cervical TDA with the SpinalKinetics® M6-C Artificial Cervical Disc and to discuss the clinical approach and implications for patient management.

**Methods**  
We reviewed the clinical course of a patient who underwent cervical TDA with the SpinalKinetics® M6-C Artificial Cervical Disc and subsequently developed progressive osteolysis. The patient's symptoms, diagnostic findings, and treatment progression were documented, with a focus on the timing of symptom onset, imaging, and the therapeutic interventions applied.

**Results**  
The patient demonstrated a delayed onset of symptoms related to osteolysis, which was identified through CT imaging as a progressive complication. The slow progression of osteolysis in this case underscores the need for careful monitoring, as early symptoms may be subtle but can lead to significant clinical implications if unaddressed.

**Conclusion**  
This case highlights the potential for progressive osteolysis as a complication following cervical TDA, emphasizing the importance of ongoing surveillance and increased awareness among spine surgeons. Early identification and monitoring of osteolysis may mitigate the risk of severe outcomes and guide timely intervention.

**Keywords**  
M6-C Artificial Cervical Disc · Cervical total disc arthroplasty (TDA) · Osteolysis · Non-inflammatory bone loss · Anterior bone loss

## Introduction

Cervical total disc arthroplasty (TDA) was introduced as a motion-preserving alternative to anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) by addressing the problems associated with fusion. This is achieved through the maintenance of functional spinal kinematics, prevention of adjacent segment degeneration, and elimination of pseudarthrosis [1–3]. TDA has demonstrated a lower rate of revision and secondary surgery when compared to ACDF. However, it has also introduced new complications, similar to those observed in larger synovial joints [3, 4].

Short-term, non-inflammatory bone loss is commonly observed after TDA but rarely requires revision. On the other hand, osteolysis, despite being less frequently reported, tends to progress and may lead to device removal [3]. We present a case of progressive osteolysis following TDA with the SpinalKinetics® M6-C Artificial Cervical Disc.

## Case Presentation

A woman in her 40s, a smoker with no prior conditions, experienced left cervicobrachialgia and underwent double-level TDA with M6-C disc implants at C5–6 and C6–7 at another hospital. She reported an uneventful surgery but had no imaging follow-up. Five years later, she developed morning neck pain, neck weakness, minor left cervicobrachial pain, and occasional episodes of choking on her saliva. After persistent symptoms, she was evaluated by our neurology department, revealing limited neck strength (grade 4) and reduced left arm abduction due to shoulder pain, later diagnosed as calcifying tendinosis and acromioclavicular synovitis. No other deficits were observed.

Electromyography showed only chronic cervical myotome changes, leading to a differential diagnosis of dropped head syndrome. Tests for creatine kinase, anti-acetylcholine receptor antibodies, and anti-muscle-specific kinase antibodies were negative. CT imaging showed osteolysis around the implants at the endplates, while MRI ruled out myeloradicular compression. After multidisciplinary discussion, a watch-and-see approach was adopted.

Follow-up CT scans over the next four years showed progressive osteolysis, which, in association with worsening cervicalgia, prompted surgical intervention. Preoperative bone scintigraphy showed slight osteoblastic activity around the implants without hypervascularization, ruling out infection.

During revision surgery, the previous right-sided incision was used. A significant foreign body reaction with a greyish color surrounding both implants was observed, particularly at the upper level. At C5–6, the implant was completely destroyed, explaining the intense inflammatory reaction. At C6–7, an 8-mm tear was noted along the anterior upper border of the annular sheath, with extrusion of the nylon thread, which was in direct relation to the osteolytic cavity. A C6 corpectomy was performed, and both implants were removed. Given the likelihood of an inflammatory reaction to implant components, an anterior iliac crest bone graft with titanium plate fixation from C5 to C7 was performed to minimize exposure to foreign materials.

Cultures were negative, and histology confirmed a foreign body reaction. Postoperative recovery was favorable, and the patient was discharged two days later with full recovery of left arm strength. She was instructed to wear a sterno-occipito-mandibular immobilizer. A follow-up CT scan approximately two months later showed discreet subsidence of the anterior construct. To enhance stability, a second surgery was performed, involving posterior fixation to the lateral masses of C5, C6, and C7 with a crosslink. The patient was discharged two days later without complications. Subsequent CT scans confirmed successful fusion without complications.

The patient reported significant neck pain relief and resumed work and daily activities without difficulty.

## Discussion

Cervical TDA is increasingly favored by spine surgeons due to its ability to preserve motion at the treated segment, potentially reducing adjacent segment degeneration, and its low rates of surgical complications (1.5%) and revision (0–0.4%) after long-term follow-up [2, 5]. Comparative studies between ACDF and TDA have shown statistically superior outcomes for TDA in terms of overall success, Neck Disability Index success, and neurological success [5]. However, cervical disc replacement is not without complications, and data on long-term adverse events remain limited [2, 6].

Reported complications include heterotopic ossification, recurrent or persistent stenosis, postoperative kyphosis, implant migration, and iatrogenic vertebral body fractures. Osteolysis following TDA has also been described, although reports on wear and failure of cervical total disc replacements are scarce. Mechanisms of failure in larger synovial joint arthroplasty are well documented and involve wear debris triggering an inflammatory response that ultimately results in osteolysis [2, 3].

Wahbeh et al. emphasized the importance of standardized terminology to distinguish non-inflammatory bone loss from inflammatory osteolysis. Non-inflammatory bone loss represents a short-term adaptive response to mechanical changes after TDA and rarely requires revision. In contrast, osteolysis is driven by an inflammatory response, tends to progress, and may necessitate device removal. Inflammatory osteolysis typically presents as large, scooped-out lesions in the bone surrounding the implant, whereas non-inflammatory bone loss appears as erosive remodeling, usually at the peripheral or anterior vertebral body (anterior bone loss) [3, 7].

The most common symptom of osteolysis is neck pain, followed by radicular pain and paresthesia. More serious consequences, such as dysphagia, respiratory compromise, and neurological deficits, have also been reported [3, 4].

The M6-C artificial cervical disc consists of a polycarbonate urethane polymer nucleus and a fibrous annulus made of ultrahigh-molecular-weight polyethylene fibers. The core and endplates are not bonded, allowing stress distribution and physiological motion under axial load. Fixation is achieved through titanium plates with keels coated in titanium plasma spray to promote bony ingrowth [1, 2, 4, 6]. A polymer sheath was designed to limit soft tissue ingrowth and contain wear debris, but it has proven ineffective in preventing particle generation. These wear particles trigger a biological cascade, leading to phagocytosis, release of inflammatory mediators, and subsequent osteolysis. This process may ultimately cause foreign body granulation tissue invasion at the bone–implant interface, resulting in instability and neurological symptoms [1, 4].

In July 2020, the Australian Therapeutic Goods Administration issued an implant hazard alert for the M6-C device, recommending routine long-term clinical and radiographic monitoring. Changes in disc position, loss of height, and periprosthetic bone loss may signal the onset of osteolysis [4].

This case illustrates a complication that remains relatively uncommon but may become more prevalent with the increasing use of cervical TDA.

## Conclusion

Short-term, non-inflammatory bone loss is common after cervical TDA and rarely necessitates revision. In contrast, osteolysis is a less frequent but progressive complication that can lead to severe consequences requiring device removal.

The slow onset of symptoms associated with osteolysis, combined with its potentially devastating outcomes, highlights the importance of long-term follow-up and heightened awareness among spine surgeons.

Osteolysis may initially be asymptomatic, posing a therapeutic dilemma. Careful consideration is required to balance the risks of revision surgery against the potential for progression and complications.

Adoption of standardized terminology to distinguish non-inflammatory bone loss from inflammatory osteolysis is essential for accurate diagnosis and assessment. Further research is needed to evaluate osteolysis in preclinical trials of artificial disc implants and to establish standardized clinical and imaging follow-up protocols for early detection.
