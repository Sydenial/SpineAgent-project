# Resolution of Traumatic Bilateral Vertebral Artery Injury

## Abstract

Blunt cerebrovascular injury (BCVI) is rare, and the incidence of blunt traumatic vertebral artery injury (VAI) is reportedly <1% among patients with blunt trauma. However, VAI has been reported in around 15% of the patients with high risk for blunt VAI who were screened. In addition, posterior circulation stroke and a mortality rate of up to 8% have been reported in up to 24% of patients with blunt VAIs. Because VAIs cause potentially serious sequelae, screening for BCVIs should be performed in patients with certain signs or symptoms and risk factors. The Biffl scale is commonly used for the classification of VAI when patients are diagnosed with it. Although the optimal treatment regimen for VAIs remains unclear, the early administration of systemic heparin or antiplatelet therapy has been recommended to reduce the risk of stroke in patients with VAI of grades I–IV. Endovascular therapy may represent a treatment option for certain injuries, but there has been no clear indication for its use. Here we describe the case of traumatic bilateral VAI, including unilateral vertebral arterial occlusion, that resolved 3 months post-injury with antiplatelet and direct oral anticoagulant therapy.

## Introduction

Blunt cerebrovascular injury (BCVI) is rare, with an incidence of less than 1% among patients with blunt trauma. However, VAI has been reported in around 15% of patients with high risk for blunt VAI who were screened. Additionally, posterior circulation stroke and a mortality rate of up to 8% have been observed in up to 24% of patients with blunt VAIs. Since VAI can lead to serious consequences, screening for BCVI should be considered for patients exhibiting certain symptoms and risk factors. The Biffl scale is commonly used to classify VAI. Although the best treatment regimen for VAIs is still debated, early systemic heparin or antiplatelet therapy is recommended for grades I–IV VAI to reduce the stroke risk. Endovascular therapy may be appropriate for some injuries, though its use has not been clearly indicated. We present a case of traumatic bilateral VAI, including unilateral vertebral arterial occlusion, that resolved 3 months after injury with antiplatelet and direct oral anticoagulant therapy.

## Case Description

A 70-year-old man, diagnosed with ankylosing spondylitis, collapsed and hit his forehead, presenting with tetraplegia. He had restricted neck extension and flexion. He was conscious and exhibited a blood pressure of 120/60 mmHg, pulse rate of 66/min, and respiratory rate of 16/min, with an O₂ saturation of 91% on room air. His neurological examination revealed tetraplegia. He presented with dyspnea and was intubated and artificially ventilated due to hypercapnia under anesthesia. A computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a C3 fracture dislocation, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed high signal intensity and intense compression of the spinal cord from C2 to C3. He was manually reduced for the C3 fracture and fitted with a halo ring and vest to support his neck under radiographic guidance. CT and cerebral angiography revealed left vertebral artery occlusion (Biffl grade IV) and right vertebral artery stenosis (Biffl grade I). The indications for screening for BCVI were met, and VAI was highly suspected. Brain MRI did not show an abnormally high signal or diffusion-weighted imaging in the posterior circulation territory. Heparin was administered to prevent posterior circulation stroke, followed by clopidogrel for ongoing therapy. He underwent posterior fixation on day 26 post-admission. Three months post-injury, cerebral angiography revealed the resolution of bilateral VAI.

## Discussion

Blunt cerebrovascular injury (BCVI) is uncommon, but cervical spine fractures are a significant risk factor. Screening criteria such as the Denver criteria suggest that patients with cervical fractures need to be evaluated for BCVI. Magnetic resonance angiography is considered the best screening method for BCVI; however, CT angiography or cerebral angiography is necessary if arterial flow voids cannot be clearly observed on MRI. BCVI is classified using the Biffl scale, with VAI grades III and IV associated with a higher risk of stroke and less likelihood of spontaneous resolution. The total occlusion of both vertebral arteries can cause brain stem infarction, making stroke prevention vital. In the present case, CT revealed C3 fracture dislocation, and cerebral angiography showed left vertebral artery occlusion (Biffl grade IV) and right vertebral artery stenosis (Biffl grade II) on admission, followed by complete resolution of bilateral VAI three months later. 

This case represents the first documented instance of traumatic bilateral VAI resolving spontaneously. Vertebral artery occlusion may spontaneously improve, but spontaneous resolution is rare. Previous studies have shown that up to 65% of patients with grade IV VAI experience persistent occlusion, 30% improve, and 5% show resolution despite anticoagulation therapy. 

Antiplatelet therapy is commonly used for grade IV VAI to prevent posterior circulation strokes. Endovascular stenting is considered in rare cases with enlarging pseudoaneurysms or significant symptoms. Routine stenting is avoided due to increased costs and potential risks. In this case, anticoagulation therapy, in addition to antiplatelet therapy, was administered due to deep vein thrombosis (DVT). Although the vertebral arteries did not recanalize with antiplatelet therapy alone, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) contributed to the restoration of arterial flow. Collateral circulation via the circle of Willis was the main factor in preventing brain stem stroke, rather than the late recanalization of the vertebral arteries after DOAC treatment.

## Conclusions

This case report describes a rare instance of traumatic bilateral vertebral artery injury, including unilateral vertebral arterial occlusion, that resolved three months after injury. Vertebral artery injury should be suspected in patients with cervical spine fracture dislocations, especially in elderly patients and those with ankylosing spondylitis. Imaging should include assessment of vertebral artery patency.
