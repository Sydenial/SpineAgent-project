# Rotational Vertebral Artery Occlusion ("Bow Hunter Syndrome")

## Abstract

A clinical case of idiopathic rotatory C1-C2 subluxation causing dynamic vertebral artery occlusion is presented. Methods include a literature review between 1960 and 2019, a discussion of diagnostic methods, and treatment options. The description of diagnostic and treatment methods in the case is provided. 

### Results
We present a patient with dynamic left vertebral artery occlusion associated with idiopathic rotatory C1-C2 subluxation. A dynamic Angio-CT showed rotatory C1-C2 subluxation with significant flow reduction in the left vertebral artery at the exit of the C2 transverse foramen, continuing to the V3 segment when the head rotated to the right. Due to clinical and radiological worsening in the following months, posterior C1-C2 arthrodesis was performed, leading to the disappearance of the symptoms. There are 193 cases reported with dynamic vertebral artery occlusion, but only two cases attributed to primary rotational atlantoaxial instability. The most common etiology is degenerative.

### Conclusion
Rotatory vertebral artery occlusion is rare, mainly affecting adults aged 50-70 years. Vertebrobasilar insufficiency is triggered by head rotation to the contralateral side of the dominant vertebral artery. Dynamic subtraction angiography is considered the gold standard diagnostic method, but dynamic Angio-CT, Angio-MRI, and Doppler ultrasonography are less invasive options. Treatment options include conservative and surgical methods, with endovascular surgery being an option in specific cases.

## Introduction

Bow hunter's syndrome (BHS) was first described in the scientific literature in 1978 by Sorensen [1]. It is a vertebral artery (VA) occlusion caused by contralateral rotation of the head beyond 45 degrees. VA occlusion is a potentially life-threatening condition that may result in cerebellar or brainstem infarctions, leading to temporary or permanent neurological disabilities, including coma and death [2]. Symptoms include visual impairment, diplopia, dysarthria, vertigo, syncope, walking disabilities, and sensorimotor deficits [3].

Frequent causes of VA occlusion are atherosclerosis and thromboembolic phenomena [4], while traumatic and mechanical etiologies are less common. Chiropractic manipulation, surgery, sports accidents, osteophytes, fibrous bands, atlantoaxial instability, and rheumatoid arthritis have been reported as mechanical causes [5]. In some cases, no related factors are found.

The right vertebral artery originates from the brachiocephalic trunk, while the left vertebral artery originates from the subclavian artery. The entrance to the transverse foramen is mostly at C6 level (extraosseous segment), exiting at C1 level (foraminal segment), continuing to the magnum foramen (extraforaminal segment), where it attaches to the dura mater. Both vertebral arteries converge in the intradural segment to form the basilar artery. The left vertebral artery is usually dominant, though evidence is scarce [6].

Due to the low prevalence of BHS, diagnosis is often delayed, with patients consulting multiple specialists. The chance of BHS being recognized as a clinical entity is often underestimated or unknown.

## Materials and Methods

A case of a patient diagnosed and treated for dynamic VA occlusion at our institution is reported. Access to the clinical records and imaging database was granted for publication, with approval from the local clinical ethics board.

A literature review from 1960 to 2019 was conducted through Medline (PubMed), Cochrane, and Embase databases, including only English-language literature.

## Results

A 58-year-old woman with a history of arterial hypertension presented with a year-long progression of dizziness and blurred vision, occurring when she rotated her head while writing on the blackboard, specifically with right rotations. She experienced several syncope episodes. After consulting with various specialists, she was referred to our spine surgery team in February 2018.

Physical and neurological exams were normal except for a defensive posture of her neck to avoid triggering symptoms. Cervical X-rays and head and neck MRI revealed age-appropriate changes, and an upper limb electrophysiological study showed no abnormalities.

### Imaging Findings
A dynamic Angio-CT scan of the neck vessels revealed a severe reduction in the left vertebral artery diameter at the C2 foraminal segment. Symptoms appeared with right rotation and extension. Due to worsening symptoms, a posterior C1-C2 arthrodesis with the Harms technique was performed, with complete resolution of the symptoms.

## Literature Review

A review of 194 cases of dynamic VA occlusion across 85 studies revealed that the majority of cases were located below C2 (54%), with 43% at the upper cervical spine (C0-C1, C1, C2, C1-C2). The condition was slightly more prevalent in men and typically occurred in patients aged 50-70 years.

### Etiologies
The most common etiology was degenerative diseases, such as osteophytes, spondylosis, and disc herniation (54% of cases). Other causes included fibrous bands, muscle hypertrophy, and tumors. Atlantoaxial instability was rare, accounting for 6% of cases, while vascular causes (e.g., aneurysm, vertebral artery dissection) were seen in 5% of cases. In 22% of cases, the etiology was unspecified.

### Diagnostic Methods
Dynamic Angio-CT, dynamic MRI, and angiography were the primary diagnostic methods. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) is considered the most accurate, but dynamic Angio-CT and Angio-MRI are useful alternatives. Doppler ultrasonography is an emerging diagnostic tool, with excellent diagnostic performance when performed by experienced operators.

### Treatment Options
Treatment options include conservative, surgical, and endovascular procedures. Conservative treatments involve cervical orthosis and antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy. Surgical treatments include decompression, fusion, or a combination of both. Decompression alone had a success rate of 80%, while fusion procedures had higher success rates.

## Discussion

BHS is a clinical condition caused by vertebral artery occlusion due to rotation and extension of the neck, which reduces blood flow to the brainstem or cerebellum. While rare, the condition often occurs at the C1-C2 level, but cases in the subaxial cervical spine are also common.

The diagnosis of BHS is typically made using dynamic imaging techniques, including Angio-CT and Angio-MRI, which are more widely available than angiography. Doppler ultrasonography is also emerging as a useful diagnostic tool, though it remains operator-dependent.

## Conclusion

Dynamic VA occlusion, or BHS, is a rare condition resulting in vertebrobasilar insufficiency. Diagnostic methods include dynamic Angio-CT, Angio-MRI, and Doppler ultrasonography, with dynamic subtraction angiography being the gold standard. Treatment varies based on etiology, with conservative management for mild cases and surgical options for more severe cases.

### Treatment Strategies
The use of fusion, either alone or with decompression, provides the best outcomes, though endovascular therapy may be a viable alternative in specific cases. Conservative treatments can be used when surgery or endovascular therapy is not feasible.
