# Vertebral Body Fracture After TLIF: A New Complication

## Abstract

**Background**  
The transforaminal posterior approach (TLIF) procedure was first described in 1982. Current literature indicates its equivalence in outcomes for fusion constructs compared with other anterior–posterior procedures. As a procedure becomes more popular and more frequently performed, the types and number of complications that occur increase. We report a two-case series of patients who underwent TLIF. Both patients had satisfactory postoperative imaging but later presented with coronal plane vertebral body fractures in the caudal vertebral body of the TLIF construct. We believe the complication may be related to: (a) unrecognized fracture of the endplate during cage impaction; (b) overloading the endplates by maximizing lordosis using the reverse jackknife position on a Jackson table; and (c) underlying mineral bone disease. As the TLIF procedure increases in popularity, caution should be exercised to avoid similar potential complications.

**Purpose**  
To describe a potential complication associated with the TLIF procedure.

**Study Design**  
Case report.

**Patient Sample**  
Two patients.

**Outcome Measure**  
Revision surgery.

**Methods**  
Case series.

**Results**  
Caudal vertebral body fracture is a potential complication following TLIF.

**Conclusion**  
TLIF procedures can result in unstable vertebral body fractures, potentially necessitating revision decompression and stabilization. Extra caution is recommended in patients with mineral bone disease, as technical errors may be magnified.

**Keywords**  
Lumbar fusion; TLIF; vertebral fracture; revision surgery

## Introduction

Degenerative spondylolisthesis is an acquired form of spinal stenosis caused by relative subluxation of one vertebra on an adjacent vertebra. Non-operative management has broad consensus among spine surgeons and typically involves multimodal treatment, including NSAIDs, physical therapy, and epidural steroid injections. Surgical treatment options are varied, as multiple fusion techniques have demonstrated success, and advances continue to be made in minimally invasive approaches.

Harms first described anterior interbody fusion through a transforaminal posterior approach (TLIF) in 1982 [1]. This procedure allows access to the intervertebral disc space from a posterior approach. The anterior column is more amenable to fusion due to compressive forces transmitted across motion segments. TLIF offers the benefit of combined anterior and posterior column fusion (360° fusion) without the morbidity of an anterior retroperitoneal approach and with a lower reported complication rate compared with direct posterior interbody fusion [2–4]. Current literature indicates that TLIF provides equivalent restoration of disc height compared with 360° fusion and may offer cost savings due to decreased operative time, less blood loss, and shorter hospital stays [5–7].

We report a previously undescribed complication: coronal plane vertebral body fracture requiring revision posterior decompression and extension of the fusion construct in two patients following single-level TLIF for degenerative spondylolisthesis and spinal stenosis.

## Case Report 1

JO is a 63-year-old male with over two years of low back pain and predominantly right leg pain that had progressively worsened. His medical history is notable for hepatitis C, and he was on a transplant list. Social history was significant for smoking 30 cigarettes per day. Prior to presentation, he underwent a year-long trial of non-operative management and required significant narcotic pain medication. Pain was exacerbated by walking, and he reported mild weakness of the right quadriceps compared with the contralateral side.

Preoperative lumbar spine radiographs demonstrated degenerative spondylolisthesis at L4/L5. MRI revealed spinal stenosis with right-sided foraminal narrowing at L4/L5. A TLIF procedure was performed without intraoperative complication. Upright postoperative lumbar spine radiographs were satisfactory, demonstrating good lumbar lordosis and alignment. The patient was discharged on postoperative day three.

At the two-week postoperative visit, the patient returned with significant back pain, increasing right lower extremity pain, and weakness. Neurologic examination revealed L4 motor strength of 0/5, L5 of 1/5, and S1 of 3/5. CT imaging demonstrated a fractured endplate at L5. Given the progressive neurologic deficit and imaging findings, the patient underwent revision posterior decompression and extension of spinal fusion from the pelvis to L1.

The decision to fuse to the pelvis was based on extensive decompression necessitated by neurologic deterioration, poor pedicle screw purchase at L3 and S1, and loosening of replaced screws at L5. In retrospect, the authors acknowledge that the extent of fusion may have been excessive. At one month following revision surgery, the patient’s neurologic examination had returned to normal.

## Case Report 2

EA is a 47-year-old female who presented with bilateral leg pain, numbness, and tingling originating in the low back and radiating to both feet. Neurologic examination was normal, but the patient had a BMI of 38. Symptoms began three years prior and had progressively worsened, limiting her ability to walk more than one block. Pain was worse with extension and refractory to NSAIDs and physical therapy, significantly impairing activities of daily living.

Plain radiographs demonstrated a sacralized L5 vertebra with Grade 1 degenerative spondylolisthesis at L3/L4. MRI revealed spinal stenosis at the affected level. After failing three years of non-operative management, the patient elected surgical intervention. A TLIF at L3/L4 was performed without complication, and she was discharged home on postoperative day three.

Approximately two weeks postoperatively, following a fall from standing height, the patient presented to the emergency department with worsening back and leg pain exceeding preoperative levels. Radiographs revealed graft subsidence, loss of lordosis, recurrence of spondylolisthesis, and fracture through the L4 vertebral body. Initial management included clamshell orthosis. Subsequent CT imaging demonstrated further fracture displacement and absence of union. Due to lack of clinical improvement and progressive imaging findings, the patient underwent revision posterior spinal fusion. Fusion from L1 to the pelvis was selected due to the extent of decompression and the need to include two intact vertebral bodies above the fracture.

## Discussion

Complications associated with TLIF have been categorized as minor or major [4]. Minor complications include interbody graft or implant malposition not requiring reoperation, pedicle screw malposition not requiring reoperation, cerebrospinal fluid leak, transient neurologic deficit, contralateral radiculopathy, hematoma, and anemia [4,8]. Major complications include graft or implant malposition requiring reoperation, pedicle screw malposition requiring reoperation, neurologic deficits lasting longer than three months, infection, prolonged ileus, vascular injury, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism [4].

Overall complication rates following TLIF are low and comparable to anterior–posterior lumbar interbody fusion [4]. Pedicle screw misplacement occurs in approximately 5% of cases, similar to other procedures requiring pedicle fixation [3]. Transient neurologic deficits occur in 2–7% of patients, while persistent deficits lasting longer than three months occur in approximately 4% of minimally invasive TLIF cases [4]. Incidental durotomy has been reported in 4% of cases, although one series reported a rate as high as 20% [5]. Postoperative infection rates are approximately 5% [5]. Gastrointestinal complications, primarily ileus, have also been reported, including rare cases of pseudomembranous colitis [5].

Clinical outcomes following TLIF are generally favorable. Potter reported that over 80% of patients experienced greater than 50% reduction in preoperative pain [5]. Limitations of the procedure include reduced success in patients with rigid coronal plane or kyphotic deformities.

To our knowledge, vertebral body fracture following TLIF has not been previously reported. In the first case, a wedge compression fracture through the inferior endplate of L5 resulted in spinal instability and recurrence of listhesis with foraminal compression. The second case involved a coronal plane fracture of the inferior instrumented vertebra.

Several mechanisms may explain these complications. Pedicle screw misplacement can increase stress on TLIF spacers. Occult fractures at the time of TLIF insertion may go unrecognized; impaction of the spacer can weaken the endplate through microfracture, particularly when the irregular surface of the spacer impacts subchondral bone. Disc preparation may also damage bony endplates, exposing weaker subchondral bone that subsequently collapses under axial loading.

The senior author’s technique included jackknifing the Jackson table after TLIF insertion to maximize lordosis, with reverse break angles increased up to 20°. This maneuver may have produced a nutcracker effect on the TLIF spacer and adjacent endplates, particularly in weakened bone. Tensioning of the intact anterior longitudinal ligament during lordosis correction may further exacerbate this effect. In the second case, a reported fall may have precipitated the fracture.

Patient factors also played a role, as both patients were either significantly overweight or had metabolic bone disease. Formal preoperative bone density testing was not performed, and results may have altered instrumentation strategies. It is notable that the senior author had performed over 200 TLIF procedures prior to these cases without observing this complication; both occurred within two months of adopting a more aggressive jackknife technique.

Following these events, the senior author modified the technique to limit jackknifing to 10° to reduce endplate overload and avoid a nutcracker effect from an intact anterior longitudinal ligament. Additional preventive measures include meticulous disc removal to preserve endplate integrity, cautious use of lordosis-restoring maneuvers, and heightened vigilance in patients with compromised bone quality. Preoperative bone density testing is recommended for patients at risk of osteoporosis.

## Conclusion

Excessive lordosis achieved during TLIF, resulting in indirect reduction of the foramina and spinal canal, can lead to vertebral body fracture at the inferior instrumented level, necessitating revision surgery and extension of the fusion construct. Surgeons should proceed with caution when performing TLIF in patients with osteopenia, osteoporosis, or other metabolic bone disorders, as compromised bone quality increases the risk of endplate fracture and subsequent need for more complex surgical intervention.
