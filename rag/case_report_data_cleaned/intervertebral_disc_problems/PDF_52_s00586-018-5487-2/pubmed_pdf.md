# Sagittal imbalance treated with L5 pedicle subtraction osteotomy with short lumbar fusion from L4 to sacrum using four screws into L4 for enhanced fixation: two additional vertebral screws: a technical note

## Abstract

**Purpose** To report a technique using four screws in a single vertebra (two pedicle screws and two direct vertebral body screws) for enhanced fixation with a single-level caudal pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO).

**Methods** A 60-year-old woman underwent L4/5 fusion surgery for degenerative spondylolisthesis. Two years later, she was unable to stand upright for short periods because of lumbar kyphosis caused by subsidence of the fusion cage and Baastrup syndrome in the upper lumbar spine (sagittal vertical axis [SVA] 114 mm, pelvic incidence 75°, lumbar lordosis [LL] 41°). She underwent short-segment fusion from L4 to the sacrum with L5 PSO. The construct was reinforced with two vertebral body screws at L4 in addition to conventional L4 pedicle screws.

**Results** After surgery, sagittal parameters improved (SVA 36 mm; LL 54°). Two years after corrective surgery, she maintained a low SVA, though high residual pelvic tilt indicated ongoing compensation for residual sagittal malalignment.

**Conclusion** PSO for sagittal imbalance usually requires long fusion (at least two levels above and below the osteotomy) to achieve stability and better global alignment. However, longer fixation may reduce quality of life and increase the risk of proximal junctional failure. This technique may allow a shorter fixation area after osteotomy.

## Introduction

Sagittal alignment of the spine has become a key consideration when treating adult patients with spinal deformity. The cause of sagittal imbalance is usually a combination of loss of lumbar lordosis and increased thoracic kyphosis [1, 2]. Further, the pelvis is a key factor in conceptualizing ideal spinal alignment in the sagittal plane; sagittal imbalance is not only deviation of lumbar lordosis or thoracic kyphosis, but also discordance of the lumbar curve from its base, the pelvis [3].

Long fusion from the thoracic spine to the sacrum is usually selected in adult patients to achieve adequate sagittal balance of a degenerative spine [4, 5]. However, longer fusion can lead to pseudoarthrosis [4, 5], proximal junctional failure [4], and patient dissatisfaction [6].

Here, we present the case of a 60-year-old woman with sagittal imbalance after single-level fusion surgery for degenerative L4/5 spondylolisthesis. The main cause of the imbalance was iatrogenic L4/5 segmental kyphosis after surgery, but she could not compensate by pelvic retroversion and hyperextension of the upper lumbar spine. She was treated successfully with short fusion from L4 to the sacrum together with L5 pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO). The pathology of sagittal malalignment in this case and the surgical technique are discussed in detail.

## Case report

A 60-year-old woman presented with low back pain and bilateral radiculopathy of the lower limbs. A lateral radiograph revealed degenerative spondylolisthesis at L4/5. Her Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score [7] was 13/29. She underwent L4/5 transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion with pedicle screw fixation. After surgery, her radicular pain disappeared, and 6 months postoperatively her JOA score improved to 26. The postoperative segmental lordotic angle at L4/5 (angle between the proximal endplate of L4 and the distal endplate of L5) was 16°. However, the segmental lordosis at L4/5 decreased gradually because of cage subsidence into the L4 vertebra. One year after surgery, the L4/5 lordotic angle was 5°, and she developed back stiffness.

Her symptoms worsened gradually over 7 years, and eventually she had difficulty maintaining an upright standing posture even for a short time, without neurological symptoms. Imaging revealed severe whole-spine sagittal imbalance with a sagittal vertical axis (SVA) of 117 mm. Sagittal alignment parameters are shown in Table 1. There was a severe mismatch between pelvic incidence (PI) and lumbar lordosis (LL) ($\mathrm{PI-LL}=34^\circ$), and pelvic tilt (PT) was large at 36°. An extension lateral radiograph showed that the spinous processes from L1 to L4 were touching (Baastrup syndrome, or kissing spine [8, 9]), which might prevent further lumbar extension. We concluded that sagittal imbalance was caused by iatrogenic L4/5 malalignment despite compensatory pelvic retroversion and hyperlordosis of the upper lumbar spine.

Because back muscles were almost normal and magnetic resonance imaging showed no degenerative changes or canal stenosis at upper lumbar levels, we planned short lumbar fusion from L4 to the sacrum with L5 pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO) and L5/S1 transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion. To increase construct stability, two supplemental vertebral body screws were placed at L4 together with bilateral S2-alar-iliac screws, connected using a four-rod construct. After surgery, sagittal alignment improved dramatically. LL increased from 41° to 54°, mainly due to increased L4/5 segmental lordosis (31°), although PT remained large at 37°. Final follow-up SVA was 36 mm, and back stiffness and difficulty standing or walking completely disappeared. Complete bony union at the osteotomy site and at L5/S1 was achieved at latest follow-up.

## Discussion

Aging of the spine, vertebral fracture, and inappropriate fusion surgery can cause kyphosis resulting in sagittal balance disorder [1, 2, 10, 11]. The early compensatory mechanism in sagittal deformity is usually hyperextension of adjacent vertebrae and hip hyperextension [12]. In severe deformity, patients require knee flexion and ankle extension to maintain standing posture [12]. If sagittal deformity is too large to compensate, surgical intervention may be required to restore appropriate spinal curvature.

In this patient, the main cause of sagittal malalignment was L4/5 segmental kyphosis due to cage subsidence, with a rapid decrease in L4/5 segmental lordosis from 16° to 5° (Table 1). Although the pelvis showed retroversion (PT 36°) and the upper lumbar spine was hyperextended, she could not compensate for global sagittal alignment.

Surgery for sagittal malalignment usually requires long fusion to the lower lumbar vertebrae or sacrum. In older patients, the sacrum is often selected as the lower instrumented vertebra, and good clinical results have been reported using iliac screw reinforcement [13, 14]. The ideal proximal level remains controversial. Longer fusion can improve global alignment theoretically, but it may decrease quality of life [6] and raises concern for proximal junctional failure, especially with longer fusion up to L3 [15]. Lamartina et al. classified sagittal imbalance into six patterns; this case corresponds to the “lower lumbar kyphosis pattern,” for which they recommend short fusion from L4 to sacrum [16]. Here, disc degeneration was limited up to L3/4, so we chose L4 as the proximal end to minimize fusion length.

Because PI was large (75°) and PI–LL mismatch was large (34°), we planned an L5 PSO. Standard PSO reconstruction principles include fixation two levels above and below the osteotomy to achieve adequate stability [17, 18], so we considered standard L4 pedicle screw fixation alone potentially insufficient. Therefore, we added two reinforcing vertebral screws inserted from just below the bilateral L4 nerve roots and used a four-rod construct. At follow-up, bony union was achieved at both the osteotomy site and L5/S1 without complications. Postoperative LL became 54°, and SVA improved dramatically. However, PT after the second surgery was almost unchanged. According to the global alignment and proportion (GAP) score [19], ideal LL and PT would be 75° and 22°, respectively, suggesting insufficient correction at the surgical site. Although adjacent L3/4 showed little degenerative change at follow-up, careful surveillance is needed because GAP score was 6 (moderately disproportioned), indicating moderate risk of postoperative mechanical complications [19].

Although this short fusion method showed good clinical results in this case, surgical indications remain unclear and require further clarification. The mechanical strength of the four-screw construct (two conventional pedicle screws and two vertebral screws) should be examined in cadaveric studies. Younger patients with less disc degeneration and without osteoporosis may be good candidates.

## Conclusion

A patient with sagittal deformity resulting from L4/5 iatrogenic kyphosis and kissing lumbar spine was treated with L5 PSO and short fusion from L4 to the sacrum using four screws at L4 and a four-rod construct. This strategy may be an option for treating sagittal malalignment, although indications for the surgery require further clarification.
