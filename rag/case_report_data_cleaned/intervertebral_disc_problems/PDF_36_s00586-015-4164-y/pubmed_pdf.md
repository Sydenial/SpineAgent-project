# Chest wall reconstruction after en bloc Pancoast tumour resection with the use of MatrixRib and SILC fixation systems: technical note

## Abstract
Study design: Technical note.

Objective: In cases where partial resection of the rib cage is performed with vertebrectomy, chest wall reconstruction may be challenging due to the lack of an anchor point that would normally be the proximal end of a rib or a transverse process. We report a straightforward technique for chest wall reconstruction using two fixation systems commonly applied in spinal practice.

Methods: Surgery for a squamous cell carcinoma (Pancoast tumour) of the right lung infiltrating T2, T3, and T4 was performed through a T4 lateral thoracotomy. Posterior instrumentation with transpedicular screws T1-3-5 on the left and T1-5 on the right was followed by right upper lobectomy and hemivertebrectomy. The laminae and facet joints of T2-T4 were removed on the tumour side, and osteotomy was performed medial to the pedicle at the lateral aspect of the dural sac. Proximal parts of four adjacent ribs were removed to allow radical en bloc resection with tumour-free margins. The distal end of each of four rib plates (MatrixRib Precontoured Plate system) was attached to the proximal end of the corresponding rib. The proximal end of each plate was then attached to the rod of the posterior fixation construct using a flexible polyethylene terephthalate (PET) band of the SILC fixation system. The other end of the PET band was passed through a top-loading clamp subsequently attached to the posterior rod.

Results: No additional procedures for chest wall reconstruction were required. At 7 months, chest CT showed satisfactory expansion of the remaining lung tissue with proper spinal alignment and anatomical rib cage shape.

Conclusions: This technique can be applied for chest wall reconstruction in cases of total or subtotal vertebrectomy with resection extending to the rib cage. It appears straightforward, safe, and effective, allowing good cosmetic and functional outcomes.

## Introduction
According to many authors, prognosis in patients with Pancoast tumour depends mainly on tumour T stage, response to preoperative chemoradiotherapy, and completeness of resection [1-6]. Surgical treatment is associated with 5% mortality and 7-38% mortality rates [4]. The overall 5-year survival for R0 resections appears to be as high as 60% (54-77%) [2, 4]. The main pattern of recurrence is distant metastasis, especially in the brain. Invasion of the vertebral column is not a contraindication for surgery, which should be performed en bloc with pulmonary parenchyma resection [5, 7-12].

Technical aspects of spinal reconstruction after en bloc resection are well described [3, 5, 10, 13-15]. Surprisingly, there are few detailed reports on rib cage repair after radical excision [12, 16]. None describe in detail chest wall reconstruction after extensive Pancoast tumour resection including vertebrectomy and proximal rib excision. We present our technique, which we find novel, relatively easy to apply, and effective.

## Methods

### Case presentation
A 66-year-old fit ex-smoker with asbestos exposure, chronic cough producing clear sputum with occasional blood, breathlessness, and 2-3 months of dull back pain presented. Chest X-ray and CT showed a mass in the right upper lobe infiltrating the vertebral bodies of T2, T3, and T4 (Fig. 1a). Biopsy confirmed squamous cell carcinoma meeting topographical criteria of a Pancoast tumour [17].

### Surgical technique
The first stage was posterior instrumentation T1-T3-T5 on the left. On the right, transpedicular screws were placed into T1 and T5 without a rod.

A right posterior lateral thoracotomy was performed with the patient prone. The table was tilted about 45 degrees, and the pleural cavity was entered through the 4th intercostal space. The tumour involved the anterior surfaces of T2, T3, and T4. The upper lobe was dissected and the spinal team proceeded.

Hemivertebrectomy T2-4 was performed as described by Jain et al. [15]. The laminae and facet joints were removed on the tumour side. Osteotomy was performed medial to the pedicle at the lateral aspect of the dural sac. Proximal parts of three adjacent ribs were removed, allowing radical en bloc resection with free margins (Fig. 2). The anterior column was supported with an autologous rib graft (5th rib) fixed with screws to the remaining vertebral bodies (Fig. 3a). Posterior fixation was completed with insertion of a titanium rod on the right.

Chest wall reconstruction used rib plates (MatrixRib Precontoured Plate system; Synthes, USA) and the SILC fixation system (Globus Medical, Inc., USA). A plate of appropriate length was contoured to ensure at least two screws on the proximal end of the rib. Through a drill guide, two holes were created, and locking screws secured the plate to the resected rib end. The proximal end of the plate was attached to the posterior rod using a flexible PET band of the SILC system, passed through the most proximal hole of the Matrix plate. The other end of the band was passed through a top-loading clamp and secured to the posterior rod (Figs. 3b, 4).

The wound was closed in layers; two chest drains and one wound gravity drain were placed. We did not use a Gore-Tex mesh as suggested by Ng et al. [18], as the defect was well covered by the scapula and trapezius muscle [19].

## Results
Histopathology confirmed squamous cell carcinoma. All resection margins were negative. The patient required 3 weeks in intensive care and was discharged after 8 weeks of inpatient recovery. At 8-month follow-up he was fully mobile and independent, without clinical or radiological signs of recurrence. He had right arm and scapular pain controlled with 200 mg gabapentin per day.

On follow-up chest X-ray 7 months postoperatively, there was satisfactory expansion of remaining lung tissue, proper anatomical rib cage shape, and good spinal alignment (Fig. 1b).

## Discussion
Since Parham’s late 19th-century report of chest wall resection and reconstruction, several advances have been made [20]. Despite this, repair of full-thickness chest wall defects remains complex and controversial [19]. When a flail chest wall segment exceeds 5 cm, ventilation becomes progressively inefficient. In patients with two or more rib segmental loss, surgical stabilization decreases ventilator days, improves long-term outcomes, and lowers hospitalization costs [21, 22].

Alloplastic materials represent a major advancement in chest wall reconstruction [20]. The MatrixRib Precontoured Plate system, using plates and screws to bridge defects and support the chest wall after resection, is gaining popularity [18, 23, 24]. However, after hemivertebrectomy, the lack of a proximal fixation point made it insufficient alone. A simple wire was considered suboptimal due to potential fatigue failure. We therefore applied the SILC fixation system, designed primarily for multidimensional spinal deformities. Its band is typically applied around the lamina or both lamina and transverse process, with a top-loading clamp attached to the rod, enabling standard reduction maneuvers without pedicle purchase. In our case, this allowed a smooth, firm connection between the rib plates and the posterior rod. In our experience, the technique is relatively straightforward, reproducible, and cost-effective, and may have a role in major resection and reconstruction for locally advanced tumours of the lung and chest wall.
