# Isolated Multiple Lumbar Transverse Process Fractures With Spinal Instability: An Uncommon Yet Serious Association

## Abstract
We describe a case of multiple lumbar transverse process fractures associated with unexpected yet clinically significant spinal instability. A 38-year-old man sustained high-energy trauma after being struck by a reversing truck. Neurological examination was normal. Computed tomography (CT) demonstrated multiple right-sided transverse process fractures from L1 to L5 and a single left-sided transverse process fracture at L2, with subtle facet joint distraction and no other spinal lesions or visceral injuries. The injury was initially deemed stable, warranting symptomatic treatment and in-patient observation. However, standing radiographs obtained in a thoracolumbosacral orthosis (TLSO) before discharge revealed marked subluxation at L2-L3 and L3-L4. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed significant discoligamentous injuries involving the anterior longitudinal ligament, annulus fibrosus, posterior longitudinal ligament, and posterior ligamentous complex. The patient underwent posterior spinal instrumentation and fusion from L1 to L5. This appears to be the first description of multisegmental lumbar transverse process fractures associated with highly unstable injuries of the spine’s major soft-tissue stabilizers that showed only subtle changes on CT. In high-energy trauma with seemingly benign spinal injuries, careful scrutiny for instability is required. In this case, the standing in-brace radiograph prevented misdiagnosis and a potentially unfavorable outcome.

## Introduction
Isolated transverse process fractures (ITPF) of the thoracolumbar spine without other vertebral lesions are commonly found on CT scans during emergency evaluation of spine trauma. These fractures are generally considered stable and are managed with supportive care, including pain control with or without bracing, and gradual return to unrestricted weight bearing as tolerated. Further diagnostic workup or surgery is usually not recommended because the major soft-tissue stabilizers of the spine—anterior longitudinal ligament, posterior longitudinal ligament, intervertebral disc, and posterior ligamentous complex (PLC; ligamentum flavum, facet joint capsule, interspinous ligament, supraspinous ligament)—are not anatomically linked to the transverse processes. In a systematic review, the most common mechanism in younger patients was blunt high-energy trauma (motor vehicle accidents, sports collisions) [1]. Associated injuries to thoracic and abdominal viscera were frequent, indicating substantial energy transfer and the need for thorough assessment. However, none of 398 patients (including 82 pediatric cases) across four studies had neurological deficits at injury or follow-up [1], and one series reported full ambulation in all 306 patients with ITPF with only 1.1% reporting persistent back pain [2].

The anterior longitudinal ligament and PLC serve as anterior and posterior tension bands and are pivotal for spinal stability. Traumatic injuries to these structures are major drivers of instability per the Thoracolumbar Injury Classification and Severity Score (TLICS) and the AO Spine Thoracolumbar Spine Injury Classification System [3, 4]. Because of modest interobserver reliability of MRI for PLC injuries, the current AO classification emphasizes CT findings; MRI may still help detect soft-tissue injuries to differentiate unstable from stable patterns [4]. Consensus suggests MRI for patients with neurological deficits or with CT signs of potentially unstable injury [5]. Isolated transverse process fractures are not considered mechanically unstable or neurologically threatening per AO Spine classification and typically do not warrant MRI [4]. Here, we present a previously undescribed case of isolated transverse process fractures unexpectedly associated with mechanically unstable discoligamentous injury showing only subtle CT changes.

## Case Report
A 38-year-old obese Hispanic man presented to the emergency department with right flank and lower back pain after a pedestrian accident. While crossing the street, he was pushed from behind and knocked to the ground by a reversing truck. He was briefly trapped between the truck and the ground in a prone knee-chest position, bearing more load on his right side, until the truck moved forward and released him. No tires ran over the patient. Past medical history included gastric bypass and abdominoplasty. He was alert and hemodynamically stable with bruising over the right flank and iliac spine and lumbar tenderness. Neurological examination was normal except for 3/5 right hip flexion strength secondary to pain. The remainder of the examination was unremarkable, and no other injuries were noted.

CT of the thoracolumbar spine showed a slight lower lumbar curve with mild coronal pelvic tilt on the scout view (Fig. 1, left). Coronal and axial images demonstrated displaced transverse process fractures of all lumbar vertebrae on the right and at L2 on the left (Fig. 1, middle). A subtle coronal tilt of the L2/L3 disc was visible on one coronal slice (Fig. 1, right). Additional findings included a right psoas hematoma, fluid near the right pericolic gutter, and a nondisplaced right posterior tenth rib fracture. On sagittal views (Fig. 2), there was no malalignment, vertebral body wedging, regional kyphosis, axial rotation, horizontal translation, or interspinous diastasis. Mild degenerative changes at L1/L2 with an anterior osteophyte and endplate sclerosis were present, and the L2-L3 disc appeared unusually tall relative to other lumbar discs. Parasagittal images (Fig. 2) suggested minimal facet diastasis at L3/L4. Axial images showed normal alignment of lumbar facet joints, particularly at L2/L3 and L3/L4 (Fig. 3). There was minimal asymmetric widening of the right sacroiliac joint without pelvic fracture. These findings were considered stable, and the patient was admitted for monitoring and pain control. Right hip and knee strength improved as pain was managed.

The patient was treated with a TLSO brace for sitting and ambulation. On post-injury day 2, he reported satisfactory pain control at rest but had significant pain when attempts were made to transfer him out of bed. Per institutional protocol, standing radiographs with the brace were obtained before discharge in patients with high-energy spine trauma who require bracing. While standing in the brace for radiographs that afternoon, he experienced significant back pain radiating to both legs, worse on the right. Standing anteroposterior radiographs showed new deformity at L2/L3 and L3/L4 with lateral and angular subluxation: L2 was displaced approximately 2 cm to the left of L3, and L3 was coronally tilted on L4 with an asymmetrically widened L3/L4 disc on the right (Fig. 4, left). The lateral view showed L2/L3 disc space narrowing, mild retrolisthesis with superimposition of L2 on L3, and disproportionate widening of L3/L4; vertebral body heights were maintained, and regional kyphosis measured about 5 degrees at L2/L3 (Fig. 4, right). Bed rest was initiated immediately. Repeat neurological examination remained normal.

MRI revealed significant injury to the anterior longitudinal ligament, annulus fibrosus, posterior longitudinal ligament, and posterior ligamentous complex, including supraspinous and interspinous ligaments and ligamentum flavum at L2/L3 and L3/L4. There were signal changes compatible with blood products at the L2/L3 intervertebral disc and increased fluid within bilateral facet joints at L2/L3 and L3/L4. No additional bony injuries were detected beyond the transverse process fractures seen on CT (Fig. 5).

Within 24 hours, the patient underwent posterior segmental instrumented fusion from L1 to L5. The postoperative course was complicated by persistent wound drainage and deep surgical site infection requiring irrigation and debridement and antibiotics. At 6 months, he ambulated comfortably and reported only occasional discomfort with daily activities.

## Discussion
The principal injury was bilateral lumbar transverse process fractures. Such fractures are common, occur most often in the lumbar region, and frequently involve multiple levels. Although considered minor compared with vertebral body, pedicle, or laminar fractures, they typically result from high-energy trauma and have been associated with other significant lumbar spine injuries and major intra-abdominal visceral injuries in up to 48% of cases [6–9]. Per the AO Spine Injury Classification, transverse process fractures are A0 injuries with minimal clinical significance regarding biomechanical integrity or neurological risk [10]. Isolated lumbar transverse process fractures (without other spinal injuries) generally recover quickly without adverse short- or long-term functional outcomes [1, 2, 6, 9]. Some authors discourage urgent spine consultation for isolated thoracolumbar transverse process fractures in the absence of other vertebral injuries to save resources and allow earlier mobilization [2, 9, 11]. Transverse processes are muscle and ligament attachment sites that are not linked to the spine’s major soft-tissue stabilizers; their fractures rarely cause neurological deficits given their distance from the canal and the low risk that displaced fragments endanger the lumbar plexus. Standard practice for isolated thoracolumbar transverse process fractures is supportive, nonsurgical care without further diagnostic assessment, with bracing used for comfort as needed [11].

MRI is the most accurate modality for assessing ligamentous injury in spine trauma, with reported accuracy of 88–97% [5, 12, 13], but its time and cost limit urgent use to patients with neurological deficits or injury patterns suggestive of mechanical instability (e.g., flexion-distraction, hyperextension) [5]. CT, the first-line modality, has only modest reliability for detecting PLC injuries in the thoracolumbar spine. Increased interspinous distance may help distinguish compression-type injuries from anterior and/or posterior tension band injuries, but intra- and interobserver reliability for signs of PLC injury range from 0.62–0.86 and 0.26–0.71, respectively [14]. A systematic review reported CT sensitivity and specificity for PLC injury detection of 28.6–53.6% and 60.4–100%, respectively [15].

In this case, CT showed subtle changes that could have raised suspicion for discoligamentous instability. MRI was not initially indicated because there was no neurological deficit and CT findings were not deemed sufficiently suggestive of instability. Careful CT review, however, showed slight coronal tilt of L2 on L3 on coronal reconstruction, subtle facet joint diastasis at L3/L4 on sagittal reconstructions, and increased L2/L3 disc height (a possible “disc diastasis”) (Figs. 1 and 2).

Posterior distraction forces begin at the facet capsules and then propagate through the interspinous ligament, supraspinous ligament, and ligamentum flavum. Facet joint diastasis alone may therefore be insufficient to define posterior tension band incompetence [16, 17]. Moreover, facet diastasis related to instability may reduce in the non-weight-bearing supine position required for CT, making detection challenging. Asymmetric facet subluxation on axial CT has been suggested as a hint [13], although this was not visible in our axial images (Fig. 3).

Several aspects of this case were concerning for significant instability and prompted closer assessment: high-energy mechanism and multiple bilateral lumbar transverse process fractures (all right-sided and left L2) in a young, otherwise healthy, obese patient. To our knowledge, this injury pattern has not been previously specified in the literature as being associated with spinal instability. The degree of instability on the upright radiograph would likely surprise most spine surgeons even if MRI findings were known beforehand. The critical learning point is how to avoid missing such instability. We recommend that patients with high-energy thoracolumbar trauma and seemingly benign injuries be screened with standing radiographs; as demonstrated in cervical spine trauma, even a normal CT cannot exclude instability, and trauma protocols should include standing radiographs to thoroughly assess stability [18].

It is not feasible to redefine MRI indications from a single case. However, our case shows that subtle CT findings of potential instability in the context of high-energy trauma may indicate serious discoligamentous injury, which MRI can promptly confirm. Further studies are needed to define the prevalence of subtle CT changes (e.g., facet diastasis) after high-energy trauma and their correlation with MRI indicators of discoligamentous injury and mechanical instability.
