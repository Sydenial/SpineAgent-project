# An inflammatory pseudotumor in the thoracic epidural space presenting with progressive paraplegia: a histopathological diagnosis with clinical and radiological uncertainty. Case report with literature review  

## Abstract

### Introduction
Inflammatory pseudotumors (IPTs) are benign lesions with an unknown etiology, likely an immunological reaction to trauma or infection, or sometimes considered an IgG4-related autoimmune disorder. These lesions can occur as isolated or multi-centric masses, and are reported in nearly all parts of the human body. Although lung and orbital IPTs are common, involvement of the central nervous system is rare. Only seven cases of spinal epidural IPTs have been reported to date. These are clinically and radiologically a diagnosis of exclusion, with histopathology being definitive.

### Case report
We present a 49-year-old female with a two-month history of progressive weakness in the lower limbs, with no history suggestive of traumatic, infective, inflammatory, or neoplastic pathology. Clinical and radiological investigations were inconclusive. An epidural mass was found at the T1-T3 vertebral levels, compressing the thoracic spinal cord. Due to the progressive nature of her neurological deficit, an emergency decompressive laminectomy from T1-T3 was performed, excising the compressive mass. Histopathological examination revealed lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates with storiform spindle cells and dense fibrosis, diagnostic of IPT. Post-operatively, she showed rapid neurological recovery and was ambulatory within two weeks.

## Case description

A 49-year-old female presented with progressive weakness in the lower limbs over the past two months. She had a history of diabetes for 14 years but no other co-morbidities. The weakness started suddenly and was not associated with trauma or constitutional symptoms. The patient also reported numbness in the abdominal wall and lower limbs. Symptoms of gait instability and clumsiness followed, with a rapid deterioration in the last two days.

On examination, right lower limb power was MRC grade 0/5, and left lower limb showed varied strength. Abdominal reflexes were absent on both sides. Sensory testing revealed impaired sensation in a Brown-Sequard syndrome pattern. Deep tendon reflexes were exaggerated with a bilateral plantar extensor response. The patient also had facial puffiness, anterior neck fullness, and confirmed hypothyroidism.

## Diagnostic imaging

Radiological evaluation included an X-ray of the spine, which was normal. MRI showed a hypointense lesion on both T1- and T2-weighted images, encircling and compressing the dura and thoracic cord at the T1-T3 levels. The lesion enhanced homogeneously post-contrast. The differential diagnosis considered included epidural metastasis, lymphoma, myeloma, spontaneous epidural abscess, or hematoma. However, none of the imaging findings or blood tests were conclusive.

## Procedure done and histopathological findings

Surgical management was initiated due to the progressive neurological deficit. Decompressive laminectomies of T1, T2, and T3 were performed, preserving the posterior facet joints. Two masses were excised from the posterior and postero-lateral epidural space. The mass was non-infiltrative and avascular, suggesting a benign lesion. Histopathological examination revealed an un-encapsulated fibro-inflammatory lesion, with lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates and storiform spindle cells, diagnostic of IPT.

Post-operatively, the patient showed significant neurological recovery. A course of oral steroids was administered for two months, followed by gradual tapering. The patient's recovery was monitored, and she remained asymptomatic at the one-year follow-up.

## Historical review of the condition, epidemiology, diagnosis, pathology, differential diagnosis

IPT is a rare, benign condition that mimics a malignant lesion clinically and radiologically but is typically non-fatal. First observed by Brunn in 1939, IPT was named by Umirker in 1954. It is most commonly found in the lungs and orbit, but can occur in any organ system. The etiology remains unknown, with IPT being considered an immunologic or fibrogenic disorder, or a specific response to infectious agents or neoplasms. Some IPTs are associated with IgG4-related sclerosing disease, commonly seen in autoimmune pancreatitis.

MRI does not have distinguishing features for IPT, making it a diagnosis of exclusion. Typically, IPTs are hypointense on both T1- and T2-weighted MRI images and show strong enhancement with gadolinium contrast. Differential diagnoses include lymphoma, metastatic tumors, multiple myeloma, meningioma, epidural abscess, and hematoma. A biopsy and histopathological examination are essential for diagnosis.

## Rationale for treatment and evidence-based literature

Surgical excision is the most effective method for obtaining a definitive diagnosis, as radiological findings cannot distinguish IPTs from other epidural space-occupying lesions. Steroid therapy and other treatments have been tested in extra-spinal IPTs, with variable results. In cases of progressive neurological deficit, surgical excision is mandatory for both diagnosis and spinal cord decompression. Cyclophosphamide has been reported to induce remission in steroid-resistant cases of epidural IPTs. Although IPTs are benign, recurrence in extra-spinal locations occurs in 20-25% of cases.

## Outcome, follow-up

The patient's neurological recovery after surgery was excellent. She began physiotherapy post-operatively and was able to ambulate with support by two weeks and independently by two months. At her final follow-up after one year, she was asymptomatic, although radiological imaging was not performed to assess for recurrence.
