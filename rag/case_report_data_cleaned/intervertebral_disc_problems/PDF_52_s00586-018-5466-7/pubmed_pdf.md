# Posterolateral Cervical Transpedicular Corpectomy for the Surgical Management of Metastatic Tumor

## Abstract
Management of metastatic spinal disease in the upper cervical spine can be particularly challenging. Depending on the lesion level and patient anatomy, multiple anterior approaches have been described for resection, followed by posterior fixation and instrumentation. Although a single-stage posterolateral approach is well established for thoracic pathology, less is known about its applicability in the cervical spine. We present a case using a posterolateral transpedicular approach for corpectomy and graft placement to achieve circumferential decompression and reconstruction in the cervical spine, illustrating the feasibility of this technique, particularly when anatomy or pathology impedes an anterior or combined circumferential approach.

Keywords: Malignant spine tumor; Metastatic tumor; Transpedicular corpectomy; Cervical spine

## Introduction
Metastatic spread of cancer to the spinal column is a frequent manifestation of systemic disease and occurs in up to 70% of cancer patients [1]. Up to 10% of patients become symptomatic [2]. In the cervical spine, metastatic lesions commonly cause pain, loss of mobility, and neurologic deficit due to spinal cord compression [3]. Treatment is multimodal and includes chemotherapy, radiation, and surgery.

Surgical approach is dictated by factors such as tumor location and biology, bone quality, surrounding anatomy, medical condition, and prior treatments [3,5,6,12]. Decompression and resection of cervical metastases are typically performed via an anterior approach because many lesions arise from the ventral vertebral body [3–6]. However, anterior approaches may be difficult in the setting of previous radiation (complicating tracheal and esophageal retraction), poor medical condition precluding circumferential surgery, failed posterior instrumentation necessitating revision, kyphosis and instability requiring long-segment fusion, or high (C1–2) and low (cervicothoracic) lesions that make the anterior corridor challenging [5,7,8].

Anterior exposures to C1–2 include transoral, transcervical retropharyngeal, and transmandibular approaches. These are deep and narrow, and carry risks of dysphagia, aspiration, and infection with oral flora—particularly significant in patients undergoing chemoradiation [3,5,7,13]. Exposure of C2–3 is also challenging due to its position behind the mandible. While the Smith–Robinson approach has been used with satisfactory results [14–18], it may be problematic in patients with short muscular necks or large mandibles with chins extending over the C2–3 disc [14,15]. A submandibular approach is another option but requires detailed knowledge of regional neurovascular anatomy [19].

Single-stage posterolateral approaches are well described for thoracic metastatic disease to avoid transthoracic morbidity [9–11]. Posterior transpedicular corpectomy in the cervical spine has been reported by Ames et al. and Eleraky et al. for malignant tumors involving C2–C7 [3,11]. Although follow-up is limited, reported patients did well in the immediate perioperative period. This approach avoids morbidities of complex anterior exposures and provides circumferential decompression with immediate fixation and stability in a single-stage operation.

## Case Report

### History and Examination
A 73-year-old woman was brought to the LAC+USC Medical Center for waxing and waning confusion. She had a history of triple-negative breast cancer treated with mastectomy, chemotherapy, and radiation 20 years earlier. Although a urinary tract infection was deemed the cause of her altered mental status, computed tomography revealed widely metastatic breast carcinoma to the lungs, chest wall, and axial and appendicular skeleton. Neurologic examination showed full strength with a Frankel grade of E, but with bilateral Hoffmann sign, sustained clonus, and hyperreflexia.

### Diagnostic Imaging
Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a single epidural compressive lesion centered at C3 (Fig. 1). Computed tomography angiography showed a patent and dominant left vertebral artery, with apparent right vertebral artery narrowing due to tumor compression.

### Rationale for Treatment
Because the patient had a short neck and large mandible obscuring the C2–3 disc space even in extension, an anterior approach was unfavorable. Pathology at C2 or C3 is amenable to posterior resection because the C2 and C3 nerve roots can be sacrificed without motor deficit, and the more lateral vertebral artery allows a working channel for corpectomy and graft placement [3]. The spinal cord also occupies a lower proportion of the dural sac at these levels, reducing risk compared with subaxial cervical or thoracic exposures. We therefore proceeded with a single-stage, posterior-only posterolateral transpedicular C3 corpectomy with graft placement and C1–C6 posterior spinal fusion.

### Surgical Technique
After standard posterior cervical exposure, instrumentation was placed from C1 to C6. Bilateral C2 nerve roots were sacrificed to improve visualization of the C1–2 joint for C1 screws. A wide laminectomy was performed from C2 to C4, and a right-sided C2–3 facetectomy was completed. The right C3 nerve root was sacrificed, and the C3 lateral mass was removed with a high-speed drill just medial to the transverse foramen.

Tumor-invaded vertebral body was removed using a pituitary rongeur. The posterior longitudinal ligament was mobilized away from the ventral dura with a Woodson elevator to complete circumferential decompression. Anterior column reconstruction was performed with a structural fibular strut allograft cut to size and seated under fluoroscopic guidance. Closure was performed in standard multilayer fashion with irrigation and bone grafting.

Somatosensory and motor evoked potentials were monitored and remained stable throughout compared with a supine preflip baseline. Total operative time was 5 h 26 min. Approximately 1.5 h were required for circumferential decompression and corpectomy, and 1 h for sizing and seating the fibular strut graft. Estimated blood loss was 450 cc. There were no intraoperative complications, and no neurologic or vascular injuries.

## Discussion
Anterior column reconstruction was accomplished with a tricortical fibular strut allograft. This was chosen over a Harms cage or Steinmann pins with methyl methacrylate due to corridor limitations and equipment availability. Although titanium mesh and Steinmann pins with methyl methacrylate are commonly used in metastatic disease, structural allograft remains a popular and effective option based on a recent systematic review and expert consensus [2].

## Outcome
Postoperative CT and MRI demonstrated appropriate graft and hardware positioning with circumferential decompression at C3 (Fig. 4). The patient remained Frankel grade E, tolerated an oral diet, and ambulated well on postoperative day 1. She was discharged home on postoperative day 5 without complications.

At follow-up with oncology 2 weeks later, she elected home hospice for comfort care without further systemic treatment. She entered hospice 18 days after surgery and subsequently died sometime after.
