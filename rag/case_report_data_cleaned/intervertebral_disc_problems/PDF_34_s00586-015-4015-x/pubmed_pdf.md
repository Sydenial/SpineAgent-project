# New Concept of Pathogenesis of Impaired Circulation in Traumatic Cervical Spinal Cord Injury and Its Impact on Disease Severity: Case Series of Four Patients

## Abstract
Purpose The purpose of this study is to justify a new concept of the pathogenesis of secondary changes in the cervical spinal cord and its correlation with the depth of neurological disorders in spinal injury.

Methods Standard magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and angiography of the cervical and vertebral arteries of four patients were performed to diagnose the prevalence of ischemia and edema and to examine spinal cord vasculature. Correlation of imaging data with neurological status was performed.

Results Collateral circulation is most apparent in the upper cervical region above the C4 vertebra. Following occlusion of the vertebral artery, the circulation above C4 is maintained by collaterals of the ascending cervical artery. With extensive damage to the spinal cord, the intensity of edema and ischemia can be regarded as the effect of damage to radicular medullary arteries, which are injured in the intervertebral foramen. Secondary changes of the spinal cord are most apparent with impaired circulation in the artery of cervical enlargement.

Conclusions Collateral circulation is a significant factor that limits the damage to the cervical spinal cord. Impaired circulation in the artery of cervical enlargement is significant in extension of perifocal ischemia. The appearance of early arteriovenous shunting in the region of a primary spinal cord injury (contusion focus) by angiography is pathognomonic. The data obtained open a perspective for the endovascular treatment of spinal cord injury.

## Introduction
According to previously published studies, the main factors in the pathogenesis of spinal cord injury are attributed to primary and secondary mechanisms. Primary spinal cord injury occurs at the time of initial trauma, forming a zone of spinal cord contusion. Vascular disorders can be found locally, causing edema and hemorrhage. In the damaged area, paralysis of motor, sensory, and autonomic neuronal functions can occur. Vascular dysfunction, ischemia, glutamatergic excitotoxicity, inflammation, and apoptosis also develop in the perifocal zone [1–5].

In our opinion, one of the leading mechanisms in the formation and extensiveness of secondary injury is vascular dysfunction that occurs not only in the area of spinal cord injury but also in the vessels supplying the spinal cord, i.e., in branches of the vertebral, ascending, and deep cervical arteries.

Through a worldwide literature search, we did not identify a detailed clinical description of impaired circulation in spinal injury. Based on the experimental studies of G. Lazorthes et al. on vascularization and hemodynamics of the spinal cord, we conducted a clinical study of impaired circulation in the cervical spine and spinal cord after injury and defined the role of collateral circulation in this pathology [6–8]. Based on the results obtained, we correlated vascular disorders with the severity of spinal cord ischemia and clinical severity in patients.

## Materials and Methods
From May to August 2014, we examined four male patients with cervical spinal trauma, aged 20–62 years. Standard MRI of the affected spinal area and selective angiography of the cervical and vertebral arteries were performed to determine the extent of medullary damage, spinal cord compression, the prevalence of ischemia and edema, and spinal cord vascularization. Studies were conducted from day 1 to day 30 post-injury. Neurological impairment on the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) scale corresponded to A in two patients and D in the remaining two.

## Case Reports

### Case 1
A study of collateral circulation of the vertebral arteries was conducted in a patient with spinal trauma, spinal cord injury, and dislocation fracture of C4–C5. The uniqueness of the study is the visualization of collateral circulation following occlusion of both vertebral arteries. Clinically, the patient showed no brainstem disorders with impaired vital functions, and vertebrobasilar insufficiency was not evident. Collateral circulation was carried through both branches of the ascending cervical arteries that anastomosed with the vertebral arteries above the occlusions caused by the dislocation fracture, which prevented progression of vertebrobasilar insufficiency. Collaterals appeared immediately after injury, as suggested by the absence of vertebrobasilar symptoms in the first hours.

Patient X, male, 62 years. Diagnosis: spinal injury with contusion of the spinal cord in the cervical region; dislocation fracture of C4–C5; tetraparesis; ASIA D at admission. No sensory disorders or pelvic organ disorders were observed. On day 5 post-injury, MRI identified a dislocation fracture of C4–C5 and myelopathy at C4–C5 (Fig. 1).

On day 7, total angiography revealed occlusion of both vertebral arteries: in the V2 segment on the right at C6–C5 and from the V1–V2 segment up to the level of C4 on the left, with contrast enhancement above the occlusions via anastomoses of both ascending cervical arteries. Blood flow was anterograde (Fig. 2).

On day 10, surgery was performed to correct the C4–C5 dislocation fracture using combined access. Interbody corporodesis of C4–C5 was performed with a titanium cage and plate via an anterior approach. Nitinol shape memory staples were placed at the C4 arc and the C5 acanthi via a posterior approach (Fig. 3).

Twelve days after surgery, repeat angiography of the vertebral and cervical arteries on both sides showed partial recanalization of the right vertebral artery lumen in the V2 segment at C6–C5; spinal branches began to opacify at this level, and a short occlusion of the right vertebral artery was seen above. Occlusion of the left vertebral artery occurred from the artery mouth; good flows were observed via anastomoses in the vertebral arteries from both ascending cervical arteries. Blood flow was anterograde (Fig. 4).

We observed a pattern in the intensity of spread of secondary spinal cord injury in relation to damage to the artery of cervical enlargement. The patient had severe spinal cord compression, with a myelopathy focus (contusion) at C4–C5, yet with relatively preserved neurological status. Angiography revealed the artery of cervical enlargement, described by A. Lazorthes et al. as a major arterial branch in the form of a pin, extending from the proximal segment of the occluded right vertebral artery [6–8]. The presence of blood flow in this artery maintained spinal cord perfusion and limited neurological impairment and the extension of edema/ischemia in cord regions above and below the contusion focus (Fig. 5).

### Case 2
Patient S, male, 29 years. Diagnosis: spinal injury; compression and comminuted fracture of the C7 body; contusion of the spinal cord in the cervical region. Neurological status corresponded to ASIA D after injury and did not change during the study.

Surgery was performed on day 25: corpectomy of C7 followed by corporodesis with a mesh cage and plate. Postoperative MRI of the cervical spinal cord and angiography of the vertebral arteries were performed (Fig. 6).

Circulation in the vertebral arteries was maintained. Blood flow in the artery of cervical enlargement maintained spinal cord perfusion, limiting neurological impairment and the extension of edema–ischemia. Blood flow was anterograde (Fig. 7). MRI showed minimal extension of the zone of edema and ischemia. There were no changes in neurological status after treatment.

### Case 3
Patient A, male, 25 years. Diagnosis: spinal injury; compression and comminuted fracture of the C7 body; contusion of the spinal cord in the cervical region. ASIA A.

MRI was performed on day 4 and revealed an apparent zone of edema and ischemia (Fig. 8).

Surgery was performed on day 12: corpectomy of C7 followed by corporodesis with a mesh cage and plate.

Total angiography on day 17 showed maintained vertebral arterial circulation. The artery of cervical enlargement was not visible; there was accelerated arteriovenous shunting from spinal branches of the vertebral and ascending cervical arteries on the right; spinal branches were not enhanced on the left. Blood flow was anterograde (Fig. 9).

### Case 4
Patient D, male, 20 years. Diagnosis: spinal injury; compression and comminuted fracture of the C5 body; contusion of the spinal cord in the cervical region. ASIA A.

Angiography on day 10 post-injury showed maintained circulation in the vertebral arteries. The artery of cervical enlargement was absent, and spinal branches were not enhanced. Blood flow was anterograde (Fig. 10).

Surgery was performed on day 11: corpectomy of C5 followed by corporodesis with a mesh cage and plate.

Cases 3 and 4 show damage to the artery of cervical enlargement, following rupture or spasm of the branches of the vertebral and ascending cervical arteries, as indicated by the lack of contrast enhancement of the artery of cervical enlargement and a restricted vascular pattern in branches of the vertebral, ascending, and deep cervical arteries. MRI showed extensive ischemic edema of the spinal cord in the perifocal area of primary damage. There was persistent neurological impairment with ASIA A. There were no changes in neurological status after treatment.

We also noted early arteriovenous shunting in the area of the injury focus. As a result of reduced perfusion in spinal cord injury, arteriolar circulation is disturbed. We believe this is due to compensatory recruitment of small anastomotic branches within the spinal cord. This phenomenon was observed in all four cases (Fig. 11).

## Conclusions
Collateral circulation is a significant factor in spinal cord injury that limits damage to the cervical spinal cord. Impaired circulation in the artery of cervical enlargement is significant in the extension of perifocal ischemia. The appearance of early arteriovenous shunting in the region of a primary spinal cord injury (contusion focus) by angiography is pathognomonic. The data obtained in this study open a perspective for endovascular treatment of spinal cord injury.
