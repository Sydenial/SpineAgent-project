# The Morel-Lavallée Lesion Revisited: Management in Spinopelvic Dissociation

## Abstract
BACKGROUND CONTEXT: The Morel-Lavallée lesion occurs from compression and shear forces that separate the skin and subcutaneous tissue from the underlying muscular fascia. A dead space is created that becomes filled with blood, liquefied fat, and lymphatic fluid from the shearing of vasculature and lymphatics. If not treated appropriately, these lesions can become infected, cause tissue necrosis, or form chronic seromas.

PURPOSE: To review appropriate identification and treatment of Morel-Lavallée lesions in spinopelvic dissociation patients.

STUDY DESIGN: Uncontrolled case series.

METHODS: Retrospective review of medical records. No funding was received in support of this study. The authors report no conflicts of interest.

RESULTS: We present four cases of traumatic spinopelvic dissociation, all with concomitant lumbosacral Morel-Lavallée lesions. Appropriate treatment included irrigation and debridement, drainage, antibiotics, and vacuum-assisted wound closure.

CONCLUSIONS: Our series reflects an association of Morel-Lavallée lesions with spinopelvic dissociation trauma. The rotatory injury that occurs at the spinopelvic junction may create shear forces that form the Morel-Lavallée lesion. When presented with a spinopelvic dissociation patient, one should be prepared to treat a Morel-Lavallée lesion.

## Introduction
The Morel-Lavallée lesion, or closed-degloving injury, was first described in 1848 by Maurice Morel-Lavallée [1]. It results from compression and shear forces that separate the skin and subcutaneous tissue from the underlying muscular fascia [2]. A dead space forms and fills with blood, liquefied fat, and lymphatic fluid due to shearing of vasculature and lymphatics. If not treated appropriately, these lesions can become infected, cause tissue necrosis, or form chronic seromas.

We present four cases of traumatic spinopelvic dissociation, all with concomitant lumbosacral Morel-Lavallée lesions. We propose that patients with traumatic spinopelvic dissociation may have associated Morel-Lavallée lesions. In the setting of spinopelvic dissociation requiring operative fixation, management of overlying Morel-Lavallée lesions becomes paramount to avoid complications from soft-tissue compromise or infection. All patients provided written consent for publication.

## Case Report

### Case 1
An 18-year-old man was struck by a construction dump truck while walking his bicycle and was dragged several hundred feet. He presented as a Level 1 trauma and required primary right-sided hemipelvectomy for an unsalvageable ischemic right lower extremity. Initial pelvic radiographs showed major pelvic trauma with concern for injury at the spinopelvic junction. Any major pelvic trauma should raise concern for Morel-Lavallée lesions of the pelvis; similarly, suspected spinopelvic trauma should prompt concern for lesions near the spine.

He underwent multiple operations by orthopedic, vascular, general, plastics, and urologic services for open pelvic fractures, spinopelvic dissociation, urethral disruption, bladder injury, right testicular and epididymal head injury, ligation of the external iliac artery and vein, and multiple irrigation and debridement (I&D) procedures. Approximately 1 month after injury, he was stable for spinopelvic fixation. In the operating room (OR), after incision into the subcutaneous tissue, a large amount of purulent material was found. Further exploration revealed a Morel-Lavallée lesion traveling along the right side of the midline incision. The wound was thoroughly irrigated and debrided, packed with sterile gauze, and sealed. He returned to the OR on postoperative days 2 and 4 for repeat I&D, with two 10-French drains placed in the lesion to low-wall suction.

His spine injury involved right and left sacroiliac joint disarticulation, a left iliac fracture, and a rotatory subluxation of L5 on S1 with a U-shaped sacral fracture. On postoperative day 6 from the index procedure, he underwent L2–ilium posterior spinal fusion with L5–S3 decompression and closure of the Morel-Lavallée lesion. The lesion was tacked down with suture. Two drains were placed within the lesion to low-wall suction and removed on postoperative days 3 and 4 after output fell to <30 cc per 24 hours. At 8.5 months after spine surgery, there was no evidence of spinal wound infection.

### Case 2
An unrestrained 17-year-old male was involved in a motor vehicle accident. He presented as a Level 1 trauma with traumatic amputation of the left lower extremity, an open fracture of the left upper extremity with vascular injury, pelvic fractures, a lumbosacral fracture, and free fluid on abdominal ultrasound. He underwent exploratory laparotomy for splenic and hepatic lacerations, treatment of the left upper extremity injury, and a left above-knee amputation.

His spine injuries included a left-sided spinopelvic dissociation with fracture of the left L5–S1 facet with displacement and distraction. Approximately 2 weeks after injury, he was cleared for spinopelvic fixation. In the OR, a left-sided Morel-Lavallée lesion was encountered at the level of the planned incision. There were no obvious signs of infection, so he underwent L5–ilium posterior spinal fusion and instrumentation. The lesion was tacked down with suture, and two drains were placed to low-wall suction. Drains were removed on postoperative days 3 and 4 after outputs were <30 cc per 24 hours. At approximately 6 months postoperatively, there were no signs of spinal wound infection.

### Case 3
A 58-year-old man presented as a Level 2 trauma after a tractor rollover with multiple pelvic fractures. He had a left L5/S1 fracture-dislocation. Approximately 1 week after injury, he underwent L4–ilium posterior spinal fusion and instrumentation with a left sacroiliac screw. A Morel-Lavallée lesion to the right of midline was thoroughly irrigated without drain placement within the lesion; a single 10-French subfascial drain was placed during this surgery.

On postoperative day 3, he developed significant drainage from the spine incision, fevers up to 103 F, and bogginess around the incision. He returned to the OR for I&D. Two 10-French drains were placed subfascially and two within the Morel-Lavallée lesion. Intraoperative cultures grew pan-sensitive Enterobacter cloacae, treated with long-term intravenous vancomycin and ertapenem. On postoperative day 7 from the index procedure, persistent fevers prompted repeat I&D with placement of one 15-French drain in the lesion. Drains within the lesion were placed to low-wall suction and removed after output was <=30 cc in 24 hours.

He developed a partial wound dehiscence treated with vacuum-assisted closure (VAC). Ultimately, a 10 × 4 cm sacral wound required I&D and complex closure with plastic surgery approximately 3 months later. Two and a half weeks after this procedure, he presented with purulent drainage and returned to the OR for I&D and VAC for secondary closure. At approximately 7 months after injury, the sacral wound was slowly healing by secondary intention with VAC therapy. He had recently stopped antibiotics without subsequent signs of infection.

### Case 4
A 29-year-old woman was transferred to our Level 1 trauma center after being struck by a train as a pedestrian. She sustained lumbopelvic dissociation, an L1 compression fracture, multiple rib fractures, a scapula fracture, renal and splenic lacerations, and a closed head injury. The posterior skin was devascularized over the sacrum, and there was a large Morel-Lavallée lesion extending to the thoracic spine. Her hospital course was complicated by rhabdomyolysis, splenectomy, and nephrectomy.

She initially underwent soft-tissue resection of necrotic posterior skin and debridement of the Morel-Lavallée lesion, followed by spinopelvic fixation and anterior ring fixation. Posterior skin coverage remained a substantial problem, requiring multiple I&D operations with VAC exchanges. Plastic surgery achieved coverage with a split-thickness skin graft after two and a half months of wound management. She subsequently developed a chronic draining sinus and underwent removal of instrumentation to help eradicate infection. Durable posterior sacral soft-tissue coverage has not been achievable due to traumatic embolization of the gluteal arteries and extensive soft-tissue trauma. She remains ambulatory with significant pelvic pain. Further spinopelvic stabilization is not possible until soft-tissue coverage can be achieved.

## Discussion
The incidence of Morel-Lavallée lesions is commonly reported in pelvic trauma, approximately 8.3%, and may be higher when subclinical lesions are included [3]. Because spinopelvic dissociation and pelvic trauma both arise from major trauma, these injuries may occur in the same patient. We found no literature clearly prioritizing which injury should be addressed first regarding outcomes; in our experience, polytrauma patients are initially stabilized medically and managed with a pelvic external fixator if needed. After definitive pelvic fixation, the patient can be positioned for posterior spinal surgery, with spinal precautions maintained (bed rest, log-rolling). Given the association suggested by our series, definitive spinal fixation is not advisable until any Morel-Lavallée lesion has been addressed. Pelvic fixation may proceed during this interval. Multidisciplinary coordination among trauma surgery, orthopedic trauma, and spine surgery is essential.

Diagnosis of a Morel-Lavallée lesion may be assisted by computed tomography or ultrasound; however, magnetic resonance imaging is the modality of choice [4]. In our series, we retrospectively identified these lesions as subcutaneous fluid collections on initial trauma CT imaging. These collections can be subtle amid extensive pelvic injuries. Importantly, Morel-Lavallée lesions are primarily a clinical diagnosis: skin findings may include shear injury (eg, road rash, tire tread marks), a fluctuant boggy mass, extensive bruising, and decreased sensation over the area. Delayed treatment may allow encapsulation by granulation tissue, forming a fibrous pseudocapsule and a chronic lesion [5]. A high index of suspicion is required, as up to a third of patients are undiagnosed initially [6].

Complication rates are higher in patients with Morel-Lavallée lesions. Suzuki et al. treated 20 vertically unstable sacral fractures operatively, including five with Morel-Lavallée lesions; both postoperative infections occurred in patients with these lesions [7]. Hak et al. reported positive cultures in 11 of 24 (46%) lesions, supporting aggressive I&D [8]. Time to OR did not correlate with culture positivity (negative: mean 13.7 days, range 4–32; positive: mean 12.5 days, range 2–60). These cases often involve polytrauma with other life-threatening injuries that delay intervention.

Conservative management, including aspiration and compression, has been described [9]. Carlson et al. reported that nonoperative observation in patients without an underlying fracture generally results in spontaneous resolution, usually without skin necrosis [10]. However, success depends on lesion size, proximity to other wounds, pressure effect on the skin, and the need for hardware-based fixation.

Surgical management typically involves I&D, either via two small proximal/distal incisions or a longitudinal incision along the lesion. Multiple debridements with drains or VAC are common, followed by closure of the dead space by suturing the subcutaneous tissue to fascia or by using a sclerotic agent or adhesive [11–13]. Carlson et al. treated 22 lesions by longitudinal incision, evacuation, curettage/rongeuring to viable tissue, pulsatile lavage, fracture treatment (if present), drain placement (2–5 days for acute lesions and several weeks for chronic), and closure, with no infections or fluid re-accumulation; two patients had marginal flap necrosis [10]. Tornetta et al. described two small incisions with scrub-brush debridement and irrigation, placement of two Jackson-Pratt drains to low-wall suction, and removal when output was <30 cc per 24 hours [14].

Our series supports an association between Morel-Lavallée lesions and spinopelvic dissociation. High-energy pelvic mechanisms produce shear forces that predispose to these lesions; the additional rotatory component in spinopelvic dissociation may contribute to shear at the spinopelvic junction. When managing a spinopelvic dissociation patient, one should be prepared to treat a Morel-Lavallée lesion. If there are no obvious signs of infection upon exposure, thorough I&D with concurrent surgical fixation may be performed. Two drains should be placed within the lesion to low-wall suction until output is <30 cc per 24 hours, and the lesion should be tacked down to the underlying fascia to eliminate dead space. In these high-risk wounds, vancomycin powder and incisional VAC therapy may be appropriate adjuncts. If the lesion shows signs of infection or significant compromise, multiple I&Ds may be warranted before definitive fixation. Appropriate identification and treatment of Morel-Lavallée lesions in spinopelvic dissociation likely improve the probability of successful outcomes.
