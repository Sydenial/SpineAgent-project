# A rare case of concomitant cervical disc herniation and intradural meningioma treated with one-stage posterior surgery

## Abstract

**Study design:** Case report.  
**Purpose:** To treat a case of cervical radiculopathy concomitant with an intradural tumor, using a single one-stage posterior surgery combining tumor resection and transdural discectomy.  
**Summary of background data:** Coexistence of symptomatic disc herniation and intraspinal tumor in the same cervical segment is extremely rare. Usually, two-stage anterior and posterior surgeries are needed to treat the two conditions, respectively. One-stage posterior surgery to treat both pathologies simultaneously has not been reported in the literature.  
**Methods and results:** A 76-year-old man presented with leg weakness and numbness for 6 months and left arm pain for 2 months. He was diagnosed with a C3/4 disc herniation and a coexisting intradural lesion on contrast-enhanced MR imaging. A one-stage posterior procedure was performed. Histological studies confirmed a herniated disc and a meningioma.  
**Conclusion:** In rare cases, an intradural tumor coexists with cervical disc herniation. When suspicious findings are noticed, or clinical symptoms cannot be fully explained, contrast MR imaging is helpful in differential diagnosis. Microscopic transdural discectomy is safe and could be used as an optional procedure for cervical disc herniation in some cases.

**Keywords:** Cervical disc herniation; Spinal tumor; Intradural meningioma; Transdural discectomy

## Introduction

Cervical disc herniation is a common spinal disorder that can lead to radiculopathy and myelopathy [1]. For those who fail to respond to conservative treatments, anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) currently is the standard treatment [2]. Cervical intraspinal tumors are a group of less common diseases that may produce symptoms similar to those of cervical disc herniation [3]. Meningioma is one of the most common intraspinal tumors in the cervical spine, which typically needs surgical removal via a posterior approach [4].

Symptomatic disc herniation and intradural tumor occurring at the same cervical spinal level is extremely rare. Given the different surgical approaches for the two pathologies, two-stage surgery would be a common choice for most spine surgeons. Here, we report a rare case of C3/4 disc herniation concomitant with an intradural meningioma at the same level, which was successfully treated using a single one-stage posterior surgery of tumor resection and transdural discectomy.

## Case presentation

A 76-year-old man presented with leg weakness and numbness for 6 months and left arm pain for 2 months. Six months prior, he developed leg weakness and numbness. While walking, he often felt that his legs were “stepping in cottons.” Although the weakness reduced his mobility to some extent, he could live on his own and did not seek health care. Two months prior to presentation, he developed left arm pain radiating from his neck down to his left shoulder and elbow. The pain was severe and intolerable, accompanied by worsening gait disturbance. He was evaluated at a local hospital, where magnetic resonance (MR) imaging revealed a herniated disc at C3/4. He received conservative treatment for 2 weeks without relief. By the time he consulted us, he could hardly walk independently. He did not have bladder or bowel problems.

On physical examination, there was tenderness in the upper neck. Spurling sign and Eaton sign were positive on the left side. Muscle power was generally decreased in bilateral upper and lower extremities (manual muscle test grade III–IV). Reflexes at biceps, triceps, patellar, and Achilles tendons were hyperactive. Pathological reflexes, including Hoffman sign, Babinski sign, and dynamic Romberg sign, were positive. The Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA) score was 9 out of 17.

On routine MR images, the C3/4 intervertebral disc herniated posteriorly and dural sac compression was apparent. Moreover, there was heterogeneous signal within the C3 spinal cord. In addition, it appeared that cerebrospinal fluid flow was discontinued at that level. The patient was suspected to have a cervical intradural mass and thus underwent gadolinium contrast MR imaging. Contrast MR imaging revealed a hyperintense intradural lesion at the right portion of the C3 canal. Axial MR images demonstrated that the cervical spinal canal behind the C3/4 disc was occupied by a centrally and left-sided herniated disc and an intradural lesion on the right. Spinal cord compression and left foraminal narrowing were remarkable.

A one-stage posterior surgery, including C3/4 laminectomy, intradural tumor resection, transdural C3/4 discectomy, and C3/4 lateral mass instrumentation and fusion, was performed to treat the two distinct pathologies simultaneously. After C3/4 laminectomy, the dorsal dural sac was opened to identify an intradural lesion (approximately $10\times15~\mathrm{mm}^2$) on the right side of the dural sac. Under microscopy, the mass was carefully removed from the dura mater. On the left portion of the canal, there was a hump compressing the ventral dural sac. The spinal cord was gently pushed to the right and protected. A needle was inserted into the hump, and intraoperative X-ray confirmed that it was at the C3/4 disc level. Under microscopy, a 0.5 cm longitudinal incision was made in the ventral dura mater, through which herniated nucleus tissue was removed. Due to limited exposure, the ventral dural incision was difficult to suture and was sealed with gelfoam and human fibrin adhesive. The dorsal dural sac was sutured, followed by C3/4 lateral mass screw instrumentation and fusion.

After surgery, the patient’s left arm pain disappeared and limb numbness substantially improved. Walking ability improved quickly, and he was able to walk independently by postoperative day 10 when discharged. Pathological studies confirmed a herniated disc and a meningioma.

At 6-month follow-up, the patient walked independently with normal gait. He had no pain and no numbness in arms or legs. His JOA score increased to 16 points. X-ray revealed a solid C3/4 posterior fusion, and MR images demonstrated that the spinal canal was enlarged without dural sac or nerve root compression.

## Discussion

Coexistence of symptomatic disc herniation and intraspinal tumor in the same cervical segment is extremely rare. In the few cases reported, the spinal tumor usually is an asymptomatic incidental finding when MR study was conducted for cervical disc herniation [5, 6]. In the current case, C3/4 disc herniation was mainly responsible for left arm radiculopathy, as evidenced by foraminal involvement on MR images. Yet, both the herniated disc and the meningioma contributed to cervical myelopathy. As both pathologies were symptomatic, treating only one might not relieve all symptoms.

Although cervical discectomy is routinely performed through an anterior approach and cervical intradural tumor resection typically requires a posterior approach, we designed a one-stage surgery of posterior intradural resection and transdural discectomy to treat both pathologies simultaneously. Because the spinal cord was compressed by the tumor from the right and by the herniated disc from the left, and because the patient had severe left arm pain with corresponding signs of narrowed C3/4 foramen on MR images, we performed a full laminectomy to achieve sufficient decompression. Although both lesions could potentially be removed through a right hemilaminectomy and posterior instrumentation and fusion might be avoidable, discectomy through a contralateral window of hemilaminectomy is risky [7] and depends heavily on surgical experience. Even so, the one-stage strategy involves far less overall trauma and cost compared with two-stage surgery, and the patient achieved full recovery.

In the English literature, only two similar cases were reported, and both were treated with two-stage surgeries [5, 6]. Bapat et al. reported concomitant disc herniation and intradural schwannoma at C5/6 [5]. The intradural lesion was overlooked initially and C5/6 ACDF was performed. Only after recurrent myelopathy did physicians retrospectively identify the coexisting intradural lesion, and the patient underwent a second posterior surgery; histology revealed schwannoma. Similarly, a coexisting meningioma was overlooked in Gandolfi’s case, requiring a second surgery for cervical tumor resection [6].

Among spinal tumors, meningioma and schwannoma constitute approximately 90% of extramedullary intradural tumors [8]. Although typically benign [9], intradural meningiomas can cause symptoms similar to disc herniation. Meningiomas usually present isointense signal similar to spinal cord on both T1- and T2-weighted MR images, which can obscure detection. This is especially relevant in middle-aged adults, where disc bulging or herniation is common and neurologic symptoms may be attributed to disc pathology, as in Bapat’s case [5]. When heterogeneous signal within the spinal cord is noticed on conventional MR images, when diagnosis is unclear, when pain is exacerbated in recumbent position, when pain continues despite treatment, or when neurological symptoms cannot be explained by slight compression from disc herniation, suspicion of tumor should be raised and contrast MR imaging is indicated [10, 11]. Strong and homogeneous enhancement with gadolinium, as in our case, is characteristic of meningiomas [9]. Posterior approach provides adequate exposure and is routinely used to remove cervical meningiomas [4].

Although ACDF is the standard surgical procedure for symptomatic cervical disc herniation, posterior transdural discectomy was historically attempted. As early as 1910, Taylor introduced laminectomy and a transdural approach to remove ventral lesions [12]. Due to high risk of spinal cord injury, this procedure was not widely adopted [13]. After the anterior Smith-Robinson approach was introduced in 1958, posterior transdural discectomy was largely abandoned [14]. With advances in microscopy and technique, transdural discectomy may be less risky than previously thought. In a report of 30 cases of cervical spondylosis treated with microscopic transdural discectomy, no spinal cord injury or cerebrospinal fluid leakage occurred [15]. This procedure may preserve the cervical segment because fusion is not required [15]. However, it is not familiar to most spine surgeons and has been recommended only as an alternative option for selected patients with paracentral cervical disc herniation associated with multilevel canal stenosis [13]. In the current case, because the intradural meningioma required posterior laminectomy and dural opening and the herniated disc was directly ventral to the dura, transdural discectomy was practical and allowed the patient to avoid a second anterior surgery.

## Conclusions

In rare cases, an intradural tumor may coexist with cervical disc herniation. Neurological symptoms may be attributed to the herniated disc, and the intradural tumor may be overlooked. When suspicious findings are noticed, or clinical symptoms cannot be fully explained, contrast MR imaging is helpful in differential diagnosis. One-stage posterior surgery including laminectomy, intradural tumor resection, and transdural discectomy can be used to treat two pathologies simultaneously and reduce medical costs. Transdural discectomy is an optional surgery for cervical disc herniation in some patients with other concomitant spine disorders.
