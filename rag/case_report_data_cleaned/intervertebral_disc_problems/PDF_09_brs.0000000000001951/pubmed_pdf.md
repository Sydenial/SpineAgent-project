# Giant Cell Tumor of Cervical Spine Presenting as Acute Asphyxia

## Abstract

**Study Design.** Case report and literature review.  
**Objective.** To describe treatment of a unique case of acute airway obstruction by a large C7 giant cell tumor (GCT) with preoperative denosumab followed by surgical resection, and to review the literature on this rare entity.  
**Summary of Background Data.** Standard treatment for GCTs includes surgical resection or curettage and packing. Large lesions in the spine may require preoperative therapy with denosumab, a human monoclonal antibody to RANKL, to facilitate surgery. It is highly unusual for GCT arising in the cervical spine to present with acute asphyxia requiring tracheostomy.  
**Methods.** We report a patient with a large C7 GCT that caused tracheal compression with almost complete airway obstruction requiring emergency intubation.  
**Results.** The tumor responded to subcutaneously administered denosumab with marked decrease in size and relief of symptoms. Increased tumor mineralization in response to therapy facilitated subsequent successful surgical tumor resection. The patient remains symptom-free 2 years after surgery without tumor recurrence.  
**Conclusion.** Denosumab can shrink the size of large GCTs, providing symptom relief before surgery and facilitating tumor resection.

## Introduction

Giant cell tumor (GCT) of bone is a benign tumor that most often involves the long bones. Spinal disease is rare and most commonly involves the sacrum [1,2]. We report the case of a young woman with a large GCT of C7 that caused marked tracheal narrowing with acute asphyxia. Therapy with denosumab resulted in a dramatic decrease in tumor size, relieving airway obstruction, and increased tumor mineralization, which facilitated surgical resection.

## Case Report

A 20-year-old woman presented to an outside emergency center with acute dyspnea requiring intubation after being treated as asthma for progressive shortness of breath and wheezing over the preceding 4 months. Computed tomography showed a large expansile lytic lesion involving the C7 vertebra with a soft tissue component resulting in anterior tracheal displacement and marked tracheal narrowing. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a heterogeneously enhancing, large, lobulated mass arising from a severely compressed C7 vertebral body measuring $10.0 \times 9.0 \times 7.5\ \text{cm}$ (craniocaudal × transverse × anteroposterior), with associated spinal canal stenosis due to C7 retropulsion.

The endotracheal tube was exchanged for a tracheostomy because of continued dyspnea, and the patient was transferred to our institution. Review of the outside biopsy material confirmed the diagnosis of GCT.

As the tumor was deemed inoperable at presentation, the patient was treated with three cycles of subcutaneous denosumab. Follow-up computed tomography demonstrated a marked reduction in tumor size to $6.2 \times 6.3 \times 6.8\ \text{cm}$ and increased tumor mineralization. Magnetic resonance imaging showed markedly decreased tumor enhancement with nonenhancing areas corresponding to mineralization seen on CT. The tracheal compression and displacement had resolved.

A two-stage surgical resection was performed. This consisted of an initial anterior C7–T1 intralesional corpectomy with titanium cage reconstruction, followed by C7–T1 partial laminectomy and posterior element resection with anterior C6–T2 and posterior C4–T4 stabilization. Histological examination of the resected specimen showed no residual GCT. The patient remains neurologically intact, with good neck range of motion and no radiologic evidence of tumor recurrence 2 years after surgery.

## Discussion

Cervical spine GCTs are rare and almost invariably symptomatic [1–3]. Small cervical GCTs may be treated with curettage and packing; however, lesions confined to the anterior vertebral body are associated with high recurrence rates ranging from 30% to 50% [4]. As a result, resection with wide margins is preferred when feasible. Two-stage surgery is often required, combining anterior and posterior excision supplemented by spinal fusion [4–10].

When wide-margin resection is not possible because of the risk of neurological deficit or spinal instability [4,11,12], alternative or neoadjuvant therapies may be beneficial. Serial arterial embolization and alpha-interferon therapy have shown efficacy in selected cases [4,13,14]. Bisphosphonates have also been advocated for locally aggressive or inoperable GCTs [15–17]. The role of radiotherapy remains controversial due to reported risks of sarcomatous transformation and high recurrence rates when used as monotherapy [3–5,12,18].

Denosumab is a human monoclonal antibody directed against receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-β ligand (RANKL), a key mediator of osteoclastic activity [19–21]. By inhibiting the RANK–RANKL interaction, denosumab suppresses osteoclast-mediated bone destruction. In a phase 2 clinical trial, 96% of patients with unresectable GCTs responded favorably, and 90% of patients initially planned for major surgery were able to avoid surgery or undergo less morbid procedures after denosumab therapy [19,21,22]. In addition to reducing tumor size, denosumab often induces new bone formation [4,11,22–25], as demonstrated in the present case. Neoadjuvant downstaging with denosumab before resection of spinal GCTs has been previously reported [11,24].

## Conclusion

We describe a unique case of a large C7 giant cell tumor presenting with almost complete airway occlusion requiring emergent intubation and tracheostomy. Treatment with denosumab resulted in significant tumor shrinkage, relief of tracheal compression, and increased mineralization, which facilitated successful subsequent surgical resection.

## Key Points

- Giant cell tumor of the cervical spine presenting as acute asphyxia is rare.  
- Therapy with denosumab can result in tumor shrinkage and increased mineralization.  
- These effects can facilitate definitive surgical resection.
