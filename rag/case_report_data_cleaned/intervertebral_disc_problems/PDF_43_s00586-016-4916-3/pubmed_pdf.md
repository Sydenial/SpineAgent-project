# Traumatic Atlantoaxial Rotatory Fixation in an Adult Patient

## Abstract
Introduction Atlantoaxial rotational fixation (AARF) is a rare entity in adults, with only a few cases reported in the English literature and often associated with a traumatic mechanism. It is an underdiagnosed condition that must be taken into account in the initial assessment of all craniocervical trauma. Both diagnostic and therapeutic delay may be a potential cause of severe neurological damage or even death of the patient. The therapeutic management is controversial given the difficulty of achieving optimum stability and permanent reduction.

Methods and results A 28-year-old woman was involved in a traffic accident a week before coming to the emergency department with rotation and irreducible cervical flexion from trauma and severe neck pain. CT and MRI were performed and showed a cervical spinal AARF with transverse and alar ligaments intact and preserved atlantoaxial distance (Fielding I). The patient was treated by progressive cervical traction with 5 kg, and manual reduction was completed in 24 h. Subsequently, external immobilization with a rigid cervical collar was maintained for 16 weeks. The clinical course was good, with the patient regaining full mobility and improvement in neck pain.

Conclusions The purpose of this paper is to show a case of a young woman with posttraumatic AARF successfully treated conservatively. This case delineates the difficulties in diagnosing this pathology, as well as the challenges encountered in its management.

## Introduction
The unilateral rotational subluxation of the atlantoaxial joint is a relatively common pathology in cervical trauma [1]. However, bilateral rotational subluxation is rare, particularly in the adult population. There are only a few cases reported in the English literature, of which only 16 are Fielding type I with no other associated lesions (Table 1).

In children, it is a common condition, usually caused by a mild trauma or, in many cases, by a respiratory infection of the upper respiratory tract (Grisel syndrome) [2–4]. The reason that it predominates in children is due to the higher relative proportion of head size relative to the body; the insufficient development of the neck muscles; the elasticity of the articular capsule and the large angle of rotation between C1 and C2; and the horizontal configuration of the facet joints between atlas and axis [5–7].

In adults, the mechanism of injury is high-energy trauma such as car accidents, falls, or sports accidents [2]. Given the nature of the trauma, patients often have other complications such as damage to the articular cartilage or fractures in the facet joints [1]. They may also have associated spinal cord injury, which will depend on the integrity of the transverse ligament, the displacement of C1 on C2, and the invasion of the spinal canal [8].

The pathophysiology of AARF is not well defined. The atlantoaxial joint contributes about 50% of axial neck rotation, and the transverse and alar ligaments provide most biomechanical ligamentous stability. The transverse ligament and facet joint capsule prevent anterior translation of C1 on C2. The alar ligaments connect the posterolateral apex of the odontoid with the lateral aspect of the foramen magnum bilaterally, mainly limiting anterior shift of the atlas on the odontoid and excessive rotation of C1 on C2 to approximately 50° [3, 9]. There is evidence from cadaveric dissection and magnetic resonance imaging studies to suggest that the pathologic mechanism of rotatory subluxation involves disruption of the facet capsule followed by alar ligament disruption. The lateral mass of the atlas rotating posteriorly can lock behind the ipsilateral lateral mass in severe forms of atlantoaxial rotatory instability [3, 10].

Fielding and Hawkins described four types of AARF [11]. This classification, which has been widely accepted, correlates with increased risk of spinal instability and potential neurologic impairment. Type I indicates displacement of the facet joints without a corresponding increase in the atlantodens interval, and rotation within the normal range; this is the only case that can occur without ligament rupture and where the dens acts as a pivot. The transverse ligament may rupture, producing an increase in atlantodens interval by 3–5 mm (Type II). If the atlas is dislocated bilaterally with displacement more than 5 mm, this is Type III, and if it displaces backward causing narrowing of the spinal canal, it is Type IV [11].

The clinical findings typically show painful torticollis with lateral neck flexion and contralateral rotation, the position known as Cock-Robin [11, 12]. A high index of suspicion, physical examination, and appropriate imaging tests are the basis for early diagnosis.

CT of the cervical spine is the study of choice to detect this type of injury, since it provides excellent visualization of abnormal relations between C1 and C2, allows determination of unilateral versus bilateral involvement, and identifies associated fractures. Three-dimensional reconstructions offer an overview of the deformity [9, 11]. MRI is essential to assess the spinal cord, evaluate for disc fragments in the canal, and confirm the integrity of the ligamentous structures, especially alar and transverse ligaments [8].

## Case Report

### Presentation
A 28-year-old woman was involved in a traffic accident; she was driving a car when she suffered a frontal collision and the car turned right over in mid-air. She did not lose consciousness and exited the car on her own. She went to the hospital, where a cervicodorsal X-ray was performed, and she was discharged with a diagnosis of whiplash trauma. Six days later, she went to the hospital for persistent and severe neck pain and paresthesias in the first three fingers of her left hand. Physical examination revealed irreducible cervical rotation to the right and flexion to the left since the trauma.

### Imaging
CT of the cervical spine showed rotational subluxation between C1 and C2 without fracture lines. Cervical MRI confirmed AARF with enlargement of the right C1–C2 lateral recess occupied by increased soft tissue, with integrity of the transverse and alar ligaments. The atlantoaxial distance was preserved, and there were no signs of spinal cord compression. See Figs. 1, 2, and 3.

### Procedure
The patient was taken to the operating room and treated with progressive cervical traction up to 5 kg and fluoroscopically guided manual reduction, which was completed in 24 h. A cervical CT scan at 72 h showed reduction of the AARF with a discreet residual rotating component. She was discharged after 7 days with improvement of neck pain and disappearance of paresthesias. External immobilization with a rigid cervical collar was maintained for 16 weeks. Neck pain persisted for several months, but with rehabilitation and medical treatment her condition improved. See Figs. 4 and 5.

### Outcome
After 4 years of follow-up, the clinical outcome is excellent. She has recovered cervical mobility and has only slight neck pain when performing intense exercise.

## Discussion
Therapeutic management is controversial because of the rarity of the disease and generally requires some form of immobilization, either internal or external. The goals of treatment are to restore pain-free range of motion, prevent or reverse any neurological compromise, and restore spinal stability [9]. Options include conservative treatment with immobilization, traction, and manual reduction, or surgery [4, 6]. Early diagnosis is important to enable early treatment and reduce the probability of permanent neurological damage [3, 6].

Some authors, such as Weisskopf et al. [10], have reported surgical treatment by posterior fixation, but many others [9, 11, 12] recommend conservative treatment using manual cervical traction and external immobilization, either a halo or rigid collar for 6–12 weeks; this achieves good rotational stability in most cases. Surgical treatment is reserved for spinal instability, neurological deficits, delayed diagnosis, or failure of conservative treatment [1].

In this case, the patient was treated with progressive cervical traction and manual reduction, followed by external immobilization with a rigid collar for 16 weeks, achieving good stability on follow-up imaging. Conservative management was selected due to spinal stability.
