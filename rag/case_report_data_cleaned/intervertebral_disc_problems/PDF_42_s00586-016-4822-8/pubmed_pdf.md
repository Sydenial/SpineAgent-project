# Retroperitoneal haematoma in a postoperative ALIF patient taking rivaroxaban for atrial fibrillation

## Abstract
Background: Novel oral anticoagulants (NOACs) are increasingly used for secondary prevention of thromboembolic stroke in atrial fibrillation. Perioperative management is challenging, and unexpected complications have been reported.

Case report: We report a rivaroxaban-induced retroperitoneal haematoma in a 72-year-old man who underwent L5/S1 anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) for grade 1 spondylolytic spondylolisthesis. He was taking rivaroxaban, a factor Xa inhibitor, for thromboembolic risk reduction. Following NOAC guidelines, rivaroxaban was stopped 2 days preoperatively and restarted on the third postoperative day. On the ninth postoperative day he presented with severe left iliac fossa pain, nausea, vomiting, and swelling and bruising around the surgical site. CT demonstrated a large expanding retroperitoneal haematoma. He underwent surgical evacuation and recovered without further complications.

Conclusion: This is the first reported case of rivaroxaban-induced retroperitoneal haematoma following elective anterior lumbar spine surgery. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for retroperitoneal haematoma in patients on NOACs who present with abdominal symptoms after anterior lumbar approaches.

Keywords: Anterior lumbar interbody fusion, Rivaroxaban, Retroperitoneal haematoma

## Abbreviations
- AF: Atrial fibrillation
- NOACs: Novel oral anticoagulants
- ALIF: Anterior lumbar interbody fusion

## Case Presentation
A 72-year-old man presented to his local emergency department 9 days after ALIF with collapse and severe left iliac fossa pain. He was nauseous, felt unsteady on his feet, and had one episode of non-bilious vomiting in transit. He appeared pale but was conscious and haemodynamically stable. The surgical wound was clean without dehiscence, but there was significant flank swelling and ecchymosis. His haemoglobin was 101 mg/dl and international normalised ratio (INR) was 2.5 on admission.

Past medical history included hypertension, atrial fibrillation (AF), a 3.5-cm abdominal aortic aneurysm, and bilateral common iliac artery aneurysms. Medications included rivaroxaban 20 mg/day for thromboembolic risk reduction secondary to AF, and amlodipine, losartan, and bisoprolol for hypertension; he also took paracetamol and ranitidine regularly.

Preoperatively, he had a 6-year history of severe left-sided back and leg pain resistant to non-operative management. Plain radiographs and MRI showed L5/S1 spondylolytic spondylolisthesis, for which he underwent ALIF. He was instructed to stop rivaroxaban 2 days prior to surgery, and he restarted it on the third postoperative day [1-3]. At re-admission (postoperative day 9), his last rivaroxaban dose was 24 hours earlier. Repeat blood tests showed haemoglobin 77 mg/dl, INR 1.8, and blood gases consistent with lactic acidosis. He received 5 units of packed red blood cells and prothrombin complex concentrate (Octaplex), and a decision was made to arrange transfer to a complex spine centre.

## Diagnostic Imaging
Given his vascular history, recent surgery, and NOAC use, a CT angiogram was obtained. It revealed a 25-cm haematoma displacing the left kidney superiorly and extending into the groin inferiorly. On arrival at our institution, repeat CT showed an enlarging haematoma extending posteriorly to the left kidney along the left hemi-abdomen to the pelvis, with a diameter of 31 cm. There was no evidence of rupture of known aneurysms and no discrete bleeding vessel was identified despite contrast enhancement. A working diagnosis of rivaroxaban-induced retroperitoneal haematoma was made.

## Literature Review
To our knowledge, this is the first report of rivaroxaban-induced retroperitoneal haematoma following an anterior approach to the lumbar spine. Retroperitoneal haematoma post-ALIF has been reported [4,5], but sources were either unidentified or due to inferior epigastric artery injury rather than anticoagulant administration. Anticoagulant-induced spontaneous retroperitoneal haematomas have been described [6,7], largely with warfarin, heparin, and low molecular weight heparin. Postoperative and spontaneous spinal epidural and subdural haematomas have been reported in patients on rivaroxaban [8], and these events are known complications when combined with spinal or neuraxial anaesthesia [9].

Operative vascular injury is a feared complication of the anterior lumbar approach; most present intraoperatively and can be addressed. Postoperative ligature loosening can cause abdominal wall and retroperitoneal haematomas, and exploration often reveals a discrete bleeding vessel. It is important to recognize haematoma formation as an independent haemorrhagic risk in patients on NOACs, where discrete bleeding vessels may not be encountered on re-exploration.

## Rationale for Management of Orthopaedic Patients on Rivaroxaban
Institutional guidelines are based on the summary of product characteristics for Xarelto (rivaroxaban) [2] and the NHS Devon formulary [3]. Despite adherence to current evidence-based guidance, this patient developed an anticoagulant-associated complication. There was no renal insufficiency and no concurrent use of CYP3A4 or P-glycoprotein inhibitors that would alter rivaroxaban elimination. Bleeding risk must be considered case by case; high-risk procedures may require a longer interruption interval. The rapid onset and relatively short half-life of NOACs negates the need for bridging anticoagulation (as is required with warfarin). Although our patient did not have particularly high thromboembolic risk, bridging anticoagulation during the surgical period may rarely be indicated.

## Treatment
After medical optimization, the patient underwent exploration and evacuation of the retroperitoneal haematoma under general anaesthesia via the original left retroperitoneal approach. A large, partially organized haematoma was found extending to the paracolic gutter and presacral space. All clotted blood was removed, and previously opened tissue planes were explored; no discrete active bleeding was identified. One large-bore suction drain was placed in the retroperitoneal space, which was irrigated with 6 L of normal saline.

Chemical thromboembolic prophylaxis was withheld for 72 hours postoperatively, consistent with institutional spinal surgery protocols. Cardiology consultation was obtained regarding resumption of NOACs and anti-embolic prophylaxis in the context of AF. Mechanical prophylaxis with thromboembolic stockings and intermittent pneumatic calf compression was used. Following cardiology review and in accordance with the patient’s wishes, NOACs were not restarted. He was discharged 2 days after re-exploration without further complications.

## Outcome and Follow-up
At 6-month follow-up, the patient reported complete resolution of leg and back pain, and plain radiographs demonstrated satisfactory implant positioning. He reported residual discomfort in the left iliac fossa and groin, possibly related to retroperitoneal haematoma stretch of cutaneous nerves in the posterior abdominal wall.

## Conclusion
With the increasing use of rivaroxaban in patients with AF, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for haemorrhagic complications after surgery. This case highlights the potential for retroperitoneal haematoma in such patients, in whom a discrete bleeding vessel may not be identified. We recommend placement of a suction drain in the retroperitoneal space in these patients. Consider stopping NOACs longer than current guidelines suggest when thromboembolic risk is sufficiently low to permit this. The availability of effective reversal agents for NOACs would greatly aid perioperative management and warrants further research.
