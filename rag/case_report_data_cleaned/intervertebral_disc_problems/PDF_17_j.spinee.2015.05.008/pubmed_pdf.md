# Dorsal transdural migration of a sequestered intradural lumbar disc

## Abstract

A 43-year-old man presented with low backache for three years and radicular pain in the left lower limb for one year. Neurological examination revealed a positive straight leg raising test at 60 degrees on the left. MRI showed a left L5-S1 paracentral sequestered disc prolapse compressing the thecal sac and nerve root. A contrast MRI was done to rule out an intradural tumour, with the sequestered fragment showing peripheral enhancement. At surgery, following a L5 laminectomy, a large sequestered disc fragment was found extradurally, just beneath the ligamentum flavum. Part of this fragment had to be extracted from within the thecal sac through a large defect in the dorsal dura, which the fragment had created. There was dense fibrosis around the roots within the thecal sac, likely sealing off any possible cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak. A rent in the ventral dura was identified and repaired. Postoperatively, the patient's radicular pain subsided. 

## Introduction

Intradural lumbar disc herniations (ILDH) are rare, accounting for less than 0.5% of all herniated discs. The commonly affected levels are L4–L5 (55%), L3–L4 (16%), and L5–S1 (10%). The average age of occurrence is between 50 and 60 years, with a male preponderance. Common presentations include cauda equina syndrome or radiculopathy simulating an intradural tumour. Theories for ILDH include congenital fusion or the formation of adhesions between the posterior longitudinal ligament and the ventral dura due to local inflammatory reactions, leading to perforation of the ventral dura and preventing lateral migration of the disc fragment.

## Methods

A contrast MRI scan was performed to differentiate the herniated disc from discitis or tumour. The presence of circumferential contrast enhancement, as seen in our case, raised the possibility of pathologies like an abscess or epidural metastasis. Optimal treatment includes laminectomy and durotomy, careful dissection of the nerve roots, removal of all intradural ruptured disc material, and repair of the dural defect to prevent CSF leakage.

## Results

In this case, the long-standing sequestered disc fragment likely induced a chronic inflammatory erosive process in both the ventral and dorsal dura. The arachnoiditis-like appearance seen intraoperatively, with no CSF leak from within the thecal sac, supports this hypothesis. 

## Discussion

No previously reported cases of ILDH describe the dorsal migration of an intradural disc fragment in the manner observed in this report. The fragment's chronicity and inflammatory effects may explain the dorsal transdural migration. Additionally, the repair of the dural defects and the removal of the disc material were crucial to resolving the radicular pain in this patient.

## Conclusion

Dorsal transdural migration of an intradural lumbar disc is a rare and novel occurrence, as demonstrated by this case. The proper imaging and surgical techniques, including laminectomy, durotomy, and dural repair, are essential in managing such cases effectively.
