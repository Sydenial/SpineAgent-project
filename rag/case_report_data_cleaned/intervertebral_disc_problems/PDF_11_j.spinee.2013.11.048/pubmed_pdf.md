# Revision Surgery After Cervical Laminoplasty: Report of Five Cases and Literature Review

## Abstract
Background context: Revision surgery after laminoplasty is rarely performed, and there are few reports of this procedure in the English literature.  
Purpose: To evaluate the reasons why patients underwent revision surgery after laminoplasty and to discuss methods of preventing the need for revision surgery. A literature review with comparative analysis between previous reports and present cases was also performed.  
Study design: Case report and literature review.  
Patient sample: Five patients who underwent revision surgery after laminoplasty.  
Outcome measures: Diagnosis was based on preoperative computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging findings. Neurologic findings were evaluated using the Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA) score.  
Methods: A total of 237 patients who underwent cervical laminoplasty for cervical spondylotic myelopathy from 1990 to 2010 were reviewed. Patients with ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL), renal dialysis, infection, tumor, or rheumatoid arthritis were excluded. Five patients who underwent revision surgery for symptoms of recurrent myelopathy or radiculopathy were identified, and their clinical courses and radiological findings were retrospectively reviewed.  
Results: The average interval from the initial surgery to revision surgery was 15.0 years (range 9–19). The patients were four men and one woman with an average age at the time of the initial operation of 49.8 years (range 34–65). Four patients developed recurrent myelopathy after their initial surgery for the following reasons: adjacent segmental stenosis after inadequate opening of the lamina with degenerative changes, and trauma after inadequate opening of the lamina. One patient developed new radiculopathy because of foraminal stenosis secondary to osteoarthritis at the Luschka and zygapophyseal joints. All patients experienced resolution of their symptoms after revision surgery.  
Conclusions: Revision surgery after laminoplasty is rare. Inadequate opening of the lamina is an important reason for revision. Degenerative changes after laminoplasty may also necessitate revision. Surgeons should be aware of degenerative changes that can cause neurologic deterioration after laminoplasty.

## Introduction
Since open-door laminoplasty was first reported by Hirabayashi et al. [1], this technique has been widely used for cervical decompression in patients with compressive cervical myelopathy involving three or more levels. Cervical myelopathy resulting from cervical spondylosis or OPLL is commonly treated by laminoplasty. The aim of laminoplasty is to expand the spinal canal while preserving the posterior structures of the cervical spine, thereby achieving stability and preventing postlaminectomy membrane formation. Although some patients may experience short-term postoperative symptoms such as C5 nerve root palsy [2] or axial pain [3], laminoplasty for compressive myelopathy resulting from cervical spondylosis or OPLL generally has stable results with good long-term outcomes [4,5]. Few reports have focused on mid- and long-term revision surgery after cervical laminoplasty. We reviewed patients who underwent revision surgery after laminoplasty to evaluate reasons for reoperation and to discuss prevention strategies, along with a literature review.

## Methods
We reviewed all patients who underwent revision surgery after cervical laminoplasty between 1990 and 2010. Patients with OPLL, renal dialysis, trauma at the time of the initial surgery, tumor, rheumatoid arthritis, and infection were excluded.

### Surgical Techniques
Two bilateral open-door laminoplasty techniques were used:
- Bilateral open-door laminoplasty with interpositional bone grafting.
- Bilateral open-door laminoplasty without interpositional bone grafting, using sutures placed between the ligamentum flavum and the facet capsule to maintain canal expansion.

Patients with cervical spondylotic myelopathy were randomly assigned to one of these procedures.

From 237 laminoplasty patients (1990–2010), five underwent revision surgery. Follow-up was 2 years or longer in 85.2% (202/237). Four patients underwent revision for recurrent myelopathy (revision laminoplasty or laminectomy), and one for new-onset radiculopathy (foraminotomy). Clinical records and imaging (X-ray, CT, MRI) were retrospectively reviewed. Recorded variables included age, sex, interval between initial and revision surgery, neurologic deficit, surgical technique, and reason for revision.

### Case 1
A man with upper and lower limb numbness and gait disturbance underwent C3-C7 laminoplasty at 34 years of age. His symptoms resolved after surgery. Fifteen years later, after a traffic accident, he gradually developed recurrence of upper and lower limb numbness and gait disturbance. CT myelography showed inadequate opening of the lamina and canal stenosis at C5-C6. He underwent repeat C5-C6 laminoplasty.

### Case 2
A man with upper limb numbness and dysfunction underwent C4-C6 laminoplasty at 65 years of age. Ten years later, he gradually developed inability to walk and urinary dysfunction. MRI showed adjacent canal stenosis at C3-C4 and C6-C7. He underwent laminoplasty at C3 and C7.

### Case 3
A woman with upper and lower limb numbness underwent laminoplasty at 57 years of age with symptom resolution. Eighteen years later, she developed numbness of both upper limbs and clumsiness. MRI showed cervical stenosis at C6-C7 due to inadequate opening of the lamina and buckling of the ligamentum flavum. She underwent revision laminectomy.

### Case 4
A man with upper limb numbness and muscle weakness underwent C3-C7 laminoplasty at 54 years of age. Sixteen years later, he gradually developed trunk and lower limb numbness and became unable to walk. MRI showed adjacent canal stenosis at T1-T2. He underwent laminectomy at T1.

### Case 5
A man with upper and lower limb numbness and inability to walk without a cane underwent C3-C7 laminoplasty at 39 years of age, with symptom resolution. Sixteen years later, he developed numbness of the right forearm and thumb. Symptoms did not improve with conservative treatment, and he underwent C4-C5 and C5-C6 foraminotomy.

## Discussion
Over the past decade, many spine surgeons have reported stable results with good clinical outcomes after laminoplasty. We reviewed five patients who underwent revision surgery after laminoplasty, a scenario rarely reported.

### Case Selection
This study focused on revision after laminoplasty for cervical spondylotic myelopathy. Patients with OPLL have different characteristics and may have different reasons for revision. For example, Hori et al. [6] reported progression of OPLL thickness in 21.8% of 55 patients who underwent laminoplasty with at least 5 years of follow-up. Revision surgery in patients with OPLL should therefore be studied separately.

### Reasons for Revision Surgery
Revisions were performed for recurrent myelopathy or new-onset radiculopathy. Following Liu et al. [7], reasons for post-laminoplasty symptom recurrence can be grouped into technique-related issues, inadequate treatment, and disease progression. Our categorization of etiologies of failure is as follows:

- Technique-related issues (early onset)
  - Complications of laminoplasty: C5 motor paresis; hematoma; dislocation of grafted bone
  - Surgical technique: cord compression due to lamina closure; nerve root or cord compression due to hinge fracture
- Inadequate treatment (residual preoperative symptoms)
  - Residual foraminal stenosis
  - Large anterior cord compression
  - Cervical kyphosis (local or global)
- Disease progression (late onset)
  - Within laminoplasty levels: spondylosis; OPLL; herniated disc; buckling of ligamentum flavum
  - Adjacent segment disease: facet arthrosis (zygapophysis or Luschka); herniated disc; osteophytic or spondylotic change; OPLL; buckling of ligamentum flavum; junctional kyphosis

Two patients in our series were technique-related (inadequately opened laminae). Three were due to disease progression (adjacent canal or foraminal stenosis). None were due to inadequate initial treatment, as all had symptom resolution after the index surgery.

### Prevention of the Need for Revision Surgery

#### Technique-Related Issues
Two patients had inadequately opened laminae and required revision because of lamina closure. The reported incidence of lamina closure after laminoplasty ranges from 1.5% to 34% [4,8–10]. Definitions vary (eg, a 4-mm decrease in anteroposterior canal diameter has been proposed [8]), which likely contributes to the wide range. Prior reports largely concern single-door techniques and include both asymptomatic and symptomatic cases; the incidence after bilateral open-door laminoplasty remains unclear. Techniques to reduce lamina closure include interpositional bone grafting, mini-plate implantation, and suture anchors [11–15]. In our series, no patients who received interpositional bone grafting required revision, suggesting potential benefit. However, closure has been reported even with suture anchors [9], and further research is needed.

#### Inadequate Treatment (Residual Symptoms)
Coexistent foraminal stenosis, marked anterior cord compression due to osteophytes, disc abnormalities, and cervical kyphosis may contribute to residual symptoms [7]. Although our series did not include patients with inadequate initial treatment, such cases can be difficult to diagnose postoperatively.

#### Disease Progression
Three patients experienced neurologic deterioration due to degenerative changes. One developed radiculopathy 16 years after the initial surgery; careful neurologic examination was key because the wide canal after laminoplasty led to an initial underappreciation of radiculopathy. His symptoms resolved after foraminotomy. Two patients had recurrent myelopathy due to adjacent canal stenosis. In one, initial laminoplasty was at C4-C6, and new stenoses occurred at C3-C4 and C6-C7 with buckling of the ligamentum flavum; range of motion at C4-C6 was only 2 degrees after the initial surgery, suggesting increased mechanical stress at adjacent segments. In another, initial laminoplasty was at C3-C7, and new stenosis occurred at T1-T2 with buckling of the ligamentum flavum; range of motion at C6-C7 was 0 degrees before revision, again suggesting stress concentration. Few reports describe adjacent canal stenosis after laminoplasty [17–19]. Proposed mechanisms include biomechanical changes due to lamina elevation or unintended fusion from drilling near facet joints. Intraoperative assessment in one of our cases showed facet ankylosis with markedly reduced motion from C4 to C6. Careful attention to preventing intersegmental fusion may help mitigate adjacent-level degeneration.

### Revision Techniques
Procedure selection should be based on the compression location, patient condition, cervical sagittal alignment, and surgeon preference. In this series, all revisions used the posterior approach because compression was posterior/foraminal. Others have used anterior cervical decompression and fusion with a fibular strut when multilevel anterior compression and instability were present [16].

### Limitations
This study has a small sample size; revision after laminoplasty is rare, making large series difficult. A multicenter study would be needed to better evaluate this complication. The study was retrospective, and some patients were lost to follow-up; some revisions may have occurred elsewhere, preventing precise calculation of revision rates.

## Conclusion
Revision surgery after cervical laminoplasty is uncommon. The primary factors associated with revision were inadequate opening of the lamina and degenerative changes, including adjacent segment disease. Interpositional bone grafting may help prevent late canal re-narrowing. Awareness of these factors and meticulous technique may reduce the need for revision surgery.
