# Management of Unusual Atlantoaxial Dislocation

## Abstract
The aim of this study was to describe the successful treatment of one posterior atlantoaxial dislocation without fracture and to review the relevant literature. Posterior atlantoaxial dislocation without fracture of the odontoid process is extremely rare. Management of these patients is still unknown. A 58-year-old man with incomplete quadriplegia was treated surgically with posterior atlantoaxial pedicle screws internal fixation and fusion after closed reduction. The patient had complete recovery of neurologic deficit, and bony fusion of the atlantoaxial joint was confirmed on the follow-up computed tomography taken 3 months after posterior fixation. To our knowledge, no case of posterior atlantoaxial dislocation with neurologic deficit has been previously reported in English medical literature. We describe a rare case and present the treatment procedure.

## Introduction
Posterior atlantoaxial dislocation without fracture of the odontoid process is extremely rare; only 12 cases have been reported in the literature. The management of such cases remains controversial. This report describes a rare instance of posterior atlantoaxial dislocation with neurologic deficit caused by a traffic accident. The patient was treated with closed reduction followed by posterior pedicle screw fixation.

## Case Report
A 58-year-old man was admitted with a 3-day history of incomplete quadriplegia, which manifested immediately after a traffic accident. Neurologic examination revealed paresthesia below the shoulders and decreased muscle strength of the arms and legs. The patient complained of significant neck pain and stiffness, with his neck skewed to the right. Initial plain radiographs of the cervical spine showed posterior dislocation of the atlas with respect to the axis, and soft tissue swelling was observed around the neck.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated compression of the spinal cord, although no intramedullary cord signal abnormalities were found at the level of the posterior atlantoaxial dislocation (PAAD). The MRI showed abnormal signal intensity from C1 to C5 vertebrae on both T2-weighted and T1-weighted images. Axial computed tomography (CT) scans confirmed the odontoid peg was ventral to the anterior arch of the atlas, with the anterior arch of the atlas intact.

The patient was initially treated with occipital-mandibular leash traction, starting at 3 kg and gradually increasing to 6 kg under fluoroscopy. The atlas was repositioned over the axis during this procedure. Reduction of the dislocation was confirmed by subsequent radiographs. The procedure was performed without anesthesia, and the patient reported sensory changes during the process, without any neurological deterioration. Pain relief was immediate after reduction. Halo-vest fixation was applied in preparation for posterior surgery under local anesthesia.

The patient showed significant improvement over the following 11 days. Neurologic examination revealed complete recovery of muscle strength and sensation. Two weeks after halo-vest fixation, the patient complained of neck pain, and fluoroscopy revealed instability of the atlantoaxial joint. A posterior midline approach was then used to perform pedicle screw internal fixation for atlantoaxial arthrodesis. Postoperative imaging revealed satisfactory alignment, and a solid bony union of the atlantoaxial joint was achieved. The patient remained asymptomatic with no neck pain at the 10-month follow-up.

## Discussion
The atlantoaxial joint is limited by the C1-C2 articulation, transverse ligament, and alar ligaments. Posterior atlantoaxial dislocation (PAAD) without fracture is a rare injury, typically caused by high-energy trauma such as a traffic accident. In our case, the injury resulted from a traffic accident where the patient was struck from behind, and the cervical spinal muscles were relaxed at the time of impact. Although PAAD without fracture often does not cause significant neurologic deficits, this case presented with incomplete quadriplegia, making it an exceptional instance.

The mechanism of PAAD is typically a high-grade hyperextension with varying amounts of distraction. Several studies suggest that the dislocation can cause severe spinal cord distraction, leading to immediate death. The recovery of our patient was aided by the gradual reduction of the dislocation using closed cervical traction, a technique that should be considered as the first-line approach, especially for patients with neurologic deficits.

## Conclusion
We presented a rare case of posterior atlantoaxial dislocation with neurologic deficit. The use of closed reduction followed by posterior pedicle screw fixation proved to be an effective treatment strategy. It is crucial to carry out reduction carefully and evaluate the patient's condition comprehensively to avoid further neurologic injury.

## Key Points
- This is the first case illustrating a rare instance of posterior atlantoaxial dislocation with neurologic deficit.
- Complete recovery of quadriparesis was achieved following reduction, and bony fusion of the atlantoaxial joint was confirmed after posterior fixation.
- The C1-C2 level is critical for managing atlantoaxial dislocation, and careful treatment is essential for a favorable outcome.
