# Risk Factors for Rapidly Progressive Neurological Deterioration in Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy

## Abstract

**Study Design.** A retrospective single-center study.  
**Objective.** This study sought to clarify the risk factors and to evaluate the surgical outcome in patients with rapidly progressive cervical spondylotic myelopathy (rp-CSM).  
**Summary of Background Data.** Cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) is a degenerative spine disease that typically presents with slowly progressive myelopathy. Some patients, however, show rapidly progressive neurological deterioration (especially gait disturbances) without any trauma. At present, there is little information about this condition.  
**Methods.** We studied 71 consecutive CSM patients (52 men, 19 women) with a mean age of 67.1 years; the follow-up period was 1 year. Patients were divided into two groups: rp-CSM and chronic CSM (c-CSM). The Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score and various clinical differences, including age, sex, comorbidity, the waiting period from symptomatic onset to surgery, cervical range of motion, and intramedullary MR T2-hyperintensity were analyzed. Independent risk factors were determined using logistic regression analysis.  
**Results.** Eighteen of 71 patients (25.4%) were diagnosed with rp-CSM. There were no significant differences between the two groups with regard to age, sex, or cervical range of motion. In the rp-CSM group, the preoperative upper/lower extremity motor function and bladder function were worse, and the waiting period for surgery was shorter (rp-CSM 1.2 mo, c-CSM 25.7 mo). Patients with rp-CSM more frequently had a history of cardiovascular event (CVE) (rp-CSM 44.4%, c-CSM 15.1%) and presented with MR T2-hyperintensity (rp-CSM 94.4%, c-CSM 58.5%), especially at the C4/5 disc level. Independent risk factors were a history of CVE (odds ratio $=4.7$) and MR T2-hyperintensity (odds ratio $=12.5$). The rp-CSM group showed better neurological recovery after decompression surgery (JOA recovery rate: rp-CSM 64.5%, c-CSM 40.7%).  
**Conclusion.** A history of CVE and MR T2-hyperintensity were risk factors for rp-CSM. Despite rapid neurological deterioration, rp-CSM patients showed good neurological recovery after surgery, indicating that rp-CSM is a reversible condition.  
**Key words:** cardiovascular event; cervical spondylotic myelopathy; gait disturbance; intramedullary MR T2-hyperintensity; laminoplasty; magnetic resonance imaging; microvascular insufficiency; rapidly progressive neurological deterioration; spinal cord edema; spinal cord ischemia.

## Introduction

Cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) is an age-related degenerative spine disease and is believed to be a slowly progressive disorder (chronic CSM [c-CSM]). In the natural course of activities of daily living, the condition gradually declines over the long term, and subjects with mild functional impairment can be treated conservatively. [1] However, some patients with CSM show rapidly progressive neurological deterioration, particularly with respect to gait disturbance and difficulty in walking. Although numerous studies have reported the pathogenesis of CSM, there is little information available on the clinical characteristics of rapidly progressive CSM (rp-CSM), a subtype within the CSM spectrum.

The leading pathologies of CSM reportedly include spinal cord compression, dynamic factors, and subsequent ischemia of the spinal cord. [2] Compression of the spinal cord within a narrow spinal canal can cause mechanical neural injury and poor blood flow, resulting in spinal cord ischemia and edema. [3–5] We therefore hypothesized that factors affecting spinal cord blood flow and ischemia might contribute to diversity in the pathophysiology of CSM, resulting in the CSM spectrum.

The purpose of this study was to clarify the characteristics and factors associated with rp-CSM.

## Materials and Methods

### Definition of Rapidly Progressive Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy

In this study, rp-CSM was defined as previously reported by Morishita et al. [6] In brief, patients with rp-CSM had difficulty maintaining a standing posture or walking without support within 4 weeks of symptomatic onset due to rapidly progressive neurological deterioration.

### Study Population

This retrospective, single-center study consisted of 71 consecutive patients diagnosed with CSM who underwent surgery between January 2008 and December 2015. There were 52 men and 19 women with a mean age of 67.1 years (range, 38–89 yr). We focused on patients with no evident history of trauma. Patients with a history of any spinal surgery were excluded. All patients demonstrated long tract signs such as clumsy hands, gait disturbance, and deep tendon hyperreflexia in the lower limbs on admission.

According to the definition, patients were divided into an rp-CSM group and a c-CSM group. Overall, 18 of 71 CSM patients (men, $n=16$; women, $n=2$; mean age, 69.2 yr) were diagnosed with rp-CSM. None of the patients with rp-CSM could maintain a standing posture or walk without support. They had no obvious episodes of trauma before deterioration.

Clinical factors evaluated included age, sex, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, kidney disease, and history of cardiovascular event [CVE]), and the waiting period from symptomatic onset of myelopathy to surgery (Table 1). In this study, CVE included myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke.

This study was approved by our institutional review board, and informed consent was obtained from all patients.

### Outcome Assessment

Neurological status (including motor and sensory functions of the upper and lower limbs and bladder function) was evaluated preoperatively and at 12 months postoperatively using the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) scoring system. [7,8]

### Imaging Evaluation

We assessed the affected segmental level by examining intramedullary hyperintensity lesions on magnetic resonance T2-weighted images using a 3.0-T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) system. The preoperative cervical range of motion (ROM) was evaluated using flexion and extension sagittal radiographs. [9]

### Operative Procedure

Open-door cervical laminoplasty was performed for all patients. The C4–6 laminae were opened en bloc with C7 rostral-side and C3 caudal-side laminotomy or C3 laminectomy to preserve insertion of the deep extensor musculature into the C2 spinous process. The insertion of the nuchal ligament into the C7 spinous process was preserved to maintain cervical lordotic alignment after surgery. Cervical collar placement lasted 1 week after surgery. [10,11]

### Statistical Analyses

Univariate analyses compared postoperative outcomes between groups (JOA score) and clinical/imaging findings including age, sex, comorbidity (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, kidney disease, history of CVE), waiting period to surgery, cervical ROM, intramedullary MR T2-hyperintensity, and affected segmental level.

Fisher’s exact probability tests compared sex, comorbidity, and MR T2-hyperintensity. The Mann–Whitney U test compared JOA score, age, waiting period, and cervical ROM. Multivariate logistic regression determined factors associated with rp-CSM. Variables with significant differences in univariate analysis were entered into multivariate analysis, with a predefined inclusion threshold of $P \le 0.10$. Statistical significance was set at $P < 0.05$. Analyses were conducted using EZR (version 1.36). [12]

## Results

Eighteen of 71 patients were diagnosed with rp-CSM (25.4%). There were no significant differences between groups regarding age, sex, or cervical ROM (Table 1). No clinical instabilities were identified based on prior criteria (>3.5 mm dynamic translation or angulation greater than that in the adjacent segment). [6,13]

In the rp-CSM group, preoperative motor functions of the upper/lower extremities and bladder function (JOA domains) were worse ($P<0.01$), and the waiting period from onset of symptoms to surgery was shorter (rp-CSM $1.2\pm0.8$ mo vs. c-CSM $25.7$ mo; $P\le 0.001$) than in the c-CSM group.

Univariate analyses showed that rp-CSM patients more frequently had a history of CVE (rp-CSM 38.9% vs. c-CSM 15.1%; $P=0.019$) and MR T2-hyperintensity (rp-CSM 94.4% vs. c-CSM 58.5%; $P=0.004$), especially at the C4/5 disc level (Table 1).

Multivariate logistic regression showed that a history of CVE (odds ratio [OR] $=4.7$, 95% confidence interval $=1.3$–$17.5$, $P=0.021$) and MR T2-hyperintensity (OR $=12.5$, 95% confidence interval $=1.5$–$106.0$, $P=0.020$) were independently associated with rp-CSM (Table 2).

All patients underwent open-door laminoplasty. The rp-CSM group showed better neurological recovery after decompression surgery than the c-CSM group: the 1-year JOA recovery rate was higher in rp-CSM (64.5% vs. 40.7%; $P=0.001$) (Table 1).

Regardless of anticoagulant therapy, there were no critical adverse events (e.g., postoperative hematoma, stroke, myocardial infarction) in patients with a history of CVE.

## Case Presentation

A 75-year-old man developed progressively worsening difficulty walking without any trauma and became unable to walk without support after 2 weeks. He also reported bilateral clumsiness of the hands and paresthesia in the left arm. He had been diagnosed with cervical radiculopathy and treated conservatively for 1 year at a private clinic. He was also treated with aspirin for a history of cerebral infarction.

On admission, cervical MRI showed spinal cord compression and T2-hyperintensity at the C4/5 disc level with edema below the C4 level, probably due to spinal cord ischemia (Figure 1A). Differential diagnoses of progressive neurological deterioration in older patients with CVE include spinal cord infarction and epidural hemorrhage due to anticoagulant therapy; however, his clinical course and MR images were incompatible with these diseases. We diagnosed acute-on-chronic CSM (rp-CSM) and performed cervical laminoplasty after a 1-week washout period for aspirin. His neurological function remarkably improved; he was able to walk again and use chopsticks at 1 week after surgery. There were no perioperative complications. Postoperative MRI revealed remission of spinal edema and localization of T2-hyperintensity at the C4/5 disc level without any T1-hypointensity lesions (Figure 1B).

## Discussion

Our study had two main findings. First, we identified independent risk factors for rp-CSM, including a history of CVE and MR T2-hyperintensity. Second, we showed that decompression surgery is effective and recommended for managing rapidly progressive neurological deterioration in CSM.

### The Spectrum and Subtypes of Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy

The natural history of CSM typically involves stepwise deterioration in motor and sensory functions with stationary plateau periods. In a previous study, 82% and 56% of patients with CSM were still conservatively followed without serious neurological deterioration at 5 and 10 years after initial treatment, respectively. In contrast, patients with CSM sometimes experience rapidly progressive neurological deterioration, particularly gait disturbance. [6,14] However, limited attention has been paid to this subtype, potentially hampering recognition of the clinical characteristics of rp-CSM.

### Incidence of Rapidly Progressive Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy

In our series, 25.4% of CSM patients demonstrated rapidly progressive neurological deterioration within 2–3 weeks of symptom onset without obvious trauma (e.g., falling). Morishita et al. reported 8 of 43 (18.6%) patients with CSM suffered rapid clinical deterioration. [6] Tachibana et al. reported acute exacerbation of cervical compression myelopathy; 19 of 59 (32.2%) received early surgery because of acute gait disturbance or acute paralysis (Table 3). [14] Prevalence has varied across single-center, small sample-size studies; further investigation is required.

### Clinical Characteristics and Pathophysiology of Rapidly Progressive Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy

Consistent with prior studies, [6,14] rp-CSM patients tended to show rapidly progressive gait disturbance rather than impaired hand dexterity. A large majority presented with MR T2-hyperintensity lesions, as reported by Morishita et al. [6] MR T2-hyperintensity may reflect edema, gliosis, demyelination, and myelomalacia. [15] The high frequency of MR T2-hyperintensity in rp-CSM may indicate spinal edema following acute spinal ischemia (Table 3).

Although prior studies have reported associations between MR T1 signal changes and neurological outcomes in CSM, [16,17] T1-hypointensity did not reflect poor prognosis in rp-CSM in our study. Nouri et al. reported MR T1-hypointensity as a guide for predicting worse outcomes in CSM but noted limitations: it is associated with greater disease severity, present in only ~20% of patients, and demonstrates only fair inter-rater agreement. [16,17] In our study, rp-CSM patients maintained walking ability before symptom onset (lower baseline severity) and underwent time-sensitive surgery aimed at restoring spinal cord blood flow and avoiding permanent cord injury, which may explain the lack of association between T1 changes and prognosis.

Aging is common in degenerative spinal disease, and Tachibana et al. discussed the association between an aged spinal cord and acute exacerbation of CSM. [14] Aging may reflect not only deterioration of spinal structure/stability but also medical comorbidities affecting spinal cord blood flow and durability. Regarding comorbidities, Dokai et al. reported diabetes was associated with poor surgical outcome in CSM, [18] and Karadimas et al. emphasized microvascular dysfunction as a component of diabetes pathophysiology affecting CSM natural history. [19] However, coexistence of diabetes and other comorbidities only suggests potential microvascular dysfunction and may not fully characterize microvascular status.

In the present study, rp-CSM patients tended to have a history of CVE. A history of CVE may suggest arteriosclerosis and endothelial dysfunction, potentially causing vasomotor dysfunction and insufficient blood flow in the cervical spinal cord. Such unstable blood flow may be less tolerant of blood pressure fluctuations and dynamic/static cord compression, ultimately resulting in acute spinal cord ischemia observed as MR T2-hyperintensity.

Biomechanically, both static and dynamic factors contribute to the CSM spectrum. Bednarik et al. prospectively investigated asymptomatic cervical spinal cord compression with discogenic or osteoligamentous spondylotic changes and found coexisting risk factors, such as symptomatic radiculopathy and MR T2-hyperintensity, were associated with development of myelopathy. [21] The clinical course of an rp-CSM patient with cervical radiculopathy and MR T2-hyperintensity in our study aligned with this evidence. Yue et al. reported an association between development of myelopathy and a congenitally narrow cervical canal standardized by vertebral body diameter (Torg–Pavlov ratio). [22] Regarding dynamic factors, Oshima et al. found large cervical ROM $(\ge 50^\circ)$ and segmental instability at the narrowest canal were related to progression of myelopathy in mild c-CSM. [1] Cervical instability remains controversial in rp-CSM: dynamic anterior slip [6] and spondylolisthesis [14] have been reported as associated factors. Morishita et al. hypothesized that dynamic stress in addition to static compression may play an important role in rapid deterioration. [6] Static and dynamic factors may interact in both c-CSM and rp-CSM.

We summarized the pathophysiology of rp-CSM as follows: acute spinal cord ischemia followed by rapidly progressive neurological deterioration in CSM may be caused by static/dynamic cord compression and microvascular insufficiency induced by a history of CVE (Figure 2).

### Surgical Outcome and Its Significance in Rapidly Progressive Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy

In rp-CSM, the preoperative total score and each JOA domain (motor/sensory functions of limbs and bladder function) were significantly lower than in c-CSM. Nevertheless, the JOA recovery rate at 1 year was significantly higher in rp-CSM.

A history of CVE may make spine surgeons hesitant to perform decompression surgery due to concern about perioperative complications (stroke, heart attack), postoperative hematoma, and paralysis related to anticoagulant therapy. In our study, no such perioperative complications occurred and outcomes were satisfactory. Surgically treated rp-CSM patients regained hand dexterity and walking ability at final follow-up.

Spinal ischemia should be treated in a time-sensitive manner, as evidence suggests rapid restoration of blood flow may improve outcomes. [19,23,24] Delayed decompression and prolonged ischemia can lead to excessive postoperative spinal blood flow and exacerbate ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), which has been associated with cytokine expression, microglial activation, astrogliosis, and poorer recovery. [25] Early surgical decompression can restore appropriate blood flow and avoid secondary spinal neuronal damage and death induced by inflammatory chemokines due to ischemia-reperfusion. [25,26]

Surgical treatment should therefore be considered for rp-CSM and performed without missing the treatment opportunity window (Table 3). Informed consent is important, as comorbidities on admission have been associated with increased risk of perioperative stroke in elective spinal surgery.

### Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the definition of rp-CSM was based on clinical features; biological evidence is still lacking. However, prior findings [6,19,23–26] conceptually support the pathophysiology described here. Second, we could not confirm whether MR T2-hyperintensity indicates temporary spinal edema or permanent neuronal changes over the long term. In this study, MR T2-hyperintensity was associated with rp-CSM presence but not poor surgical outcome. Future studies should investigate characteristics and patterns of MR signal changes in both T1 and T2 images to better understand rp-CSM. Third, the optimum timing of surgery for rp-CSM was difficult to determine. Although early decompression may be recommended, we did not dichotomize and compare early versus delayed surgery within rp-CSM. Early surgery was prioritized due to severity and rapid progression; thus, a shorter waiting period may not be linked to better outcome in this study. Finally, the cohort size was limited; larger studies are needed to confirm these results.

## Conclusion

We identified clinical characteristics of rp-CSM and noted that rp-CSM presents with rapidly progressive neurological deterioration over a few weeks. A history of CVE and intramedullary MR T2-hyperintensity were independent risk factors for rp-CSM. Our results also suggest that microvascular dysfunction and subsequent spinal edema may be important pathophysiological factors in rp-CSM.

In managing CSM, clinicians should take care not to miss rp-CSM. Despite rapid deterioration, rp-CSM patients showed good neurological recovery after surgery, indicating rp-CSM is a reversible condition. Decompression surgery should be planned after appropriate neurological and differential diagnosis. 

## Key Points

- Patients with rp-CSM had difficulty maintaining a standing posture or walking without support within 4 weeks of symptomatic onset due to rapidly progressive neurological deterioration.  
- Independent risk factors for rp-CSM were a history of CVE $(OR=4.7)$ and intramedullary MR T2-hyperintensity $(OR=12.5)$.  
- Decompression surgery is effective and recommended for managing rapidly progressive neurological deterioration in CSM because this condition is reversible.
