# Rupture of the spleen following thoracoscopic spine surgery in a patient with chronic pancreatitis

## Abstract

**Purpose**  
To highlight the perioperative risk of intracapsular haematoma of the spleen or splenic rupture during thoracoscopic spine surgery in patients with chronic pancreatitis.

**Methods**  
A 38-year-old patient with an L1 burst fracture (AO A3.3) underwent a standard thoracoscopic corpectomy and replacement of the vertebral body with an extendable vertebral body replacement 10 days after posterior instrumentation of T12–L2. The patient had a history of chronic abusive alcoholism with related diseases such as pancreatitis, followed by hemipancreatectomy. Six hours after surgery, the patient became hemodynamically unstable. An emergency CT scan revealed a splenic rupture, and an emergent splenectomy was performed.

**Results**  
After surgical treatment of the L1 burst fracture, a rupture of the spleen was detected. Immediate splenectomy was performed. At the 18-month follow-up, an unchanged stable position of the cage was observed on CT.

**Conclusions**  
Due to its proximity to the thoracolumbar junction, the spleen is vulnerable to injury during spine surgery. If the patient has undergone previous intraabdominal operations or chronic inflammation of the pancreas is present, special care of the spleen during the operation is necessary.

## Introduction

Video-assisted thoracoscopic (VATS) instrumentation of the thoracic and thoracolumbar spine is a well-known method for treating fractures of the spine. Previously reported intraoperative and postoperative complications include intercostal neuralgia, tension pneumothorax, hemothorax, and injuries of the lung and vessels [1–5]. Due to its proximity to the thoracolumbar junction, the spleen is vulnerable to injury during thoracoscopic spine surgery.

Overall, few reports of intraoperative injury of the spleen exist, with most described during colonoscopy and gastroscopy. To the best of our knowledge, only one case of splenic rupture following thoracoscopic anterior spine surgery has been reported [6]. In that case, the authors described an obese patient with a pathologic T12 fracture in whom the spleen was injured intraoperatively during incision of the diaphragm at its attachment.

## Case Report

### History

A 38-year-old man (height 1.78 m, weight 65 kg, BMI 20.5) was brought to a level 1 trauma center with pain in the thoracolumbar region. Detailed information about the accident and its circumstances could not be recalled due to alcohol intoxication. A CT scan showed an AO A3.3 burst fracture of L1; an initial splenic injury was excluded.

The patient had a history of chronic abusive alcoholism with related diseases such as pancreatitis, chronic gastritis, duodenitis, and steatosis hepatitis, without splenomegaly. Two years earlier, a hemipancreatectomy had been performed without further complications.

### Surgical Procedure and Postoperative Course

On the day of hospital admission, posterior instrumentation of T12–L2 was performed. A scheduled VATS procedure with partial corpectomy, implantation of a vertebral body replacement (VBR), and anterior instrumented fusion was conducted 10 days later. Thoracoscopy was performed through a standard left-sided approach without complications. Intraoperative visualization was good in the slim patient.

The diaphragm was retracted using a soft paddle, and the operation site was sufficiently exposed. No excessive force was required to retract the diaphragm. Partial corpectomy of L1 was performed, followed by anterior spondylodesis using a VBR and autologous bone, and instrumentation with an angle-stable plate. The operation time was 157 minutes, and intraoperative blood loss was 300 mL. No signs of hemodynamic instability or excessive bleeding were observed intraoperatively.

Six hours after surgery, the patient suddenly became progressively hemodynamically unstable, with a pulse rate of 130 beats per minute and blood pressure of 90/60 mmHg, accompanied by a decline in hemoglobin from 11.4 g/dL postoperatively to 8.4 g/dL. An abdominal CT angiography was immediately performed, revealing a large intracapsular hematoma of the spleen and minimal hemoperitoneum. An immediate splenectomy was therefore performed. During surgery, massive hemoperitoneum was observed, and the splenic rupture was found to be complete. No injury to the diaphragm was identified.

After splenectomy, the patient recovered rapidly. Postoperatively, four units of erythrocyte concentrates were administered. The patient was discharged from hospital 10 days after splenectomy.

Four weeks later, the patient presented with signs of systemic infection. A CT scan of the thorax revealed a pleural empyema. After an unsuccessful attempt at drainage, decortication with intrathoracic application of vacuum-assisted closure was required. Antibiotic prophylaxis was administered for 8 weeks, and the implant was left in place. At the 18-month follow-up after the primary surgery, the implant showed an unchanged and stable position.

## Discussion

Most cases of intraoperative or postoperative splenic rupture reported in the literature have occurred after endoscopic procedures, including colonoscopy [7, 8]. Morrison and Jacobs [9] reported a splenic lesion after laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication using a harmonic scalpel, although the pathomechanism remained unclear.

Splenic rupture after anterior spine surgery has primarily been described in open procedures. Heyworth et al. [10] reported splenic injury after a thoracoabdominal approach, suggesting that traction on the spleen or adjacent viscera caused avulsion of vessels in the suspensory ligaments. Similar mechanisms were reported by Hodge and Sin [11, 12].

Only one report describes splenic rupture following thoracoscopic spine surgery [6]. In that case, a morbidly obese patient required incision of the diaphragm and use of a retractor for visualization, likely necessitating increased pressure on the diaphragm.

In the present case, the patient’s physical condition was ideal for thoracoscopic spine surgery. However, his medical history included chronic alcohol abuse and chronic pancreatitis treated by hemipancreatectomy. Although no splenomegaly was present on preoperative imaging, the spleen appeared to be more vulnerable. Previous studies have reported atraumatic splenic hematomas and spontaneous ruptures in patients with acute or chronic pancreatitis [13–16]. The suspected mechanism involves extension of inflammatory processes from the pancreatic tail to the splenic hilum, resulting in perisplenitis [17]. In a prospective study of 500 patients with chronic pancreatitis, a 1.2% rate of atraumatic splenic rupture or intracapsular hematoma was reported [18].

## Conclusions

Iatrogenic injury of the spleen during thoracoscopic spine surgery is a rare complication. Despite the use of a large, soft paddle without hard edges to retract the diaphragm, splenic rupture occurred in this case. In patients with chronic pancreatitis, the spleen appears to be more vulnerable. Therefore, during thoracoscopic instrumentation of the thoracolumbar spine, splenic injury may occur more easily than in healthy individuals, and this risk should be discussed with the patient preoperatively.
