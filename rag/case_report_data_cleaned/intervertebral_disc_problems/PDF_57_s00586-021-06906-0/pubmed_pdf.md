# Anterior Artery Release, Distraction, and Fusion (ARDF) for Radiculopathy Caused by a Vertebral Artery Loop

## Abstract

Surgical techniques with anterior and posterior approaches have been described for the surgical treatment of vertebral artery (VA) loops once conservative treatments fail. We herein present a case treated with anterior artery release, distraction, and fusion (ARDF) and further provide an updated review of surgically managed VA loops in the subaxial spine.

**Case Description**: A 76-year-old female presented with a 6-year history of pulsating, shooting pain in her right arm radiating to the thumb. MRI revealed a VA loop compressing the right-sided C6 nerve root. A neurovascular decompression through ARDF, which led to loop straightening, was performed. The patient immediately improved postoperatively and remained pain-free for one year.

**Conclusion**: Neural irritation due to VA loop formation is a rare cause of cervical radiculopathy. While various surgical strategies exist, we believe that anterior and anterolateral approaches are the safest for yielding neurovascular decompression.

**Keywords**: ARDF, Vertebral Artery, Artery Loop, Neurovascular, Cervical Spine

## Introduction

Vertebral artery (VA) loop formation as an anatomic variation may occasionally cause neurovascular conflicts with adjacent cervical nerve roots [1]. Recognizing the abnormal course of the artery as a cause for these patients' radiculopathy can be challenging, as it may be overlooked on MRI. Management options for VA-related nerve root compression include conservative therapies and neurovascular decompression surgery [2]. We present a case of long-lasting painful C6 radiculopathy treated by anterior artery release, distraction, and fusion (ARDF), which led to loop straightening and removal of the radicular impingement. This case also provides an updated review of surgically managed VA loops in the subaxial spine.

## Case Report

A 76-year-old female presented with a 6-7 year history of worsening right-sided neck pain. Her pain was sharp, with intermittent shooting sensations radiating to her right arm towards the thumb. Symptoms were position-dependent, with exacerbation when lying flat. Lateral neck flexion to the affected side aggravated the pain, whereas flexion to the contralateral side reduced it. The patient had undergone multiple sessions of physiotherapy and acupuncture. Despite regular analgesic use (paracetamol) and fentanyl patches, her pain persisted. She was otherwise healthy, managing her daily duties independently. Her quality of life was significantly affected, reflected in a Neck Disability Index (NDI) of 30%. Clinical examination showed no sensory or motor deficits. X-rays revealed degenerative changes, including reduced intervertebral disc space at C5/6. MRI showed discrete foraminal narrowing at C5 and C6 but no clear stenosis. A CT-guided infiltration of the C6 nerve root provided no relief. On follow-up MRI, an atypical tortuous course of the VA with a loop at C5/6 near the neuroforamen was detected. CT angiography confirmed the abnormal course.

Given her severe pain, neurovascular decompression via ARDF was performed 18 months after her first visit to our spine unit.

## Surgical Technique

The patient was positioned supine under general anesthesia. An anterior approach was performed through a horizontal incision along the skin folds on the left side, similar to the approach for anterior cervical discectomy and fusion. The carotid sheath was retracted laterally, and the trachea and esophagus medially. The correct level was confirmed with intraoperative fluoroscopy. The right longus colli muscle was dissected off the vertebral bodies of C5 and C6. After temporary distraction over the pins, the intervertebral disc and posterior longitudinal ligament were removed. Under microscopic magnification, no nerve root compression was identified. Uncectomy was performed using a high-speed diamond burr. The VA loop was exposed and carefully separated from the nerve root with blunt dissection. With intervertebral distraction, the VA loop was straightened, resolving the neurovascular conflict. An 8-mm interbody cage (ACIS ProTi 360 Lordotic Standard, DePuy Synthes) was inserted to restore disc height and maintain the straightened course of the artery. Autograft bone from the removed uncinate process and demineralized bone matrix were used to promote fusion.

## Postoperative Course

Postoperatively, the patient reported immediate resolution of her radicular pain. The straightened course of the VA was confirmed with postoperative CT angiography. One year later, the patient remained pain-free, with her NDI score decreasing from 30% to 6%. Although some cage subsidence was noted on the one-year follow-up radiograph, the pulsatile symptoms had resolved. The remaining intervertebral disc height appeared to prevent recurrence of the neurovascular conflict.

## Discussion

### Vertebral Artery Anatomy and Variability

Detailed knowledge of the VA's anatomy is crucial for safe surgery in the subaxial spine. The VA course is divided into four segments: V1, V2, V3, and V4, with tortuosity occurring in 0.6% to 7.5% of the population [4,5]. Various types of tortuosity include straight, mild, loop formation, and loop migration. VA tortuosity can be congenital or acquired, often due to arterial hypertension or degenerative changes [6,9,10]. In this case, the VA loop formed due to severe disc narrowing, which led to overlengthening of the artery.

### Clinical Presentation and Characteristics

When symptomatic, VA loops often present as sharp, shooting, or pulsating radicular pain, similar to trigeminal neuralgia. VA loops are more common in females (1.2:1 ratio) and typically present in the fifth and sixth decades of life. The C4/5 segment is the most common site of loop formation, followed by C3/4 and C5/6, with a left-side preference [2].

### Diagnostic Workup

VA loops can be easily overlooked initially. Other causes of radiculopathy must be ruled out first, as most VA loops remain silent. Preoperative MRI should assess the VA's course in every cervical spine surgery to avoid injury. In patients with refractory shooting pain and no morphological correlate on MRI, further diagnostic workup, including CT angiography or magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), is recommended.

### Treatment Strategies

Conservative treatment is the first-line management for symptomatic VA loops. If conservative measures fail, several surgical options are available. Microvascular decompression is the primary surgical strategy, and ARDF, as shown in this case, can also effectively address the neurovascular conflict.

### Anterior Artery Release, Distraction, and Fusion (ARDF)

In contrast to previous reports, the neurovascular conflict in this case was resolved by straightening the VA loop through restoration of the intervertebral space between C5 and C6. One year postoperatively, cage subsidence was noted, but it had no clinical impact. Future considerations may include anterior plating to prevent distraction loss.

### Anterolateral MV Decompression by Loop Mobilization

An anterolateral approach is often used to mobilize the VA loop, allowing direct visualization and separation from the nerve root. Studies show promising results with this approach [1,7,8,11–16].

## Conclusion

VA loop formation is a rare but significant cause of cervical radiculopathy. Anterior and anterolateral approaches provide safe and effective neurovascular decompression. We document the first case using the ARDF technique for this condition.
