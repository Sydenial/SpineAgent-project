# Electrochemotherapy to Metastatic Spinal Melanoma: A Novel Treatment of Spinal Metastasis?

## Abstract
- Study Design: Preliminary report of a new antitumor treatment.
- Objective: To evaluate the effectiveness of electrochemotherapy as a novel treatment of spinal metastasis.
- Summary of Background Data: Electrochemotherapy (ECT) combines systemic bleomycin with locally delivered electric pulses. These pulses permeabilize cell membranes, enhance intracellular diffusion of bleomycin, and markedly increase its cytotoxicity. Previous clinical studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of ECT in several primary and metastatic solid tumors.
- Methods: Treatment planning defined electrode positioning and pulse parameters for four needle electrodes. A mini-open left L5 laminectomy enabled electrode placement. The patient was treated according to the Electrochemotherapy Protocol with bleomycin. Clinical efficacy was evaluated with a visual analog scale (VAS) for pain, Oswestry Disability Index 2.0 (ODI), Karnofsky Performance Scale (KPS), and Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST).
- Results: Follow-up was 48 months after ECT. No serious ECT-related adverse events or bleomycin toxicity were reported. Pain, ODI, and KPS improved. Histopathology at 3 months showed greater than 95% necrosis of the target lesion. Imaging suggested local stability after adjuvant radiotherapy at 6 months.
- Conclusion: This case represents, to our knowledge, the first test of ECT for spinal metastasis. ECT enabled successful local control of metastatic spinal melanoma. There is a strong scientific rationale supporting ECT as a novel treatment for spinal metastasis, regardless of histology.

## Introduction
Electroporation is a physical method in which high-voltage direct current electric pulses cause non-selective plasma membrane permeabilization, enabling intracellular delivery of molecules such as DNA, antibodies, enzymes, dyes, and drugs [1]. The use of electric pulses to facilitate chemotherapeutic drug delivery into tumor cells is termed electrochemotherapy (ECT) and was introduced by Okino et al. [2] and Mir et al. [3]. ECT efficacy is largely independent of tumor histology and is effective as a local therapy for many solid tumors, including malignant melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and adenocarcinoma. Pore formation in the cell membrane allows low-permeant chemotherapeutic agents such as bleomycin or cisplatin to enter cells, increasing toxicity up to 10,000 times for bleomycin and 80 times for cisplatin. Studies report high complete response rates of 70% to 90%, with cost-effectiveness and few local or systemic side effects [4,5].

ECT has been used clinically since 2005 and is in routine use across Europe (ESOPE) [5,6]. New indications have emerged, including applications in bone tumors [7]. Preclinical work at our institute demonstrated that electroporation can ablate bone cells without impairing osteogenic recovery [8,9]. Based on these findings and prior clinical success in other tumors, we report a case of metastatic spinal melanoma treated with ECT and present, to our knowledge, the first clinical case using ECT for spinal metastasis.

## Methods

### Clinical Data
A 51-year-old woman presented with recurrent epistaxis. Nasal endoscopy revealed a polypoid mass in the right nasal cavity originating from the inferior concha. Endoscopic excisional biopsy identified mucosal malignant melanoma. The patient subsequently underwent maxillectomy with turbinectomy and sphenoidectomy. Four years later, local recurrence was surgically treated and radiotherapy administered. One year after radiotherapy, she developed atypical severe low back pain (VAS 10). MRI showed a suspected lesion at L5 measuring 30 x 20 x 15 mm (Figure 1).

Percutaneous biopsy of the L5 vertebral body confirmed metastatic melanoma on histopathology and immunohistochemistry (Figure 2A). Oncologic scores were Karnofsky = 80%, Tokuhashi = 11, Tomita = 5, supporting a locoregional treatment at L5. There was no spinal instability and no neurological signs. ECT was offered as a local/palliative option to prevent rapid progression and avoid morbidity from extended resection. The patient consented.

The patient was monitored intraoperatively and for 48 hours post-ECT. Clinical and radiological evaluations were scheduled at 1 and 4 weeks and at 3-month intervals thereafter. Patient-reported outcomes included VAS, ODI 2.0, and KPS. Tumor response was assessed according to RECIST 1.1. A percutaneous vertebral biopsy was performed at 3 months. Local and systemic bleomycin toxicity was graded per CTCAE 4.0.

### Operation
Under general anesthesia in prone position, a posterior approach with left L5 laminectomy exposed the pedicle area while protecting the nerve root and dural sac. Four needle electrodes were placed around the L5 vertebral tumor under fluoroscopic and neuronavigation control (Figure 3).

### Bleomycin Administration
Bleomycin (Bristol-Myers Squibb, Princeton, NJ) was administered intravenously by rapid push over 30–45 s at a total dose of 26 mg, after connecting the electrodes and 8 minutes before pulse delivery. Administration followed ESOPE recommendations (10 or 15 mg/m^2) [10].

### Electrodes, Pulse Generator, and Pulse Parameters
Four custom stainless steel electrodes (1.8 mm diameter, sharpened, insulated) were connected to independently controlled outputs of the Cliniporator Vitae (IGEA, Carpi, Italy). The generator provides up to 3000 V, max current 50 A, delivering 8 rectangular pulses (rise time 1 s) of 100 s duration at 4 Hz [11]. Pulse delivery was monitored to assure effective electric field application (target current approximately 1.5 A). Current and voltage were measured and logged with precision better than 3% for delivery control and post-treatment evaluation. Pulses were delivered 8 minutes after i.v. bleomycin. The patient was partially curarized during pulse delivery.

### Electric Field Monitoring
An oscilloscope and standard current monitor recorded the pulses. Voltages were monitored across the outputs of the RIX instrument. The oscilloscope stored up to four digitized waveforms, which were saved and analyzed via a computer interface coupled to the Cliniporator Vitae.

## Results

### Tolerance of ECT
No significant hemodynamic or cardiologic changes occurred during ECT. Despite neuromuscular blockade, mild transient low-back muscle contractions occurred with each pulse and ceased immediately after each pulse. There were no local or systemic side effects. Postoperatively, the patient reported no unpleasant sensations and was discharged on postoperative day 3 with only mild incision-related pain and no neurological symptoms.

### Clinical Outcomes
Follow-up was 48 months after ECT. No serious ECT-related adverse events or bleomycin toxicity occurred. At 3 months, percutaneous biopsy demonstrated more than 95% necrosis (Figure 2B). FDG-PET/CT showed no lesion (Figure 4). At 6 months, FDG-PET/CT was positive and the patient reported recurrent back pain, interpreted as probable local recurrence. Radiotherapy was performed (10 sessions x 3 Gy = 30 Gy to L5). Thereafter, L5 tumor size remained stable and spine MRI revealed no new lesions through last follow-up.

Pain improved from VAS 10 preoperatively to 2 before radiotherapy at 6 months (P < 0.0001), and to 0 after radiotherapy at last follow-up (P < 0.0001). ODI decreased from 65 preoperatively to 9 before radiotherapy (P < 0.0001), and to 3 at last follow-up (P < 0.0001). KPS improved from 80 preoperatively to 100 by 6 months and remained 100 at last follow-up (P < 0.005). Despite the questionable local recurrence at 6 months, no subsequent local progression occurred after radiotherapy; per RECIST, the target lesion was stable disease.

## Discussion
Spinal metastases are the most frequent tumors of bone and of the spinal column; more than 90% of spinal tumors are metastatic. Management is challenging and includes medical therapy, surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation. Surgery is often palliative, aiming for pain control and maintenance of function and stability, and may be impractical for many patients [12-15].

Spinal metastasis from melanoma is uncommon and its natural history is not fully defined. In a review of 133 cases, Gokaslan et al. concluded that spinal melanoma metastasis is a late event with median survival of 4 months [16]. In this case, survival with local control extended to 48 months, prompting interest in ECT’s potential role across histologies.

ECT combines electric pulses with chemotherapeutics (bleomycin or cisplatin) that have intracellular targets and low membrane permeability, increasing drug uptake [2-5,17-21]. Once intracellular, drug-DNA interactions proceed rapidly and independently of tumor origin; electropermeabilization is a universal phenomenon, suggesting broad applicability [22]. Following ESOPE, bleomycin was selected given its up to 10,000-fold local toxicity enhancement with ECT [4,5]. Histology at 3 months confirmed more than 95% necrosis, an excellent response, as high necrosis rates correlate with better outcomes in bone tumors [23].

At 6 months, FDG uptake was detected on PET/CT. While PET is sensitive for staging and therapy control, FDG uptake is not tumor-specific and may reflect inflammation or post-treatment repair [24]. Radiotherapy was chosen as a safe option; no further local progression was observed.

Beyond direct cytotoxicity, ECT may exert additional effects, including immune activation and modulation of tumor blood flow [25-29]. ECT achieves high local objective response rates, particularly in melanoma (~80%) [30-32], with tissue-sparing healing by secondary intention and less scarring than excision. It preserves adjacent tissue function.

Treatment planning and electrode placement are critical. Miklavcic et al. reported the first clinical case of deep-seated tumor ECT based on numerical planning; suboptimal electrode positioning reduced effectiveness [17,35]. In this case, fluoroscopy and neuronavigation aided accurate placement.

ECT can be combined with radiotherapy. Bleomycin and cisplatin are radiosensitizers, and their radiosensitizing effects are enhanced when used in ECT [36,37]. Stereotactic radiosurgery is also an option for spinal metastases, even after prior irradiation [38,39].

Selecting therapy for metastatic spinal tumors depends on life expectancy and balancing risks with quality-of-life gains. In selected patients, ECT offers advantages: ease of administration, low cost, minimal toxicity (often mild local inflammation), high response rates, and compatibility with other therapies for local control alongside systemic treatment. ECT may serve as a palliative option for patients not candidates for resection and who have failed radiation and/or chemotherapy.

## Conclusion
ECT provided successful local control of metastatic spinal melanoma with improved pain and function and without significant toxicity. This case supports the scientific rationale for ECT as a novel and adjuvant treatment for spinal metastasis across histologies. Larger, well-designed randomized controlled studies with long-term follow-up are needed to confirm clinical efficacy.

## Key Points
- More than 90% of spinal tumors are metastatic; management is challenging for multidisciplinary teams.
- ECT uses electrical pulses to permeabilize cancer cell membranes and enhance cytotoxic drug penetration.
- Literature supports ECT as a novel, adjuvant local treatment across tumor histologies.
- This is, to our knowledge, the first reported case of a metastatic spinal tumor treated with ECT.
