# The Management of High-Grade Spondylolisthesis and Co-Existent Late-Onset Idiopathic Scoliosis

## Abstract
### Introduction
It is relatively common for a scoliosis deformity to be associated with a lumbar spondylolisthesis in adolescents (up to 48% of spondylolistheses). In the literature two types of curve have been described: “sciatic” or “olisthetic.” However, there is no consensus on how best to treat these deformities. Some authors advocate a single surgical intervention, where both deformities are corrected; others advocate treating them as separate entities. In this situation, it has been shown that the scoliosis will correct with treatment of the spondylolisthesis.

### Materials and Methods
We present a 12-year-old girl who had a concomitant high-grade spondylolisthesis and scoliosis. Her main complaints were low back pain and an L5 radiculopathy. We decided to treat the spondylolisthesis surgically, but observe the scoliosis rather than correcting them both surgically at the same sitting.

### Results
Although the immediate post-operative radiographs showed persistence of the scoliosis, 1-year follow-up demonstrated full resolution of the deformity. This young lady also had relief of her low back pain and leg pain following the surgery.

### Conclusion
There are no standard guidelines; therefore, we discuss the management of this difficult problem, exemplifying a case of a young girl who had high-grade spondylolisthesis along with a clinically non-flexible scoliosis treated at our institution. We demonstrate that it is safe to observe the scoliosis, even in high-grade spondylolistheses.

## Case Report
### Presentation
A 12-year-old girl presented with progressive low back pain and left-sided L5 radiculopathy. She flexed her knees and hips to maintain sagittal balance and tended to lean to her right in the coronal plane, which partially corrected when seated. Neurological examination was normal, although straight-leg raise was positive at 50 degrees for left radicular pain. Hip and knee examinations were normal.

Standing whole-spine radiographs (Fig. 1) demonstrated a grade IV spondylolisthesis (95%) at L5/S1 associated with a rigid thoracolumbar scoliosis and retroverted pelvis. There was significant coronal (10 cm) and sagittal imbalance. The scoliotic curve had two components: a lumbar curve from L2 to S1 measuring 44 degrees, and a compensatory thoracic curve from T6 to L1 measuring 36 degrees. The curve did not correct on clinical examination. There was significant rotation of the pedicles in L5, whereas the rest of the lumbar and thoracic spine did not show rotation (Fig. 2).

MRI was negative for spinal dysraphism, but there was compression of the L5 nerve roots as they exited through their foramina. The olisthesis partially reduced to grade III on MRI, indicating a mobile luxation.

While awaiting surgery, symptoms progressed to the point where she became wheelchair bound due to pain and gait instability.

## Historical Review, Epidemiology, Diagnosis, Pathology, Differential Diagnosis
High-grade spondylolisthesis in the paediatric age group has been associated with scoliosis in several case reports [1–4], with up to 48% of spondylolistheses of any type developing some scoliosis deformity [2]. The most common pattern is a lumbar curve, usually flexible. The optimal management of concomitant deformities remains debated [5–7]. Some recommend treating each disorder individually [5, 6], while others suggest they should be treated together as one pathology [7]. The difference in opinion becomes greater when the spondylolisthesis is high-grade and the associated scoliosis appears rigid.

The concomitant association of scoliosis with spondylolisthesis is traditionally attributed to:
- “Sciatic” scoliosis, where L5 nerve root entrapment leads to severe radiculopathy and altered posture to relieve nerve tension [7, 9, 10]. These curves are typically flexible and show no significant rotation.
- Rotational or “olisthetic” curves, arising from vertebral rotation due to differential slip of the vertebral body over an asymmetric pars defect [8, 10]. Here, scoliosis is intrinsically linked to the lysis and spondylolisthesis; thus, addressing the primary cause should lead to spontaneous improvement.

However, if the scoliosis is idiopathic and simply co-existent, the two need to be treated as separate surgical procedures.

Part of the observed non-flexibility may be attributed to low back pain and lumbar radiculopathy. Identification of the aetiology of the thoracic curve is more challenging; it could be compensatory, pain-related, or idiopathic. In this case, the curve did not correct on the operating table under anaesthesia, underlining the difficulty in labelling the aetiology. The case highlights the predicament in choosing optimal management when both conditions co-exist.

## Rationale for Treatment and Literature Review
Crostelli et al. [7] advocated that a scoliosis associated with spondylolisthesis should be treated together, based on the observation that they are often separate entities, with the spondylolisthesis exerting little influence on the aetiology of scoliosis. This was particularly true for large thoracic and thoracolumbar curves. Goldstein et al. [11] provided guidelines suggesting that thoracic curves can be observed, but lumbosacral scoliosis needs correcting at the time of spondylolisthesis surgery.

Our management differs. The intention was to avoid an additional procedure, given dissimilar opinions in the literature. This approach is supported by recent case reports [5, 6]. Zhou et al. [5] reported spontaneous resolution of scoliosis associated with high-grade spondylolisthesis without additional surgery for the scoliosis; their spondylolisthesis was asymptomatic and observed. Pneumaticos et al. [6] reported spontaneous resolution of scoliosis after surgical treatment of the spondylolisthesis alone. Based on these, we felt staging was safer and could prevent the need for major scoliosis correction; if the scoliosis did not resolve, surgery could be performed later.

There is also controversy in isolated spondylolisthesis regarding reduction versus fusion in situ. Some evidence suggests higher fusion rates and improved outcomes with reduction [12, 13]. Logically, if scoliosis is secondary to spondylolisthesis, correcting the slip may be necessary for scoliosis resolution. However, sagittal alignment does not improve significantly following reduction [14], arguing against the premise that improving one deformity corrects the other. Partial reduction may be sufficient for good outcomes [15]. The concern with in-situ fusion is neurological injury [12], though there are no comparative trials. We elected to correct the deformity because it was mobile and technically reducible, although anatomical reduction was not achieved.

## Procedure
The patient was positioned prone via a standard midline approach. Posterior decompression at L5/S1 was performed, and the L5 nerve roots were explored far laterally to ensure adequate release. Posterior instrumentation with bilateral pedicle screws from L4–S1 was performed. The left L5 pedicle was hypoplastic and not instrumented due to the risk of screw cut-out.

An L5/S1 discectomy was performed with bilateral disc space access. A sacral dome osteotomy was performed and TLIF completed. Reduction of the spondylolisthesis was attempted and maintained at a grade I olisthesis. Further reduction to neutral strained the screws (risking cut-out) and stretched the L5 nerve roots; therefore, further reduction was not pursued and a grade I deformity was accepted. The construct was augmented with S2 screws to maintain reduction.

## Outcome and Follow-Up
Post-operatively, the patient was ambulatory on day 2 without leg pain; however, the scoliosis and coronal imbalance persisted (Fig. 3). Immediate post-operative radiographs showed persistence of the lumbar scoliosis (37 degrees), with the thoracic curve measuring 29 degrees and a coronal deviation of 8.3 cm. The possibility that the scoliosis was a separate entity requiring further treatment was discussed with the family.

At 6 weeks, X-rays showed significant resolution of the scoliosis with restitution of coronal balance. The lumbar curve was 14 degrees and the thoracic curve 21 degrees.

At 7 months, she was attending school without leg or back pain. The final radiograph (Fig. 4) showed complete resolution of the scoliosis and solid fusion across L5/S1. No complications were noted during follow-up.

## Conclusion
Although this patient had a progressive, double-curve scoliosis with large Cobb angles and significant L5 pedicular rotation, we did not simultaneously address both conditions. The curve resolved after rotational correction and reduction of the L5 vertebral body along with pain resolution. Until the relationship between these pathologies is conclusively defined, it appears safe to observe the scoliosis—even in high-grade spondylolisthesis—after addressing the slip.
