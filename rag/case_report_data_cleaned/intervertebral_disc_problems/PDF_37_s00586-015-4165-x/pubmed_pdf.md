# Use of Intraoperative Fluoroscopy for the Safe Placement of C2 Laminar Screws: Technical Note

## Abstract
Introduction Rigid fixation of the atlantoaxial joint can be challenging due to complex anatomic variants. Numerous techniques have evolved over time, improving the surgeon's adaptability. The advent of C2 laminar screws adds to the surgeon's armamentarium but has limitations. Risk of ventral laminar breach with possible spinal cord injury, CSF leak, or poor bony fixation has led some to recommend prefabricated models or expensive intraoperative spinal navigation to aid screw placement. The purpose of this report is to detail how intraoperative fluoroscopy can aid in the safe placement of C2 laminar screws. Methods One patient with rheumatoid arthritis and progressive cervical myelopathy from C1-2 instability underwent C1-2 fixation using C2 laminar screws. Intraoperative fluoroscopy was used to guide and confirm safe laminar screw placement. Results Immediate and 6-month postoperative imaging demonstrated excellent placement of C2 laminar screws without ventral breach. At 6 months, the patient noted significant improvement of her preoperative symptoms. Conclusion Use of intraoperative fluoroscopy is an easy and safe method for the placement of C2 laminar screws. Given its use of readily available equipment, this method can be implemented without significant pre-planning, or as an impromptu salvage maneuver.

## Introduction
Techniques to achieve rigid fixation of the atlantoaxial complex have continuously evolved since their inception. Brooks and Gallie first introduced the concept of sublaminar wiring with placement of structural autologous bone graft in the 1960s. This technique requires adjuvant external immobilization with a halo or Minerva brace and is associated with a high rate of pseudoarthrosis [2, 3]. Jeanneret and Magerl improved fixation with transarticular screws for C1-2 fusion, providing greater rates of arthrodesis without prolonged external immobilization. The development of C1 lateral mass, C2 pedicle, and C2 pars screws has largely supplanted transarticular screws due to the technically demanding nature of C1-2 transarticular screw placement and risk of vertebral artery injury. However, pedicle screw placement carries similar risk, with cadaveric series reporting up to 26% of patients with vertebral artery anatomy precluding pedicle screw placement and clinical series reporting 2–8% incidence of vertebral artery injury [7, 8].

Wright [15] developed a C2 laminar screw technique that obviates the risk of vertebral artery injury inherent to pedicle or transarticular screw placement. Taking advantage of the capacious C2 lamina, crossing C2 laminar screws are often placed by a freehand technique, visualizing the slope of the lamina and directing the screw along that trajectory. Ventral breaches can be difficult to recognize, however, and can lead to neurologic injury from damage to the upper cervical spinal cord, construct failure due to poor bony fixation, and wound healing problems from cerebrospinal fluid leaks. We describe a simple technique using intraoperative fluoroscopy to visualize the trajectory of the lamina and confirm intralaminar screw placement without a ventral breach.

## Case Report
A 62-year-old female with rheumatoid arthritis presented with progressive cervical myelopathy manifested as diffuse weakness of all four extremities, bilateral hand clumsiness, and progressive gait ataxia. MRI showed pannus formation at C1-2 with spinal cord compression. Cervical spine X-rays showed reduction of C1-2 anterolisthesis in extension (not pictured), and CT showed a unilateral high-riding vertebral artery, precluding safe placement of a C2 pedicle screw on that side. The patient underwent C1-2 instrumentation and arthrodesis with iliac crest bone graft to enhance the probability of arthrodesis in the setting of disease-modifying rheumatologic medications. Using intraoperative fluoroscopy, C2 laminar screws were safely placed.

At 6 months postoperatively, the patient reported significant improvement in preoperative myelopathy symptoms, and X-rays showed a durable construct without hardware loosening or recurrence of C1-2 anterolisthesis.

## Surgical Technique
The patient is positioned prone on gel rolls, with the head rigidly secured using a Mayfield head holder. A standard subperiosteal exposure of the C1 and C2 posterior elements is performed. C1 lateral mass screw placement by freehand technique is performed as described by Harms. For C2 laminar screws, the fluoroscopy machine is brought in from over the top of the head.

Prior to the operation, the angle of the lamina from the horizontal is measured on the preoperative CT. The arc of the C-arm is adjusted to that angle until a view down the "eye" of the lamina is obtained. Screw placement proceeds using a 1 mm high-speed burr to create the pilot hole, followed by a 2.4 mm drill to establish the trajectory. A 4 mm x 32 mm laminar screw is then placed. Fluoroscopy can be used during all steps to visualize the ventral margin of the lamina and confirm appropriate placement within the lamina without ventral breach. After successful placement of the first laminar screw, the C-arm arc is rotated to obtain a view down the eye of the contralateral lamina, and the process is repeated. Postoperative CT confirms bilateral C2 laminar screws without ventral breach.

## Discussion
Rigid fixation of the atlantoaxial construct can be challenging due to anatomic variants unique to the C1-2 complex. Congenital anomalies such as os odontoideum, hypoplastic C2 pedicles, and significant vertebral artery asymmetry increase the risks associated with C2 instrumentation and, in some cases, preclude placement of C1-2 transarticular and C2 pedicle screws. Sairyo et al. [13] recommend a minimum C2 pedicle diameter of 3.5 mm for safe placement of C2 pedicle screws, and Meng et al. [8] recommend a minimum diameter of 5.5 mm, further limiting the population in which C2 pedicle screws are feasible. In their radiographic study of C2 anatomy in os odontoideum patients, Meng et al. [8] show 14 of 29 patients have C2 pedicles less than 3.5 mm in diameter and 20 of 29 patients have C2 pedicles under 5.5 mm. C2 laminar screws offer a safe alternative for rigid C1-2 fixation without risk of vertebral artery injury. In our patient, the pedicles measured 4.1 mm on the right and 2.8 mm on the left, and preoperative imaging showed a high-riding vertebral artery on the left. Both pedicle size and anomalous vertebral artery position factored into the decision to use translaminar rather than transpedicular C2 fixation.

Since Wright [15] developed bilateral crossing C2 laminar screws, several series have documented their efficacy. However, despite the relative ease of C2 laminar screw placement, ventral laminar breaches have been reported that required reoperation for revision (Table 1). Ventral laminar breaches can occasionally be detected by direct palpation under the C2 lamina; however, this technique is likely to detect only the most egregious breaches and does little to prevent damage caused by errant screw placement into the upper cervical spinal canal. Similarly, direct palpation may not recognize partial ventral breaches that are noted on postoperative imaging and that predispose to poor bony fixation and pseudoarthrosis. In cadaveric studies, plain radiographs have limited sensitivity (reported 68.8–77.4%) to detect ventral breaches [5].

Concerns over the difficulties in placing upper cervical hardware, as well as the infrequency with which many spine surgeons perform these operations, have prompted the development of navigation techniques to ensure accurate screw placement. Lu et al. [6] developed a computer-generated spine template used intraoperatively to guide laminar screw placement and prevent misplaced hardware. This system requires production of a patient-specific model and template and significant preoperative planning. Technical difficulties can be encountered if soft tissues are not sufficiently dissected from the lamina, preventing proper seating of the cast to the bone. Rajasekaran et al. [12] and Nottmeier and Foy [9] advocate intraoperative spinal navigation for safe C2 laminar screw placement, but both note difficulty with intraoperative point-to-point registration due to the complex anatomy of C1-2 and the need for advanced fluoroscopic imaging using an isocentric 3D C-arm.

The technique described here does not require advanced production of computer-generated models nor the use of expensive 3D navigation, which may not be available in all practices. Using a standard C-arm available in most settings, we were able to visualize the ventral border of the lamina and confirm safe laminar screw placement. Additionally, the ability to visualize the inner diameter of the lamina allows bilateral screw placement with entry-point modifications made to adjust to the unique anatomy of each patient's upper cervical spine. For surgeons who prefer freehand placement of C2 translaminar screws, our technique can be used as an intraoperative control to confirm appropriate screw position before leaving the operating room.

Many surgeons may be concerned about increased radiation exposure related to this technique. By measuring the angle of the lamina from the horizontal on preoperative CT and using this angle to orient the C-arm in the correct plane, radiation exposures can be limited. Furthermore, comparing our technique with a recent C1-2 fusion and odontoid screw placement performed by the senior author shows comparable or reduced radiation exposure times (Table 2).

Our proposed technique uses resources available in most clinical practices and can be used without significant preoperative planning. This allows impromptu use of laminar screws as a salvage maneuver for C2 pedicle and pars screws, even by experienced surgeons, or as a safety guard against errant screw placement by the occasional surgeon. As further advances in translaminar screws develop, including at the lower cervical levels [1, 4, 16] where the canal is less capacious, modifications to this technique may help promote safe screw placement at these additional levels.
