# Minimally Invasive Percutaneous Endoscopic Treatment for Acute Pyogenic Spondylodiscitis Following Vertebroplasty

## Abstract
This is the first report of minimally invasive endoscopic treatment for acute spondylodiscitis caused by vertebroplasty. Case presentation: A 60-year-old female was transferred with right hip flexion weakness for 1 day. She had undergone vertebroplasty for an L3 osteoporotic compression fracture at another hospital 6 weeks earlier. Percutaneous endoscopic procedures, including needle biopsy, debridement, root decompression, and drainage, were performed. One week after endoscopic treatment, symptoms of back pain and nerve palsy improved significantly. Subsequently, the patient underwent conservative treatment with appropriate antibiotics according to bacterial culture results. Six weeks postoperatively she was pain free, with no neurological deficits or signs of infection. Five months later, spontaneous fusion between the L2 and L3 vertebral bodies was observed. Conclusion: We report a case treated with an endoscopic procedure, without open surgery, for acute pyogenic spondylodiscitis following vertebroplasty.

Keywords: Vertebroplasty, Spinal infection, Spondylodiscitis, Endoscopic surgery

## Introduction
Percutaneous vertebroplasty is a widely used treatment for osteoporotic spinal fractures. As the number of vertebroplasties has increased, complications have also emerged. Among them, pyogenic spondylodiscitis is uncommon. The main line of treatment in cases of infected vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty is operative debridement and stabilization, whereas others have treated cases conservatively [1]. However, perioperative morbidity is high with invasive conventional surgeries such as anterior debridement, corpectomy, and posterior spinal fusion [2]. Elderly patients or those with poor general condition particularly benefit from minimally invasive surgery. We report a case of minimally invasive endoscopic treatment for pyogenic spondylodiscitis after vertebroplasty. To our knowledge, there have been no reported cases treated with fully endoscopic treatment for acute spondylodiscitis caused by vertebroplasty.

## Case Report
A 60-year-old female was transferred with right buttock and leg pain and inability to raise her right leg for 1 day. Her medical history included rheumatoid arthritis. She had undergone vertebroplasty for an L3 osteoporotic compression fracture at another hospital 6 weeks earlier. She reported worsening back pain after vertebroplasty. Physical examination revealed right hip flexion weakness (Grade 2), with no other motor impairment. The white blood cell count was 16,800 cells/uL and the C-reactive protein level was 3.3 mg/L. Plain radiographs demonstrated destruction and collapse of the L3 body with leaked cement (Fig. 1). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed L2 and L3 spondylitis with a left-sided psoas abscess and L2/3 discitis (Fig. 2). These findings were consistent with acute pyogenic spondylodiscitis after vertebroplasty.

Because of neurological impairment with right L2 motor palsy, percutaneous endoscopic treatment was performed via a posterolateral approach under local anesthesia and conscious sedation. As premedication, midazolam (0.05 mg/kg) was injected intramuscularly 30 minutes before surgery. Dexmedetomidine (1 mcg/kg over 10 minutes for a loading dose, 0.2-0.7 mcg/kg/h for maintenance) was administered intravenously during surgery. The patient was placed prone on a radiolucent table. The skin entry point was at the lateral edge of the paravertebral back muscle (about 8-13 cm lateral to the midline, depending on waist size), and local anesthesia was administered. After sterile preparation and draping, a spinal needle was inserted directly into the targeted disc, and the abscess was aspirated for microorganism cultures (Fig. 3). After aspiration, endoscopic procedures were performed, including wide debridement of infected disc, necrotic bone, and foreign material (cement) (Fig. 4). Necrotic bone was removed through the working cannula by piecemeal. Large cement fragments could not be removed through the working cannula alone; therefore, they were extracted by pulling the endoscope and sheath together. The largest cement fragment became lodged at the skin and was removed with a Kelly forceps (Fig. 5). Finally, a drainage tube (diameter 3.2 mm) was inserted into the debrided disc space (Fig. 6) and connected to a negative-pressure pump. Plain radiographs and MRI were obtained 1 week postoperatively (Fig. 7).

One week after endoscopic treatment, back pain and nerve palsy improved significantly. The tube was removed 10 days after the endoscopic procedure when drainage decreased to less than 10 mL/day. Cultures grew methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus epidermidis (MRSE). The patient received targeted antibiotic therapy for 6 weeks according to susceptibility testing: vancomycin (1 g intravenously every 12 hours) initially, then discontinued on day 13 due to a skin rash and switched to teicoplanin (800 mg intravenously once daily) for 4 weeks.

We anticipated spontaneous fusion during follow-up because the L2 body gradually docked into the L3 body with stability; thus, anterior debridement, corpectomy, and posterior spinal fusion were not performed. Six weeks postoperatively, she was pain free with no neurological deficits or signs of infection. Five months later, spontaneous fusion between the L2 and L3 bodies was observed (Fig. 8).

## Discussion
Spinal infection after vertebroplasty is uncommon, and its incidence has not been specifically reported in previous studies. Reported rates range from no infections in 4,547 cases to 2 cases among 104 patients (1.9%) [3, 4]. Yu et al. [5] reported 1 infection (0.5%) among 200 vertebroplasty cases, and Hamdan et al. [1] reported an infection rate of 0.46% (6/1,307) among vertebroplasty or kyphoplasty patients.

The main line of treatment in infected vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty is operative debridement and stabilization, though some cases are treated conservatively [1]. Numerous studies have reported outcomes using anterior debridement with or without supplemental instrumentation [1, 5-10]. Although effective for definitive infection control and early stabilization, these are invasive procedures with potential postoperative complications. Because many vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty patients are older or immunocompromised, invasive conventional surgery may increase mortality; perioperative mortality of 33.3% has been reported after anterior corpectomy and posterior stabilization [1].

Endoscopic spine surgery has advanced substantially as a minimally invasive alternative. With improved instruments and camera systems, endoscopic surgery now applies to various spinal procedures. Although extensive debridement and eradication of infected tissue have traditionally favored open surgery, percutaneous endoscopic debridement can successfully treat spinal infections. The diagnostic and therapeutic efficacy of percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (PELD) in spinal infections has been reported [2, 11, 12], emphasizing early infection control for satisfactory outcomes. Yang et al. [13] reported that PELD is an effective alternative to extensive open surgery for advanced infection with paraspinal abscess and postoperative recurrent infection, providing adequate biopsy specimens, symptom relief, and infection eradication with less morbidity.

To date, there has been no case report of fully endoscopic treatment for spinal infection after vertebroplasty. Typically, cement adheres firmly to bone after vertebroplasty, making complete removal challenging with endoscopy alone. Lee et al. reported a case of temporary percutaneous endoscopic irrigation and debridement after vertebroplasty, but an anterior corpectomy was performed 3 weeks later.

There are limitations to endoscopic-only treatment of spondylodiscitis after vertebroplasty. The debridement range may be more limited than in open surgery, and reoperation may be required for recurrence. However, recurrent infection can often be effectively treated by endoscopic spine surgery through a posterolateral approach at the original incision site, reserving complex revision open surgery for failure after endoscopy [13]. Success in vertebroplasty-induced spondylodiscitis depends largely on removal of cement and inflammatory tissue; endoscopic-only treatment requires a high level of endoscopic skill, and outcomes may vary with operator experience.

In this case, posterior fusion was initially planned after infection control. During follow-up, the L2 and L3 vertebral bodies stabilized in a chevron configuration and progressed to interbody fusion. Although intervertebral fusion occurred naturally here, this may not occur in all cases. If a spinal segment remains unstable and symptomatic after endoscopic infection treatment, additional fusion surgery can be considered. With ongoing advances in instruments and techniques, minimally invasive endoscopic approaches can also be applied to fusion procedures.

Mild L2/3 segmental kyphosis was observed at final follow-up, but the patient had no local or global symptoms; therefore, no corrective surgery was performed. If segmental kyphosis becomes problematic, an open combined intervention may be required.

Areas once reserved for open surgery are increasingly managed with minimally invasive techniques. Even in spinal infections traditionally requiring extensive debridement, favorable outcomes have been achieved with minimally invasive approaches. Our case suggests that even complicated spinal infections after vertebroplasty can be treated with a fully endoscopic technique, expanding the scope of endoscopic spinal surgery and offering a viable option for skilled endoscopic surgeons.

## Conclusions
Based on this case, percutaneous endoscopic treatment appears to be an effective minimally invasive method for pyogenic spondylodiscitis after vertebroplasty.
