# A Rare Postoperative Complication of Anterior Lower Thoracic Instrumentation: Diaphragmatic Laceration with Hemothorax

## Abstract

**Purpose** To highlight the importance of preventing visceral injury due to prominent anterior implants at the spinal column.  

**Methods** A 52-year-old man with cord compression was treated with a T9/10 discectomy and instrumented fusion via a right thoracotomy and transpleural approach. Postoperatively, he had improved lower limb numbness. However, after a bout of coughing, there was a sudden increase in chest drain output, hemodynamic instability, and massive fluid collection in the right chest cavity.  

**Results** Emergency re-exploration of the thoracotomy was performed, and a 6 cm laceration of the right posteromedial diaphragm was identified as the bleeding source. The laceration was found to be in close proximity to the locking nut of the anterior implants. The laceration was repaired, and a soft synthetic patch was used to cover the implants. Postoperatively, the hemothorax resolved and the patient recovered from the neurological deficit.  

**Conclusions** Prevention of diaphragmatic injury can be achieved using lower-profile and less sharp-edged implants. Implant coverage with a soft synthetic material is necessary if direct repair of the parietal pleura over the implants cannot be performed.

## Introduction

Spinal cord decompression by discectomy and instrumented fusion through a thoracotomy approach is a commonly used procedure [1, 2]. Postoperative spontaneous hemothorax is a known but rare complication after this transpleural approach to the thoracic spine [3–5]. It may be caused by re-bleeding of epidural vessels, oozing from exposed bone surfaces, inadvertent lung laceration, or vascular injury. These causes are usually identified intraoperatively or in the immediate postoperative period. However, late presentation up to 10 months postoperatively has been reported due to violation of the inferior phrenic artery by anterior instrumentation [4].

Delay in diagnosis of early hemothorax is unlikely because of obvious clinical manifestations such as high chest drain output, hemodynamic instability, and a drop in hemoglobin level. A simple chest X-ray can easily confirm the diagnosis of hemothorax.

We report a case of early spontaneous hemothorax caused by a diaphragmatic laceration from anterior instrumentation. This case serves to remind surgeons of the importance of identifying and preventing potential visceral injury intraoperatively. This can be achieved by using lower-profile and less sharp-edged implants or by covering implants with a soft synthetic material when parietal pleura closure cannot provide adequate coverage. During exploration of a postoperative hemothorax, meticulous examination of the diaphragmatic surface opposing the implants is necessary to control potential sources of hemorrhage.

## Case Report

A 52-year-old man with a history of thoracolumbar back pain for many years was admitted because of spontaneous onset of gradual lower limb weakness and numbness for three days. The right lower limb strength was 3/5 by Medical Research Council grading, whereas the left side remained 5/5. Sensory level was at T11, and sensation in both lower limbs was affected. He also had unsustained ankle clonus and an upgoing Babinski sign in the right lower limb. There was no sphincter dysfunction, and anal tone was intact.

Plain radiographs of the thoracic and lumbar spine were unremarkable. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a T9/10 prolapsed intervertebral disc causing spinal cord compression with myelomalacic changes.

Urgent anterior T9/10 discectomy and instrumented fusion were performed through a right T8 thoracotomy and transpleural approach. At the end of surgery, the implants could only be partially covered by the parietal pleura due to the bulkiness of the implants and the inability to mobilize additional pleura. The thoracotomy was repaired over one chest tube. The patient reported reduced numbness in the lower limbs immediately after surgery. No hemothorax was seen on plain chest radiograph 6 h after the operation, and the chest drain output was only 100 mL. He was hemodynamically stable at that time.

The patient subsequently coughed vigorously due to sputum retention and reported immediate retrosternal pain. Thereafter, the chest drain output increased to 1200 mL over 8 h, and his blood pressure became labile. Repeat chest radiograph 14 h after the initial operation showed significant fluid accumulation inside the right thorax. Given the rapid onset following bouts of heavy coughing, hemothorax was highly suspected.

He was immediately taken back to the operating theater for re-exploration of the right thoracotomy. During reopening of the thoracotomy, the patient developed hiccups despite general anesthesia. A right posteromedial diaphragmatic laceration of approximately 6 cm opposing the spinal implant was found, with active bleeding. The laceration was repaired, and the spinal implants were covered with a synthetic dural patch to prevent further diaphragmatic injury. Old blood clots were removed from the pleural cavity, and the thoracotomy was repaired over two chest tubes.

Subsequent chest drain output was not excessive, and the chest tubes were removed on day 4 after the second surgery. Chest radiograph taken one week after re-exploration revealed no recurrent hemothorax, and postoperative thoracic spine radiographs demonstrated satisfactory implant position. The patient had near-full neurological recovery two weeks after spinal cord decompression and was able to walk independently without aids.

## Discussion

The diaphragm is a dome-shaped muscle essential for respiration. During inspiration, contraction of the diaphragm flattens the dome, increasing intrathoracic volume and decreasing intrathoracic pressure, thereby allowing air entry into the lungs. During expiration, relaxation of the diaphragm restores its dome shape, increasing intrathoracic pressure and expelling air from the chest cavity.

When the diaphragm is in its dome shape, the peripheral muscular part lies in close proximity to the lower chest wall and lower thoracic spine. During coughing, intrathoracic pressure is markedly increased by contraction of the abdominal muscles [6]. This increase in intra-abdominal pressure causes a vigorous upward thrust of the diaphragm, resulting in a strong gliding movement between the lower thoracic spine and the diaphragm. Any hard or prominent object along this path can impede smooth gliding and potentially lacerate the diaphragm.

Both hemidiaphragms move across several intercostal spaces within a single breathing cycle. The right hemidiaphragm usually lies more rostrally due to the underlying liver, and its middle and posterior thirds exhibit greater excursion than the anterior third [7]. As a result, the posterior part of the right hemidiaphragm is particularly vulnerable to injury from prominent implants inserted in the lower thoracic spine.

Under normal circumstances, gliding movement within the pleural cavity generates minimal friction because of the smooth parietal and visceral pleural surfaces and the presence of lubricating pleural fluid [8]. Although repair of the parietal pleura is desirable to provide a barrier between implants and the pleural cavity, this is not always feasible because of implant bulkiness and pleural inelasticity. Possible solutions include mobilization of a larger area of parietal pleura or use of a smooth synthetic patch to cover the implants. Lower-profile implants also facilitate complete pleural closure.

Due to the rarity of this complication, the optimal synthetic material for implant coverage remains unknown and is beyond the scope of this report. However, the material should be nonabsorbable, sufficiently thick to provide a cushioning effect, and able to hold sutures to surrounding tissue. A watertight seal is likely unnecessary, as scarring occurs approximately 2–3 weeks after repair, allowing the material to function as a permanent cushioning device.

Postoperative diaphragmatic laceration due to anterior lower thoracic spinal instrumentation has not been previously reported. Instrumentation on the right side combined with chest tube insertion may represent additional risk factors. The liver may push the right hemidiaphragm against prominent implants, suggesting that a left-sided approach may be safer in some cases. However, surgical approach is often dictated by pathology, as in this case where right-sided symptoms necessitated a right-sided approach. Chest tube–related pain may also promote diaphragmatic breathing, increasing friction between the diaphragm and implants. Whether this increases susceptibility to diaphragmatic injury requires further investigation.

Although it is difficult to completely exclude intraoperative inadvertent injury as a cause of diaphragmatic laceration, the timing and clinical sequence strongly support our postulation. Early postoperative imaging showed no hemothorax and minimal chest drainage. Following vigorous coughing and retrosternal pain, the patient developed hemodynamic instability, a marked increase in chest drain output, and radiographic evidence of massive hemothorax. Intraoperative hiccups during re-exploration further suggested diaphragmatic irritation.

Previous reports have described hemothorax due to rib fractures requiring surgical repair [9, 10], but such cases offer limited preventive strategies. In contrast, this report highlights specific technical considerations in anterior spinal instrumentation, including implant selection and coverage. Although some may consider simple discectomy without instrumentation sufficient for thoracic disc herniation [11], instrumented fusion was chosen in this case to enhance fusion rate, minimize graft-related complications, and avoid external bracing. The key message remains the importance of low-profile, non–sharp-edged implants and adequate implant coverage using pleural repair or soft synthetic material in lower anterior thoracic surgery.

## Conclusion

Surgeons should select low-profile implants for anterior instrumentation at the lower thoracic spine. Complete coverage of implants by repair of the parietal pleura is desirable. When complete coverage is not possible because of tight or friable pleura, the use of a smooth synthetic material to cover the implants should be considered. A left-sided approach may be safer in some cases, although the choice of approach should ultimately be dictated by the underlying pathology.
