# Venous Hypertensive Myelopathy Associated With Cervical Spondylosis

## Abstract
Background: Venous hypertensive myelopathy (VHM) results from spinal vascular malformations causing venous hypertension, congestive edema, and neurologic dysfunction. There has been no report of VHM associated with cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM).

Purpose: To report an extremely rare case of VHM likely due to CSM.

Study Design: Case report with literature review.

Patient Sample: A 51-year-old man with CSM exhibiting relatively rapid neurologic deterioration and expansion of a centromedullary T2-hyperintense lesion on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) without traumatic injury.

Methods: Neurologic examination and radiologic imaging with and without contrast.

Results: The patient developed cervical radiculopathy followed by gait disturbance and motor weakness. Cervical MRI demonstrated canal stenosis due to disc bulging and ligamentum flavum hypertrophy at C5/C6 and C6/C7, and a T2-hyperintense area over C5–C7. Although decompression was planned, an acute inflammatory process (transverse myelitis or demyelinating disease) was also considered and intravenous steroids were administered, yielding transient improvement. Symptoms then worsened significantly. Repeat MRI showed marked progression of the T2-hyperintense lesion from C4 to T1. Flow voids were noted on the dorsal surface of the upper cervical cord on T2-weighted MRI. Laboratory studies, medical history, and localized enhancement on contrast-enhanced MRI indicated a low probability of spinal inflammatory disease. Two-level anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) was performed; symptoms improved promptly postoperatively.

Conclusions: VHM can be caused by spondylotic cord compression in the absence of spinal vascular malformations. Diagnostic features include progressive myelopathy, posture-related symptom fluctuation, centromedullary T2 hyperintensity spanning multiple segments, and dorsal cord flow voids on T2-weighted MRI.

## Introduction
Cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) is a common cause of compressive spinal cord dysfunction. Degenerative changes—including disc bulging, osteophytes, hypertrophy of posterior spinal ligaments, and canal narrowing—compress the spinal cord and can cause chronic neural injury and ischemia. The typical course of CSM is a relatively slow, progressive, stepwise deterioration in neurologic function with plateau periods [1,2]. Rapid neurologic decline in CSM is uncommon; when reported, it is often associated with minor trauma, and extensive intramedullary T2 signal changes are not common. We report an extremely rare case of CSM with relatively rapid deterioration and abnormal expansion of a high T2 signal area on MRI in the absence of traumatic injury.

## Case Report
A 51-year-old man with no significant medical history (including infections) presented with tingling in the left thumb and index finger with spontaneous onset. Within a week, he developed difficulty with fine motor tasks and left-hand weakness. By 3 weeks, he had left-sided upper and lower extremity weakness and gait disturbance with increased tone and stiffness. He experienced bilateral arm and leg numbness after several minutes when fully supine and slept on his right side because left-side decubitus provoked left-sided numbness.

Plain radiographs showed moderate spondylotic changes without instability. MRI demonstrated canal stenosis at C5/C6 and C6/C7 and a relatively large T2-hyperintense area (Fig. 1). There was no T1 signal change. Although decompression was planned, an acute inflammatory process (transverse myelitis or demyelinating disease) was also considered given the MRI appearance. Reflexes were increased in both upper extremities; Hoffmann and Babinski signs were positive on the left. Intravenous steroids led to 1–2 days of improvement, followed by significant worsening with gait difficulty.

Repeat cervical MRI revealed marked progression of the T2-hyperintense lesion from C4 to T1 (Fig. 2). In the axial plane at C5, a T2-hyperintense area was also seen on the right side of the cord (Fig. 2B), although right-sided symptoms were absent. Post-contrast MRI showed cord enhancement at C5/C6 (localized to the left hemicord) without significant canal narrowing laterality at that level (Fig. 3). Workup for myelitis (including Lyme disease and neuromyelitis optica) and additional brain and whole-spine imaging were unremarkable.

Multidisciplinary evaluation (neurology, neurosurgery, orthopedic spine) concluded that disc herniations at C5/C6 and C6/C7 contributed to cord edema with progressive neurologic decline. Notably, flow voids were present on the dorsal surface of the upper cervical cord on T2-weighted whole-spine MRI (Fig. 3), a specific feature of spinal VHM [4]. The patient underwent two-level ACDF with anterior plating. Surgery and recovery were uneventful. Postoperatively, gait disturbance improved significantly, though partial loss of left-hand dexterity persisted.

## Discussion
This case presented with rapid neurologic deterioration and abnormal expansion of a T2-hyperintense intramedullary lesion. Coexisting transverse myelitis or multiple sclerosis was suspected due to lesion enlargement; however, localized enhancement on contrast MRI, negative laboratory workup, and clinical history indicated a low probability of systemic inflammatory myelitis. The T2-hyperintense area was mainly within the gray matter, which also argued against demyelinating disease that typically involves white matter in brain and cord [5]. Decompression with two-level ACDF led to prompt improvement, and MRI at 8 weeks showed a small residual local T2-hyperintense area, supporting compressive pathophysiology as the proximate cause of the deterioration and lesion expansion.

In addition to lesion expansion, dorsal cord flow voids on T2-weighted MRI emerged after deterioration, resembling the characteristic appearance of spinal dural arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) [4,6]. AVFs create arteriovenous shunts that impede venous drainage, producing venous engorgement and congestive spinal edema. This can progress to cord ischemia and acute or subacute neurologic decline—VHM. Characteristic MRI features of VHM include a reversible, diffuse T2-hyperintense lesion extending across more than four segments and dorsal cord flow voids [4,6,7]. Because these reflect systemic cord edema and abnormal venous flow from impaired drainage, we hypothesize that cervical canal stenosis and cord compression in this case impaired venous outflow, resulting in progressive myelopathy.

The clinical course and MRI evolution also resembled subacute posttraumatic ascending myelopathy (SPAM), a rare disorder characterized by delayed neurologic decline and marked enlargement of intramedullary T2 hyperintensity spanning more than three segments from the initial level after spinal cord injury. Although the pathophysiology of SPAM remains uncertain, recent reports suggest venous drainage impairment and hypertension [8–10]. Supporting venous hypertension in such cases are: mismatch between the neurologic level and the extent of T2 hyperintensity; fluctuation of deficits and MRI findings over time; posture-related worsening or easing of symptoms; and dorsal cord flow voids. All of these were observed here, strongly suggesting impaired spinal venous drainage as a key mechanism.

VHM is commonly linked to spinal vascular malformations (dural AVF or arteriovenous malformations), but VHM related to cervical spondylosis is rare. Krishnan et al. reported VHM from a noncompressive C5/C6 disc herniation that mimicked AVF on MRI, with complete recovery after C5/C6 ACDF [11]. Auler et al. described traumatic mediastinal hematoma compressing the brachiocephalic vein and causing VHM, which resolved after hematoma resolution [12]. The pathophysiology of SPAM may overlap with spinal VHM, and “subacute delayed ascending myelopathy” may be a more appropriate term, as it is not exclusively posttraumatic [10]. These reports suggest VHM can develop from various causes even without vascular anomalies. Some cases of rapid deterioration in CSM of unknown cause may have been secondary to VHM [4,13].

Reversibility of imaging abnormalities and neurologic decline after relieving venous hypertension is a hallmark of VHM. However, persistent elevation of intraspinal pressure can disrupt the venous system and cause intramedullary hemorrhage, leading to irreversible cord damage and severe permanent paralysis [9]. Prompt diagnosis and intervention to reduce intraspinal pressure before irreversible injury occurs are crucial.

## Conclusion
VHM can be caused by spondylotic cord compression in the absence of spinal vascular malformations. MRI features supportive of VHM include cord swelling, abnormal expansion of intramedullary T2 hyperintensity spanning multiple segments, and dorsal cord flow voids on T2-weighted images. In progressive myelopathy with mismatch between T2 hyperintensity extent and clinical level, and with posture-related symptom fluctuation, VHM should be included in the differential diagnosis.
