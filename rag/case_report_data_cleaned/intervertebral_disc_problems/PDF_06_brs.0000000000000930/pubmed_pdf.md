# Successful Total En Bloc Spondylectomy of T7 Vertebra for Hepatocellular Carcinoma Metastasis

## Abstract
- Study Design: Case report.
- Summary of Background Data: Spinal metastasis from hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) has a poor prognosis. There are only a few studies on surgical outcomes of spinal metastasis from HCC. Because of high surgical morbidity and short life expectancy in patients with HCC and spinal metastasis, total en bloc spondylectomy (TES) is generally not considered, although several studies have reported satisfactory results for TES in some metastatic spinal tumors. Liver transplantation (LT) is a curative treatment option for early HCC; however, recurrence of HCC can occur after LT, and there have been no reports on surgery for spinal metastasis following LT for HCC. We report the first case of successful TES for T7 metastasis after living donor LT for HCC.
- Methods: A 65-year-old man had undergone living donor LT for HCC 2 years earlier. He presented with progressive gait disturbance due to spinal cord compression by a tumor at T7. Radiology and pathology revealed a solitary T7 metastasis with no liver recurrence and no other organ metastasis. TES was performed using a pedicle screw system and a mesh cage filled with frozen autografts.
- Results: Postoperatively, neurological symptoms improved. At 3 months, T4 metastasis was detected by magnetic resonance imaging, and the patient underwent heavy ion radiotherapy. He was able to walk without a cane, and there was no evidence of recurrence 1.5 years after surgery.
- Conclusions: Solitary spinal metastasis of HCC may be an indication for TES if liver function improves after LT. Key words: hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), total en bloc spondylectomy (TES), spondylectomy, spinal metastasis, liver.

## Introduction
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) frequently metastasizes to the lung (in 34%-70% of patients) and to lymph nodes (in 16%-45%), whereas bone metastasis occurs in 3%-20% of patients. The spine is the most frequent site of bone metastasis, and spinal involvement is associated with a poor prognosis [1,2]. Therefore, TES, a radical surgery for spinal metastasis, is generally not indicated for spinal involvement from HCC.

LT is a curative treatment option for early HCC, although the recurrence rate is estimated at 15%-20% [3,4]. There are no reports of spinal surgery for spinal metastasis following LT for HCC. We report the first case of successful TES for T7 metastasis after living donor LT for HCC.

## Case Report
A 65-year-old man who had undergone living donor LT for HCC 3 years earlier presented with a 2-month history of back pain. He experienced rapid progression of paralysis of the lower extremities and was unable to walk. Muscle strength in the lower extremities was grade 3/5, and there was bilateral sensory loss below the inguinal region.

Magnetic resonance imaging showed a solitary tumor with homogeneous low signal intensity on both T1- and T2-weighted images at the T7 vertebra and lamina, severely compressing the spinal cord (Figure 1). Computed tomography demonstrated low-density areas at the T7 vertebra and lamina with pathological fractures. Protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist-II (PIVKA-II), a tumor marker for HCC, was markedly elevated (13,300 mAU/mL; normal <40 mAU/mL). Positron emission tomography showed neither liver recurrence nor other organ metastasis. CT-guided needle biopsy of T7 confirmed metastatic HCC. Despite radiation therapy, paralysis of the lower legs deteriorated further. The Tomita score and the modified Tokuhashi score were 5 and 10, respectively.

The patient underwent TES. After en bloc laminectomy of T7, pedicle screws were inserted bilaterally at T5, T6, T8, and T9, and titanium alloy rods were placed. After en bloc corpectomy, anterior reconstruction was performed with a titanium mesh cage filled with frozen autografts (Figure 2A). The operation time was 5 hours 10 minutes, and intraoperative blood loss was 250 mL.

PIVKA-II decreased to 26 mAU/mL at 1 month postoperatively but increased to 469 mAU/mL at 3 months, when magnetic resonance imaging revealed a T4 vertebral metastasis (Figure 2B). The patient elected heavy ion radiotherapy. PIVKA-II decreased to 34 mAU/mL. The T4 metastatic lesion showed high intensity on T2-weighted images, with no new lesions detected (Figure 3). CT showed osteosclerotic changes around the T4 lesion. He was able to walk without a cane, and there was no evidence of recurrence 1.5 years after surgery.

## Discussion
Treatment options for spinal metastasis include radiotherapy and surgery; surgery may be preferable when neurological deterioration is present [7]. Wu et al. reported that surgery markedly improved and maintained quality of life over 9 months in patients with spinal metastasis [8]. Whether surgery should be performed for HCC patients with spinal metastasis remains controversial. The prognosis is poor, with a median survival of 3-6 months [9,10]. Zheng et al. reported that spinal surgery did not provide benefits in quality of life or survival for patients with spinal metastasis from HCC [11]. Given the high surgical morbidity and limited life expectancy, surgeons may be reluctant to operate. Few reports address surgery in this population. Kim et al. reported a series of 33 patients with spinal HCC metastasis, with survival of 203 ± 31 days after operation and a significant correlation between hepatic function and survival [12]. Zhang et al. found that a Tomita score ≤7 was a favorable prognostic factor in 36 patients with HCC spinal metastasis [13].

Several studies have reported a survival benefit of TES for spinal metastasis in selected patients [14-17]. Following Tomita’s surgical strategy for spinal metastasis, TES is indicated for long-term control in patients with a Tomita score of 2, 3, or 4 [5]. In this case, the Tomita score was 5, suggesting the surgical goal was mid- to short-term palliation rather than cure [5]. In the modified Tokuhashi system, a total score of 10 corresponds to 73% survival beyond 6 months and 30% beyond 1 year [6]. This patient, however, had good liver function, no liver recurrence after LT, and no metastases other than a solitary spinal lesion. Therefore, TES was performed with the expectation of longer-term survival. The patient remains in good condition without evidence of recurrence and is satisfied with the treatment.

After LT, the lung is the most common site of extrahepatic tumor recurrence for HCC, and bone is also frequent (18%-33%) [18-20]. To our knowledge, only one study has reported clinical features and prognostic factors in patients with bone metastases from HCC after LT [21]. He et al. reported frequent metastases in the thorax (43.4%), including thoracic vertebrae (15.1%), pelvis (24.5%), and lumbar spine (9.4%). The median survival after bone metastasis was 8.6 months, and the most common cause of death was liver failure due to hepatic decompensation or tumor progression.

This case demonstrates successful management of solitary spinal metastasis of HCC with TES after LT. A healthy liver after LT may enable successful treatment and improved outcomes, consistent with the observation that hepatic function correlates with survival [12]. Solitary spinal metastasis of HCC without other organ metastases may be an indication for TES when LT has controlled hepatic disease and liver function has improved.

## Key Points
- First reported case of successful TES for T7 metastasis after living donor LT for HCC.
- Given the poor prognosis of HCC with spinal metastasis, improved liver function after LT may enable successful surgical treatment.
- Solitary spinal metastasis of HCC may be an indication for TES if liver function is improved after LT.
