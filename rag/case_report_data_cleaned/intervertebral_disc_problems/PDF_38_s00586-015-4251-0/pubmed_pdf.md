# Cervicothoracic Spinal Cord Compression Caused by IgG4-Related Sclerosing Pachymeningitis: A Case Report and Literature Review

## Abstract

**Purpose:** To report a case of cervicothoracic spinal cord compression caused by IgG4-related sclerosing pachymeningitis (IgG4-RSP).

**Methods:** A 43-year-old male patient presented with 'neck pain for 15 days, exacerbated accompanying motor and sensory dysfunction of lower limbs with bowel and bladder dysfunction for 4 days' and was admitted to our department. Combined with the history of 'acupuncture treatment', MRI results, and rapid progression, we considered the great possibilities of spinal cord compression by intradural hematoma and performed an emergency operation of cervical double-door laminoplasty and thoracic decompression with internal fixation.

**Results:** After combined therapy of dexamethasone, mannitol, and neurotrophic drugs, sensory recovery of lower limbs started on the fifth day after surgery and became normal by the fourteenth day after surgery, with complete loss of muscle strength. Pathological examination strongly suggested the diagnosis of IgG4-related sclerosing pachymeningitis (IgG4-RSP).

**Conclusions:** IgG4-related sclerosing pachymeningitis (IgG4-RSP) is a newly recognized disease. This case of cervicothoracic spinal cord compression caused by IgG4-related sclerosing pachymeningitis has never been reported in China, with only three case reports worldwide. Prompt surgical decompression is recommended, and pathological examination is essential for diagnosis and comprehensive treatment.

**Keywords:** IgG4-related sclerosing pachymeningitis, Spinal cord compression, Immunohistochemical staining, Diagnosis, Surgery

## Introduction

The most common lesions that could lead to spinal cord compression are intraspinal primary or metastatic tumors and hematoma. To the best of the authors' knowledge, spinal cord compression caused by IgG4-related sclerosing pachymeningitis (IgG4-RSP) has never been reported in China, with only three case reports worldwide. This paper discusses a case of spinal cord compression caused by cervicothoracic IgG4-related sclerosing pachymeningitis.

## Materials and Methods

A 43-year-old male patient presented with 'neck pain for 15 days, exacerbated accompanying motor and sensory dysfunction of lower limbs with bowel and bladder dysfunction for 4 days' and was admitted to our department. The patient underwent splenectomy for ruptured spleen 15 years ago but was otherwise healthy and had no history of hypertension, diabetes, heart diseases, or infectious diseases. Due to the neck pain without obvious cause, the patient received 'acupuncture treatment' at a local clinic, which worsened the neck pain. Four days before admission, the patient developed motor and sensory dysfunction below the navel with bowel and bladder dysfunction. MRI at a local hospital showed an intraspinal strip-like lesion at C4–T2, with C4–5 and C5–6 disc herniation and spinal stenosis at the corresponding segments. The patient was admitted with a preliminary diagnosis of intraspinal space-occupying lesion, highly suspected to be hematoma compression.

On admission, the patient had clear consciousness and showed symptoms such as numbness below the navel, slight muscular hypertonia of the lower limbs, and reduced muscle strength in the upper and lower limbs. MRI at our hospital confirmed an intraspinal strip-like lesion with hypointensity on T1WI and slight hyperintensity on T2WI at C4–T2 level. Given the patient's history of acupuncture treatment and rapid progression, spinal cord compression by intradural hematoma was suspected. An emergency surgery of cervical double-door laminoplasty and thoracic decompression with internal fixation was performed. The lesion adhered tightly to the dura mater at C2–T3 level, and pathological examination suggested the possibility of an epidural metastatic tumor.

## Results

Postoperatively, the patient experienced sensory recovery of lower limbs starting on the fifth day after surgery, with full sensory function returning by the fourteenth day after surgery. However, muscle strength in the lower limbs remained completely lost. Pathological examination showed significant lymphocytic infiltration and many mature plasma cells. Immunohistochemical staining revealed CD3 (+), CD20 (+), CD38 (+), CD138 (+), LCA (+), Ki67 (30%), Pax5 (+), S-100(+), Kappa (+), and Lambda (+). Notably, IgG4-positive plasma cells accounted for over 40% of all IgG-positive cells, which strongly suggested the diagnosis of IgG4-related sclerosing pachymeningitis (IgG4-RSP). The patient, however, declined further ultrasound and whole-body MRI despite strong recommendations. Six months after surgery, the patient's motor and sensory function of the upper limbs had returned to normal, and the muscle strength of the lower limbs had improved to Class IV.

## Discussion

IgG4-related disease was first identified in relation to autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP), with Yoshida proposing the concept in 1995. The relationship between AIP and IgG4-positive plasma cells was introduced in 2001, and in 2003, Kamisawa recognized it as a systemic disease known as IgG4-related disease (IgG4-RD). In recent years, IgG4-RD has gained widespread recognition, and its understanding has significantly advanced.

IgG4-RD primarily affects older individuals, particularly males. It involves IgG4-positive plasma cells and is associated with storiform fibrosis and phlebitis. While the pancreas and bile ducts are commonly affected, other organs like the lacrimal glands, salivary glands, retroperitoneum, thyroid, kidneys, and lungs may also be involved. The disease often results in elevated serum IgG4 levels, and glucocorticoid therapy can provide some relief, though recurrence is common.

Diagnosis of IgG4-RD is challenging due to its varied clinical manifestations. Several diagnostic criteria have been proposed, including those by Japanese scholars in 2011. The criteria emphasize diffuse or focal enlargement or mass-forming lesions in one or more organs, elevated serum IgG4 levels, and characteristic histopathological findings such as prominent lymphocyte and plasma cell infiltration with fibrosis and IgG4-positive plasma cells. In our case, the abundance of IgG4-positive plasma cells and a ratio of IgG4/IgG-positive cells greater than 40% supported the diagnosis.

Spinal cord compression due to IgG4-related sclerosing pachymeningitis is extremely rare, with only a few case reports in the literature. Our case is one of the few worldwide, and surgical decompression is critical to preventing further neurological damage. With increasing awareness of IgG4-RD, clearer diagnostic criteria and more effective treatments are needed to manage this complex and rare condition.
