# Return of Motor Evoked Potentials After Knee Flexion in the Setting of High-Grade Spondylolisthesis

## Abstract

**Purpose** To present a case of loss of motor-evoked potentials (MEPs) to the left foot in the supine position after a partial reduction and instrumented fusion from L4 to the pelvis, which was managed successfully without revision or removal of implants.  
**Methods** We report a patient with high-grade spondylolisthesis who demonstrated loss of motor-evoked potentials after posterior spinal fusion and transfer to the supine position. The patient's knees were flexed to 90°, and signals were immediately restored. Systemic steroids were administered, and circumferential fusion was delayed 21 days. An anterior interbody cage was placed without complication.  
**Results** She was discharged on post-operative day 2. At 7 months, she was pain free and doing well, with plans to return to gymnastics completely.  
**Conclusions** Knee flexion can be instituted when encountering a neuromonitoring signal change following posterior spinal fusion for spondylolisthesis as a means to alleviate acute nerve stretch injury and may, in some cases, prevent the need to lessen the correction.  
**Level of evidence** IV.

## Case Presentation

A 9-year-old otherwise healthy female with a Meyerding Grade 3 spondylolisthesis at the level of L5/S1 presented with symptoms of radicular left leg pain and intermittent toe walking bilaterally. On physical examination, she displayed hamstring spasticity and a positive left straight leg raise test. MRI of the lumbar spine confirmed foraminal and canal stenosis.

## Review of Condition

In children and adolescents, spondylolisthesis is a common cause of low back pain. When considering in situ fusion versus reduction and fusion, a recent meta-analysis showed increased fusion rates with reduction without a significant overall incidence of neurologic injury, but could not provide definitive treatment guidelines [1]. The advantages of surgical reduction of a high-grade spondylolisthesis include improved fusion rate and sagittal alignment [2–5]. The addition of anterior column support with posterior instrumentation has been shown to help maintain reduction [6]. However, a retrospective comparative study reported a neurologic complication frequency of 21% [5]. While most neurologic injuries were transient, serious permanent injuries have also been reported [5].

We present a case of loss of motor-evoked potentials (MEPs) to the left foot in the supine position after a partial reduction and instrumented fusion from L4 to the pelvis, which was managed successfully without revision or removal of implants. Informed consent was obtained for presentation of this case.

## Procedure

Given the patient's activity level as a gymnast, a single-stage circumferential fusion was proposed. Intraoperative monitoring included somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEP), motor evoked potentials (MEPs), and electromyography (EMG). First, an instrumented fusion from L4 to the sacrum, wide L5 nerve root decompression, and partial reduction was performed with the patient in the prone position with flexion of the hips and knees. Following partial reduction, the L5 nerve roots appeared subjectively taut, and further reduction was not performed. The posterior procedure was carried out without changes in neuromonitoring.

The patient was then positioned supine for anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) with hips and knees extended on a flat table. After preparation and draping, but prior to incision, MEPs were noted to be decreased in the left tibialis anterior and abductor hallucis. These changes persisted despite adequate blood pressure, hemoglobin levels, and technical troubleshooting. To minimize stretch of the L5 nerve roots, the knees were flexed to 90°. Within minutes, the left foot MEPs normalized and neuromonitoring returned to baseline.

Due to concern for nerve injury and the technical difficulty of performing ALIF with knees flexed at 90°, the anterior procedure was aborted. A wake-up test demonstrated full strength and sensation in the distal extremities. In consultation with neurosurgery, intravenous methylprednisolone was administered for 24 hours. Postoperatively, the patient’s knees were flexed while in bed, though no restrictions were placed during physical therapy or ambulation. She was discharged on post-operative day 2 with unchanged serial examinations, including while standing upright and ambulating.

At post-operative day 6, completion of circumferential fusion was considered. However, the patient demonstrated toe walking and reported a “stretching” sensation in the posterior aspects of both legs. With knees extended, she could not dorsiflex her ankles beyond neutral. She was admitted and treated with systemic steroids for 24 hours with the knees flexed. Her neurologic examination returned to normal by hospital day 2, and she was discharged.

Twenty-one days from the index procedure, her examination remained normal and symmetric bilaterally. She then underwent ALIF at L5–S1. Two screws with washers were placed at L5 and S1 without complications or neuromonitoring changes.

## Rationale for Intervention

This case describes loss of MEPs, presumably due to acute L5 nerve stretch injury, following partial reduction and instrumentation for high-grade spondylolisthesis, compounded by supine positioning with extension of the hips and knees. Flexing the knees to 90° was associated with rapid normalization of neuromonitoring signals. Although causation cannot be definitively established, the immediacy of signal return and the anatomy of the L5 nerve root suggest knee flexion as the probable mechanism. As the L5 contribution to the sciatic nerve courses posterior to the knee joint, knee flexion minimizes nerve stretch.

Reduction with anterior column support has been shown to restore sagittal alignment and improve fusion rates, which supported its use in this young, athletic patient [2–4]. However, sagittal plane deformity correction carries a higher incidence of intraoperative neuromonitoring changes [7,8]. Neurologic deficits following reduction occur in a notable proportion of patients, with L5 nerve root injury being the most common in high-grade spondylolisthesis [9,10]. Consequently, careful preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative assessment of L5 nerve function is essential.

In this case, nerve irritation was likely related to tension from partial reduction, exacerbated by knee extension in the supine position. Prior biomechanical work has demonstrated that L5 nerve strain increases nonlinearly as reduction approaches completion, with the majority of strain occurring in the latter half of reduction [11].

The role of systemic steroids in acute nerve stretch injury remains uncertain. Based on available evidence, routine use is not recommended, particularly in the pediatric population [12,13]. However, given the recurrence of symptoms—including toe walking, posterior leg stretching sensations, and decreased dorsiflexion with knee extension—short-term steroid use was considered after weighing risks and benefits. These symptoms resolved with knee flexion alone.

Staging the anterior procedure after recovery from neuromonitoring changes was deemed appropriate, particularly given the viability of posterolateral fusion alone as an alternative treatment with reasonable outcomes [14,15]. Routine postoperative knee flexion following reduction of spondylolisthesis may be beneficial in minimizing L5 nerve stretch, both for prevention and management of neurologic symptoms.

This case highlights knee flexion as an additional tool when encountering abnormal intraoperative neuromonitoring changes or postoperative neurologic symptoms following reduction of spondylolisthesis. By relieving L5 nerve root stretch, knee flexion may, in select cases, prevent the need for revision or implant removal.

## Follow-Up

The patient was discharged on post-operative day 2. At 12 months, she was pain free, doing well, and planning a full return to gymnastics.
