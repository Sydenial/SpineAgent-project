# Major neurological deficit following anterior cervical decompression and fusion: what is the next step?

## Abstract

Major neurological deficit following anterior cervical decompression and fusion (ACDF) is a rare event, with incidences of up to 0.2% now reported. Post-operative MRI is mandatory to assess ongoing compression. In the past, the deficit has often been attributed to oedema or overzealous intra-operative manipulation of the cord. Reperfusion injury is a more recent concept. We describe a case of acute cervical disc prolapse with progressive neurology, and the difficult decision-making one is faced with when the neurological deficit continues to deteriorate post-ACDF.

## Case Presentation

A 30-year-old male was referred from the Emergency Department with sudden onset of left leg weakness and left arm numbness. He was referred to our Spinal Unit after exclusion of a cerebrovascular accident as the cause of his symptoms.

### Initial Assessment
On referral to the on-call team, an urgent MRI of the whole spine was arranged the same day. The neurology in the lower limbs was as follows: power in the left leg—3/5 in L2 and L3 myotomes and 3–4/5 in L4 and L5; whereas in S1 it was 4/5. The right leg power was 5/5. There was altered sensation in the left leg to both pinprick and light touch from L1–S1. There was no neurological deficit in the upper limbs. The peri-rectal examination was normal, consistent with an American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment Scale (AIS) grade D. There were also signs of cord compression in the form of hyper-reflexia in the legs and clonus.

### MRI Findings
The MRI scan showed a large disc prolapse at C6/7, which caused almost complete occlusion of the spinal canal. The disc component appeared to be acute on the MRI, rather than a chronic calcified disc. The next morning, the patient had developed right leg symptoms (MRC grades: 3–4/5 in L2–S1), and on the left, the weakness had progressed (MRC grades: 1–2/5 L2 and L3, 3–4/5 more distally). Generalized decreased sensation in the lower limbs was noted, but there was no neurological deficit in the upper limbs. The decision was made to proceed with C6/7 anterior cervical decompression and fusion (ACDF).

### Surgical Approach and Post-Operative Course
An urgent MRI after surgery revealed adequate removal of the disc prolapse, but concern remained about ongoing compression of the cord due to a constitutionally narrow canal. The case was discussed with a senior colleague, and a neurology opinion was obtained. A posterior decompression and stabilization procedure was decided upon to give the cord the best chance to recover. Post-surgery, the patient’s neurology started to improve over the next 48 hours.

## Procedures

### Anterior Cervical Decompression and Fusion (ACDF)
A routine ACDF at C6/7 was performed without significant complications. The patient was positioned supine with the head in a neutral position. Inline immobilization was performed at the time of induction. The standard Smith-Robinson anterior approach was used, and decompression was performed under the microscope. The posterior longitudinal ligament was excised, and three disc fragments were removed. The cord was observed to float upwards towards the vertebral bodies. A size 6 cage and size 16 screws were placed, with satisfactory implant positioning confirmed via image intensifier.

### Posterior Decompression and Stabilization
Posterior decompression and instrumented stabilization were performed with a Mayfield clamp to align the head and neck. Decompression of C5–7 was performed using lateral mass screws. Post-decompression, the cord was swollen and inflamed but pulsatile.

## Outcome/Follow-Up

The patient’s neurology slowly improved over the next 48 hours. By day two post-surgery, motor function in his legs had improved to MRC grade power 3/5 in L2-4 on the right and 4/5 distally. There was normal neurology in the upper limbs. Two months post-surgery, he was discharged from the rehabilitation unit. By three months, he had returned to work as an office-based engineer. Nine months post-surgery, the patient’s only complaint was mild paraesthesia in the left T1 dermatome, with normal sensation and power in the lower limbs.

## Historical Review of the Condition, Epidemiology, Diagnosis, Pathology, Differential Diagnosis

Post-operative major neurological deficits following anterior cervical spine surgery are well described in the literature, with the earliest papers now being nearly 50 years old. This is now an uncommon event following ACDF with the incidence described as being up to 0.2%. We could only find one case report on the subject detailing the difficult decision-making needed when faced with this problem in clinical practice.

### The Pathophysiology of Spinal Cord Injury
The AIS is a neurological classification system developed by ASIA and is determined following a standardized examination. It has been revised since its first publication and updated by the "International standards for neurological classification of spinal cord injury" (ISNCSCI). The classification is determined by the degree of residual motor and sensory function below a spinal cord lesion.

Differential diagnoses at this stage include incomplete decompression, either of the disc or posterior longitudinal ligament (PLL); epidural haematoma; cord oedema from manipulation intra-operatively, reperfusion injury, or vascular compromise/ischaemia. An urgent MRI is required to exclude ongoing compression, but the difficulty is interpreting it in a pressured hospital environment when faced with limb paralysis.

## Rationale for Treatment and Evidence-Based Literature

We decided to proceed with further posterior decompression in order to give this patient the best possible outcome. However, there are differing opinions on whether surgery is indicated. The difficulty lies in assessing what constitutes "satisfactory decompression of the cord"; there are no clear definitions in the literature. Post-operative MRI would have been useful at this stage to confirm adequate decompression, but it was not routine practice in our department.

The use of steroids in spinal cord injury and reperfusion injury is controversial. Several studies show conflicting results, despite the potential benefits from their anti-inflammatory properties. There are concerns regarding side effects, including gastric ulcers and infection. The use of steroids was initially started based on limited evidence suggesting their potential benefits, but advice from a regional spinal cord injury center led to discontinuation due to the limited evidence and potential risks.
