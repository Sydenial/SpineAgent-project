# Effects of spine loading in a patient with post-decompression lumbar disc herniation: observations using an open weight-bearing MRI

## Abstract
Purpose Our objective was to use an open weight-bearing MRI to identify the effects of different loading conditions on the intervertebral anatomy of the lumbar spine in a post-discectomy recurrent lumbar disc herniation patient.

Methods A 43-year-old male with a left-sided L5-S1 post-decompression re-herniation underwent MR imaging in three spine-loading conditions: (1) supine, (2) weight-bearing standing (WB), and (3) WB with 10% of body mass axial loading (WB+AL; 5% through each shoulder). A segmentation-based proprietary software was used to calculate and compare linear dimensions, angles, and cross-sections across the lumbar spine.

Results The L5 vertebra showed a 4.6 mm posterior shift at L5-S1 in the supine position that changed to an anterior translation >2.0 mm on WB. The spinal canal sagittal thickness at L5-S1 reduced from supine to WB and WB+AL (13.4, 10.6, 9.5 mm), with corresponding increases of 2.4 and 3.5 mm in the L5-S1 disc protrusion with WB and WB+AL, respectively. Change from supine to WB and WB+AL altered the L5-S1 disc heights (10.2, 8.6, 7.0 mm), left L5-S1 foramen heights (12.9, 11.8, 10.9 mm), L5-S1 segmental angles (10.3°, 2.8°, 4.3°), sacral angles (38.5°, 38.3°, 40.3°), L1–L3–L5 angles (161.4°, 157.1°, 155.1°), and the dural sac cross-sectional areas (149, 130, 131 mm2). Notably, the adjacent L4–L5 segment demonstrated a retrolisthesis >2.3 mm on WB.

Conclusion We observed that with weight-bearing, measurements indicative of spinal canal narrowing could be detected. These findings suggest that further research is warranted to determine the potential utility of weight-bearing MRI in clinical decision-making.

Keywords: Back pain; weight-bearing MRI; spine motion; lateral stenosis; segmentation; dural sac; positional MRI

## Introduction
Lumbar disc herniation (LDH) secondary to degenerative disc disease is common, with as many as 40% of adults with disc herniation experiencing LDH-induced low back pain (LBP) [1,2]. Although conservative treatment results in regression of the size of the herniated disc tissue in many cases, about 20% of LDH patients with unresolved radicular pain require surgical treatment [3,4]. Around 5–18% of LDH patients undergoing primary lumbar discectomy and decompression surgery develop recurrent lumbar disc herniation (RLDH), arising as a complication of the primary surgery [3,5,6]. Typically, RLDH is defined as a recurrence of herniation at the same site as the prior operation, or at the same level on the ipsi- or contralateral side, with at least 6 months of pain-free postoperative period [7,8].

Different imaging techniques have been used for evaluating primary or secondary disc herniation [9,10]. Specifically, gadolinium-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with fat saturation, T1- and T2-weighted turbo-spin echo and inversion recovery sequences have been used to detect the presence of extruding disc tissue [1,11]. Decisions on surgical intervention in LDH or revision surgeries in RLDH are mostly taken after evaluating the degree of encroachment into the spinal canal and the lateral foramen from protruding disc fragments, as determined by spine imaging in the supine position [3,8,12,13]. However, some studies have reported the use of positional MRI for quantifying dynamic changes in intervertebral anatomy in the supine, sitting, and standing positions in symptomatic lumbar stenosis patients [14–16].

While some positional MRI studies have investigated the effects of physiological spine loading on segmental anatomy and overall lumbar spine curvature in healthy individuals [17–19], fewer have examined changes in intervertebral morphology in symptomatic LDH patients after axially loading the spine [20–25]. These studies performed spine loading in the supine position using externally applied, MRI-compatible compression devices to analyze changes in spinal canal dimensions and shifts in disc protrusions. Although this external axial loading in the supine position simulated physiological spine compression and demonstrated noticeable dimensional changes compared with conventional supine MRI, some results were not in agreement with data from actual weight-bearing radiographic studies [8,20,26–28]. Very few studies have utilized positional MR imaging of the spine in weight-bearing positions in lumbar canal stenosis patients [15,16,24,29–33]. Although some weight-bearing MRI reports are available on non-LBP individuals [19,23,29] and on non-specific LBP cohorts [30,34], to the best of our knowledge, MR image analysis with different axial loading conditions of the lumbar spine in an RLDH patient has not been reported. Accordingly, our objective was to use an open weight-bearing MRI to identify the effects of different loading conditions on the intervertebral anatomy of the lumbar spine in a post-discectomy RLDH patient.

## Case Report

### Patient
A 43-year-old male (183 cm, 91 kg) presented with weakness in the left leg and foot involving the L5-S1 distribution. He had been diagnosed with a left L5-S1 herniation 2 years prior and had undergone a single-level, open discectomy with laminar decompression 1 year prior to reporting with symptom recurrence. On a Numeric Pain Rating Scale (1–10), he reported an average score of 7 for the last 7 days prior to the scan, with the pain being most aggravated in the supine position.

### MR Imaging Protocol
The patient was imaged using a G Scan Brio weight-bearing MRI (Esaote S.p.A., Genova, Italy), an open-field magnet (0.25 T) with the ability to rotate from supine to a fully upright, weight-bearing (WB) position in 3° increments. The patient was initially imaged in an upright, weight-bearing position (84° tilt of the gantry) with his feet shoulder-width apart, strapped securely at the pelvis. This position is recommended as a safe position that allows optimal WB of the spine without compromising postural stability [35]. Next, the patient was imaged in the supine position (0°). Lastly, the patient was imaged again in the WB position while additional loads equivalent to 10% of his body weight (9.1 kg) were supported over his shoulders, i.e., containers filled with 5% of body weight placed in two canvas tote bags, one bag (4.55 kg) over each shoulder (WB+AL).

Sagittal MR images of the entire lumbar spine were acquired at all three spine-loading conditions using a fast spin echo T2 sequence (TR=3520 ms; TE=125 ms; number of acquisitions=1; matrix=288x234; FOV=320x320; oversampling=185%; slice thickness=4 mm; gap=1 mm; acquisition time=4 min 41 s). Scanning time and sequences were similar to earlier studies and took less than 5 min at each position [30]. Pain scores of 6, 7, and 9 were recorded during imaging in the supine, WB, and WB+AL conditions, respectively. Additional images were acquired for purposes other than those specified for this study. The total time to situate the patient at different positions (allowing ~5 min before initiating each scan), acquire all images, with brief rests in between, was ~90 min.

### MRI Analysis
Images were transferred to a proprietary image analysis software system (OrthoCAD, Esaote S.p.A., Genova, Italy). The outlines of all lumbar and the first sacral vertebral bodies, including the spinal canal, were semi-automatically segmented across all image slices. One blinded investigator performed the segmentations. All images were inspected for disc protrusion, evidence of spinal stenosis, and lateral foramen narrowing. The software yielded volumetric contour models of the segmented vertebrae and spinal canal. Semi-automated quantification of linear, angular, and cross-sectional area (CSA) parameters was performed across all three loading conditions and compared. Parameters included:

- Intervertebral listhesis: distance between adjacent posterior corners of contiguous vertebrae in a motion segment.
- Intervertebral translation: distance between perpendicular projections to the centers of two adjacent vertebrae drawn from the median bisector of the segmental angle at a given level; evaluated relative to the baseline value measured in the supine position.
- Spinal canal sagittal thickness: maximum midsagittal diameter of the dural sac.
- Disc height: maximum distance between the superior and inferior endplates.
- Left intervertebral foramen height: height of the foramen demarcated over sagittal images.
- Intervertebral segmental angles: angle between endplates of adjacent vertebrae.
- L5-S1 segmental angle; lordosis angle (between superior plates of L1 and S1); sacral angle (between the horizontal plane and the sacral endplate); L1-L3-L5 angle (formed by the line joining the centers of L1, L3, and L5).
- Left intervertebral foramen CSA: vertical-plane sectional area of the lateral canals (area manually demarcated).
- Spinal canal CSA: sectional area in the axial plane.
- Sagittal-plane L5-S1 disc protrusion: increase from the supine baseline measured from the disc tip to perpendicular bisectors drawn to the adjacent L5-S1 vertebral corners with WB and WB+AL.
- Absolute vertebral translation: displacement (forward or backward) of the superior member of each vertebral segment with change from supine to WB and then to WB+AL; the supine intervertebral translation represents zero displacement. Negative values indicate backward translation.

## Results
Descriptive statistics for all evaluated parameters are displayed in Tables 1 and 2. A notable finding was a posterior shift of L5 (4.6 mm) on S1 in the supine position (intervertebral listhesis and translation). L5 shifted anteriorly on standing, and with axial loading (listhesis: 4.6 → 3.2 → 2.9 mm). In contrast, L4 at the L4-L5 junction demonstrated a posterior translation with WB and then a slight anterior shift with WB+AL (listhesis: 3.1 → 4.1 → 3.8 mm).

Other observations:
- Slight increases in spinal canal sagittal thickness at some levels were noticed on WB; however, marked narrowing occurred at L5-S1 on WB and WB+AL.
- Reduction in the L5-S1 left intervertebral foramen height (to <10 mm) with WB+AL.
- Acute shortening of the L5-S1 angle on standing and reduced disc height at the same level with WB.
- Compared to supine, the posterior midsagittal bulge of the protruding L5-S1 disc increased by 2.4 and 3.5 mm with WB and WB+AL, respectively.
- Left intervertebral foramen CSAs increased slightly with WB at all levels, whereas they were reduced to 98 mm2 at L5-S1. The spinal canal CSA demonstrated critical narrowing (>15 mm2) [28] at L5-S1 from supine to WB.

## Discussion
This work represents one of the first attempts to quantify changes in intervertebral morphology, lateral foramen, and spinal canal dimensions in response to physiological loading of the spine in the context of recurrent disc herniation. In the supine position, the posterior limit of the L5 vertebra demonstrated a prominent backward shift with relation to the S1 vertebral body, compared to WB and WB+AL. This posterior shift and accentuation of pain may have resulted from loss of dorsal support elements secondary to prior surgical decompression. Despite this shift, the spinal canal sagittal thickness and CSA were not altered in supine, possibly due to the absence of the laminae removed with the prior decompression.

Marked reduction in L5-S1 disc height on WB and the formation of bony spurs at the anterior edge of the inferior L5 end-plate may be suggestive of segmental instability [36]. Additionally, the adjacent L4-L5 segment demonstrated a paradoxical displacement pattern: WB resulted in a backward slide of L4 over L5, whereas additional loading with WB+AL resulted in an anterior translation of L4. Altered motion patterns at neighboring vertebral levels on loading may indicate early adjacent-segment pathology, as commonly evidenced with LDH or RLDH [24,37].

Studies with instrumented axial spine loading in the supine position have reported clinically significant (>15 mm2), multilevel narrowing of the spinal canal in a variety of LBP patients using MR imaging, often with reenactment of pain during loading [21,22,28]. The magnitude and duration of instrumented loading vary considerably, with axial loading up to 50% of body weight and durations between 5 and 50 min to affect quantifiable deformation of spinal canal anatomy [23,28]. In our case, notable narrowing of the L5-S1 spinal canal CSA, the left lateral foramen CSA, and the left L5-S1 intervertebral foramen height was demonstrated with ~10 min of WB. Only smaller changes were noticed with ~10 min of additional loading (WB+AL). Standing alone brought about critical changes in canal spaces and accentuation of disc protrusion. Since additional L5-S1 disc protrusion, spinal canal dimensions, and aberrant L4/L5 translations could be detected by comparing supine and WB images, WB MRI may be considered as an option for evaluating parameters required for clinical decision-making.

## Conclusion
With weight-bearing MRI, measurements indicative of spinal canal narrowing could be detected. These findings suggest that further research is warranted to determine the potential utility of weight-bearing MRI in clinical decision-making.
