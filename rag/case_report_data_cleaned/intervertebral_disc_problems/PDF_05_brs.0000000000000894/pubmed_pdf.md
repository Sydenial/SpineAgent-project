# Proximal Junctional Failure After Long-Segment Instrumentation for Degenerative Lumbar Kyphosis With Ankylosing Spinal Disorder

## Abstract

### Study Design
Case report.

### Summary of Background Data

Thus far, most studies have focused on the mobile junction as a site at which PJK/PJF can occur, and little is known about the relationship between PJK/PJF and ankylosing spinal disorders such as diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis.

### Results

PJF in the ankylosed spine may cause severe fracture instability and spinal cord deficit. The ankylosed spine should be integrated into the objective determination of materials contributing to the appropriate selection of fusion levels.

## Introduction

Proximal junctional failure (PJF) is an important complication that occurs subsequent to long-segment instrumentation for correction of adult spinal deformity. The phenomenon of PJF is distinct from proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK), in that PJF includes mechanical failure and/or spinal instability [1]. Thus far, many studies have focused on mobile junctions as a site at which PJK/PJF can occur [2,3], and little is known about the relationship between PJK/PJF and ankylosing spinal disorders such as diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH).

We report a case of PJF at the ankylosed junction, but not at the mobile junction, after segmental instrumented fusion for degenerative lumbar kyphosis with DISH. This case report suggests that PJF in the ankylosing spine may cause severe fracture instability and spinal cord deficit.

## Case Report

An 82-year-old female had been experiencing low back pain since 2007. She presented in June 2014 complaining of increasing low back pain. Neurological examination results were normal. Standing sagittal radiographs showed decreased lumbar lordosis. A lateral radiograph of the thoracic spine and a sagittal reconstructed computed tomographic scan revealed abnormal confluent hyperostosis in the anterior longitudinal ligaments and local reduction of trabeculae in the anterior vertebral body above Th10. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry showed a right femoral neck T score of −0.6, suggesting the absence of general osteopenia. The patient was diagnosed with degenerative lumbar kyphosis with DISH.

In October 2014, spinal instrumented fusion from Th10 to the sacrum was performed. Immediately postoperatively, radiographs demonstrated increased lumbar lordosis. Two weeks after surgery, severe thoracic back pain occurred while the patient attempted to rise from bed. Four weeks after surgery, she developed progressive lower extremity paresis and sensory loss below the midthoracic level. Imaging revealed fracture of Th10 and adjacent subluxation of Th9, confirming spinal cord injury caused by proximal junctional failure.

Revision surgery was performed with extension of fusion to Th5 and decompression at Th9–Th10. Th5 was selected as the new uppermost instrumented vertebra because local trabecular reduction was minimal at this level. Postoperatively, thoracic back pain improved progressively, but neurological function did not recover.

## Discussion

This case highlights two important clinical issues. First, PJF can occur at the ankylosing site adjacent to the uppermost instrumented vertebra after long-segment instrumentation for adult spinal deformity. Two primary ankylosing spinal disorders, DISH and ankylosing spondylitis, result in ankylosis of the spinal column. DISH, first described as senile ankylosing hyperostosis of the spine [4], is characterized by ossification of spinal and peripheral entheses. The prevalence of DISH in patients aged 50 years or older exceeds 15% [5,6], suggesting that ankylosis is common among patients with adult spinal deformity. Although risk factors for PJF such as advanced age, abnormal sagittal alignment, greater correction, and fusion to the sacrum have been reported [1,10], an association between ankylosing spinal disorders and PJF has not been well documented. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of PJF occurring specifically at an ankylosed junction.

Second, PJF in the ankylosing spine may cause severe fracture instability and spinal cord deficit. PJF includes both kyphotic deformity and structural failure of the vertebral body and/or posterior ligament complex. Fracture of the uppermost instrumented vertebra followed by adjacent vertebral subluxation can lead to significant neurological deficit due to spinal cord compression [11]. In patients with DISH, ankylosed spinal segments act as long lever arms, increasing fracture instability [12,13]. In this case, preexisting ankylosis above Th10 combined with long fusion from Th10 to the sacrum likely increased junctional stress, resulting in catastrophic failure.

Local reduction of trabeculae in the anterior vertebral body above the uppermost instrumented vertebra was also observed. Stress shielding due to load transfer through ossified ligaments may weaken the vertebral body [13], contributing to fracture despite the absence of systemic osteoporosis.

## Conclusion

PJF can occur at the ankylosing site above the uppermost instrumented vertebra after long-segment instrumentation for adult spinal deformity, and may result in severe fracture instability and permanent neurological deficit. Selection of fusion levels is critical in preventing PJK and PJF. Surgeons should recognize that ankylosing segments above the uppermost instrumented vertebra pose a significant risk for serious PJF. Fusion should extend beyond ankylosed segments when necessary, and proximal fixation should not terminate at vertebrae with trabecular weakening caused by stress shielding. Further studies are required to clarify the prevalence and prognosis of PJF in ankylosing spinal disorders.

## Key Points

- PJF can occur at the ankylosing site above the uppermost instrumented vertebra after long-segment instrumentation for adult spinal deformity.  
- PJF in the ankylosed spine may cause severe fracture instability and spinal cord deficit.  
- Ankylosing spinal segments should be considered carefully when determining fusion levels.
