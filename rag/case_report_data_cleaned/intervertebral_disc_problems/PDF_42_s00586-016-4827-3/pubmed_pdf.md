# Management of destructive Candida albicans spondylodiscitis of the cervical spine: a systematic analysis of literature illustrated by an unusual case

## Abstract
Purpose Candida-induced spondylodiscitis of the cervical spine in immunocompetent patients is an extremely rare infectious complication. Since clinical symptoms might be nonspecific, diagnostic delay can lead to permanent spinal cord damage, sepsis, and fatal complications. Surgical debridement is strongly recommended, but there is no standard antimycotic regimen for postsurgical treatment. This paper summarizes available data and demonstrates another successfully treated case.

Methods The systematic analysis was performed according to the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. PubMed and Web of Science were scanned to identify English-language articles. Additionally, the authors describe the case of a 60-year-old male patient who presented with Candida albicans–induced cervical spondylodiscitis after edematous pancreatitis and C. albicans sepsis. Anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion of C4–C6, additional anterior plating, as well as posterior stabilization C3–Th1 was followed by a 6‑month antimycotic therapy. There was neither funding nor conflict of interests.

Results A systematic literature analysis was conducted and 4599 articles on spondylodiscitis were scanned. Only four cases were found reporting C. albicans spondylodiscitis in a non‑immunocompromised patient. Our patient was followed up for 2 years. He remains free of symptoms and infection parameters. Standard testing for immunodeficiency showed no positive results.

Conclusion Candida albicans spondylodiscitis of the cervical spine is a potentially life‑threatening disease. To our knowledge, this is the fifth case in the literature that describes the treatment of C. albicans spondylodiscitis in an immunocompetent patient. Surgical debridement has to be considered; recommendations for antifungal regimens vary in agents and duration.

Keywords Spondylodiscitis; Candida albicans; Antibiotic treatment; Cervical spine; Infection

## Introduction
With only about 100 cases reported worldwide [1], spondylodiscitis of the cervical spine caused by Candida species is rare. The thoracic and lumbar spine are affected in 95% [2,3], and cases are usually found in immunocompromised patients [4]. Fungal spondylodiscitis is uncommon even in large series and usually accounts for 0.5–1.6%, and 6.9% of all spondylodiscitis cases in one report [5]. Another review found that Candida albicans was responsible for 62% of 59 cases of Candida species spondylodiscitis. However, cases caused by other Candida species—C. tropicalis, C. glabrata, C. krusei, C. lusitaniae, and C. parapsilosis—are increasingly prevalent [6]. Candida species spondylodiscitis is linked to catheter placement, drug abuse, parenteral nutrition, and opportunistic infections in immunocompromised patients [7,8]. The increasing use of invasive procedures such as insertion of central venous catheters, arterial lines, or Foley catheters has also increased the importance of Candida species spondylodiscitis after hospital-acquired septicemia [8,9]. Early diagnosis is crucial to prevent spread of infection, abscess formation, sepsis, and death; an estimated overall mortality of 15% has been reported [2]. Prolonged antifungal treatment is recommended, and surgical debridement has to be considered [1]. We report the fifth case of C. albicans spondylodiscitis of the cervical spine in an immunocompetent patient with no chronic disease or previous multiple trauma, followed by a systematic literature review.

## Case Report

### Clinical Course
A 60‑year‑old man presented with chronic neck pain. Two months previously he had undergone cholecystectomy for acute edematous pancreatitis and developed candidemia for which he received a 3‑week course of systemic fluconazole 400 mg/day. Shortly after discharge, he received a local Thai massage. Because of muscular tension and progressing neck pain, he presented to our outpatient clinic.

The patient had pressure pain over the middle and lower cervical spine without signs of inflammation or neurological deficits. CT and MRI showed spinal stenosis with destruction of C4–C6. He was immediately admitted. Blood tests showed marginally elevated inflammatory markers: C‑reactive protein (CRP) 4.2 mg/dl (<0.5 mg/dl), leukocytes 9.74 K/μl. No preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis was given because tissue samples were to be taken intraoperatively for microbiological analysis.

### Surgery and Postsurgical Course
Anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion (ACCF) was performed, followed by CT‑navigated dorsal stabilization from C3–C5 to C7–Th1. Microbiological analysis of intraoperative tissue samples revealed C. albicans spondylodiscitis, and intravenous liposomal amphotericin B (5 mg/kg/day once daily) was started. Four days later, kidney function deteriorated and hepatotoxic effects were recognized. Amphotericin B was discontinued, and intravenous caspofungin (70 mg/day) was initiated. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 10, and 2 months of intravenous therapy were completed on an outpatient basis. Therapy was then changed to oral fluconazole (400 mg/day) for an additional 5 months and subsequently discontinued. Thus, the patient received 7 months of antifungal treatment after surgery. He remained asymptomatic with normalized infection parameters through 2‑year follow‑up. Primary or secondary immunodeficiency was excluded by quantitative immunoglobulins including IgG subgroups, complete blood count, and lymphocyte subpopulations, all without pathologic findings.

## Methods
A systematic analysis according to PRISMA guidelines [10] was conducted to identify cases of Candida spondylodiscitis of the cervical spine in immunocompetent patients. Using the search term “spondylodiscitis,” one reviewer searched the PubMed and Web of Science databases on December 3, 2015. Publication dates were not limited. Only articles with abstracts in English were included.

## Results
Of 4599 titles and abstracts initially retrieved, 1461 duplicates were excluded. Publications involving immunocompromised patients or anatomic regions other than the cervical spine were eliminated. Only four reports met the inclusion criteria.

## Discussion

### Symptoms
Early Candida spondylodiscitis can present nonspecifically. Because of its rarity and failure to be considered, delayed detection can put the patient at significant risk. Chronic mechanical-type back and neck pain are described [4]. Immunodeficiency, prior candidemia, and use of steroids and antibacterial agents can aid earlier diagnosis. Fever is reported in 32% of patients. At later stages, radicular pain, neurological deficits (weakness, decreased sensation), hyperreflexia, and positive Babinski sign are reported. Fistulous connections from gastrointestinal tract or airway (mostly from malignant disease) can extend to the spinal cord [11,12]. Extra-spinal involvement including epidural abscess formation and psoas irritation can lead to additional symptoms.

### Diagnosis
Clinical symptoms are nonspecific and can be greatly delayed. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate, leukocyte count, and CRP are sensitive but nonspecific, and may be uninformative in immunodeficient patients [5,11]. Blood cultures are reported to be false‑negative in 24–50% of cases [9,13]. Conventional radiology shows only late erosive and destructive changes [14]. CT can show bone changes but no characteristic alterations for fungal spondylodiscitis [15]. MRI is the gold standard: vertebral bodies and discs typically present hypointense in T1 and hyperintense in T2 [16]. Contrast enhancement can improve sensitivity and specificity, particularly in early infections [5]. Some authors describe T2 hypointensity of the discs with normal intervertebral distance in Aspergillus and Candida infections compared with bacterial spondylodiscitis in three patients, but further cases are needed to confirm these findings [17]. Specific differentiation of subtypes based on MRI alone is difficult. Fluor‑18 fluorodeoxyglucose PET may help distinguish osseous from soft tissue infection [16]. Definitive diagnosis requires biopsy, histology, and susceptibility testing.

### Surgical Treatment
There are no guideline‑based recommendations for Candida‑induced spondylodiscitis in immunocompetent patients, and recommendations for immunocompromised patients are inconsistent. Conservative treatment can be considered in patients with overall critical condition and improving symptoms [3,18]. However, surgical revision was required in 33% after attempted conservative treatment in one series [8]. Minimally invasive percutaneous suction/aspiration has been unsuccessful in Candida spondylodiscitis [19]. Surgical debridement without stabilization is suggested in cases with recurrent bloodstream infections and progressive neck pain. Epidural abscess, spinal instability, progressive deformity, and neurologic deficits frequently necessitate debridement and stabilization, particularly in the cervical spine [8,16,20,21]. Goals include decompression, debridement, correction of deformity, pain reduction, and immobilization to allow fusion. Extensive debridement at the cervical spine is often achieved via a ventral approach; at thoracic/lumbar levels a minimally invasive endoscopic approach may be considered [22,23]. Osseous defects may be filled with a cage or autologous bone graft. In multisegmental defects, additional dorsal stabilization is recommended [16]. Isolated laminectomy or implantation of local antibiotic carriers is associated with high complication rates [16,24].

### Antimycotic Treatment
The optimal agent and duration for fungal spondylodiscitis are unknown, and no regimen exists specifically for immunocompetent patients. Susceptibility testing and targeted therapy are essential, as Candida species differ in susceptibility and resistance is evolving [2,25]. Rising minimum inhibitory concentrations with echinocandin use suggest developing resistance mechanisms [26], and resistance to several antifungals has been reported among common species [27]. Historically, amphotericin B was recommended first‑line in the absence of resistance [11], but due to nephrotoxicity, Infectious Diseases Society of America guidelines favor fluconazole for a minimum of 6–12 months or liposomal amphotericin B for several weeks followed by fluconazole for 6–12 months [18,21]. Similar recommendations are made by the European Society for Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases [28]. In our case, caspofungin was an effective alternative for initial intravenous therapy compared with amphotericin B. Adequate treatment duration after C. albicans sepsis may be essential to prevent secondary complications and to treat postoperative fungal spondylodiscitis.

Guideline‑based recommendations (summarized):
- C. albicans: fluconazole 6 mg/kg daily for 6–12 months, or lipid amphotericin B 3–5 mg/kg daily for several weeks followed by fluconazole 6–12 months [18].
- C. glabrata: echinocandin (e.g., anidulafungin, micafungin, or caspofungin) for 6–12 months; transition to azoles only if susceptibility confirmed [18].
- C. parapsilosis: fluconazole 6 mg/kg daily for 6–12 months [18].

### Clinical Decision Making
Progressive back pain with fever, elevated inflammatory markers (CRP, leukocytes, ESR), positive blood cultures, and neurological deficits is highly suggestive of spinal infection. History of recent spinal intervention, catheter infection, drug abuse, parenteral nutrition, bloodstream infection, or immunodeficiency warrants broad diagnostics [7,8]. MRI with contrast is the radiologic standard. If instability, cord compression, abscess, neurologic deficits, or progression are present, early surgical intervention should be considered. In the absence of these and with negative blood cultures, a CT‑guided needle biopsy should be performed [5,16]. Fungal infections may be missed if not specifically investigated; directed antifungal therapy is required for 6–12 months when indicated. If biopsies are nondiagnostic, repeat biopsy is advised. Empiric antibacterial treatment (when bacterial pathogens are suspected and cultures are pending/negative) typically includes vancomycin plus one of cefotaxime, ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, cefepime, or ciprofloxacin for 6–12 weeks [18,21]. Clinical follow‑up should be guided by laboratory findings; repeat imaging is reserved for suspected worsening.

## Conclusion
Candida albicans–induced spondylodiscitis is rare and usually occurs in immunocompromised patients [4]. Early suspicion, needle biopsy, and spine MRI can avoid delayed diagnosis and some surgeries [11,20]. We describe the fifth case in an immunocompetent patient without chronic disease or prior multiple trauma. Clinical symptoms are as nonspecific as in immunocompromised patients. MRI is the most sensitive method to detect spondylodiscitis but has limited capacity to distinguish bacterial from fungal infections [31]. Across four published immunocompetent cases [4,20,29,30], patients had chronic/repetitive underlying disease and/or prior septicemia with short antifungal therapy. Three of the four required surgical debridement and stabilization [4,20,30]. Long‑term antifungal treatment (at least 6 months) is crucial alongside surgery [16,28]. Fungal spondylodiscitis should be considered even in immunocompetent patients and included in early diagnostic strategies to reduce morbidity and mortality.
