# Management of a major atlanto-axial instability secondary to a lytic lesion of C2

## Abstract
Introduction: Management of C1-C2 instability is very challenging, especially when tumoral lesions are involved. Case report: We present the case of a 65-year-old male with a recently discovered small cell lung carcinoma, presenting progressive tetraparesis due to a secondary lesion involving the body of C2 with complete collapse of its anterior part and major C1-C2 instability in all planes. The patient underwent reconstructive surgery of the upper cervical spine during two sessions. First, an emergency surgery was done by a high anterior cervical approach, where reconstruction of the body of C2 was performed using a C3 body to odontoid long screw with bone cement filling around the screw at the level of C2, and an anterior buttress plate placed from C2 to C4. A posterior surgery was performed after 48 h to stabilize the spine with C1 to C5 instrumentation. The patient recovered from his neurological symptoms and underwent complementary adjuvant radiotherapy with chemotherapy later on. Conclusion: Literature is sparse on the treatment of major C1-C2 instability, especially when a secondary lesion is involved; this case shows a successful treatment strategy with an original technique that was not previously described. The patient was pain free at 1-year follow-up with a stable construct.

### Keywords
Atlanto-axial instability, C2, lytic lesion, tetraparesis

## Case Report
A 65-year-old man was recently diagnosed with a small cell lung carcinoma for which chemotherapy achieved good local control without the need for surgery. He presented to our emergency department 4 months later with a 1-month history of neck pain associated with tetraparesis. Physical examination revealed paresthesia of the upper and lower limbs with global motor weakness; the left upper extremity was most affected (3/5 muscle strength), and the other limbs were globally 4/5. A pyramidal syndrome was noted (positive Babinski sign), without bowel or bladder dysfunction. CT and MRI of the cervical spine revealed destruction of the body of C2 with major atlantoaxial instability. He was placed under traction with Gardner-Wells skull tongs (10 kg) with no reduction of the luxation in the emergency department (Fig. 1). Surgery was performed urgently the same day. He first underwent an anterior high cervical approach for reduction of the luxation, tumor excision, and anterior reconstruction and instrumentation from C2 to C4; 48 h later, a posterior approach provided complementary instrumented stabilization from C1 to C5.

### Diagnostic Imaging
CT scan reconstruction of the cervical spine revealed a lytic lesion of the body of C2, sparing the odontoid process, with complete collapse of the anterior part of the axis. There was major C1-C2 instability in the three spatial planes with complete C1-C2 luxation on the sagittal view, coronal translation to the left side, and rotatory dislocation to the left side (Fig. 2a-d). MRI showed significant compression at the atlanto-axial level due to the major instability and dislocation of the C1-C2 complex (Fig. 3).

## Introduction
Cervical metastatic lesions represent one quarter of all spinal metastases, with the most common primary tumor being breast cancer, followed by myeloma; pulmonary tumors represent only 8% of these primary tumors [1]. In one quarter of patients, the cervical lesion is the first manifestation of the malignant disease [2]. Metastatic deposits predominantly affect the vertebral body with a high rate of vertebral collapse, up to 73% [2], sometimes associated with a kyphotic deformity. Pain is usually the alarming symptom, but neurological symptoms are very common, with predominance of radicular symptoms over long-tract symptoms and a 10% rate of tetraparesis [2]. Plain X-rays (anteroposterior, open-mouth odontoid, lateral) may show an abnormal vertebral body in cases of a lytic lesion or collapse, and intervertebral instability can be detected; however, CT and MRI provide further information regarding the extent of bony lesions (CT) and the degree of spinal cord compression (MRI). They also help in preoperative planning when surgery is indicated; CT angiography can show the course and diameter of the vertebral arteries at C1 and C2, which is essential to determine the optimal operative procedure.

In cases of severe neck pain without neurological impairment, radiotherapy is the treatment of choice with effective pain relief [3]. Surgery is indicated in cases of neurological symptoms, major instability, severe pain with no response to classical oncological treatments, or an unknown primary tumor requiring stabilization and histopathological diagnosis.

In our patient, surgery was mandatory given the tetraparetic presentation and major C1-C2 instability. Histopathological examination of the intraoperative samples confirmed a secondary lesion from a small cell lung carcinoma.

## Rationale for Treatment
High cervical metastasis is very challenging surgically; the approach can be posterior, anterior, or combined depending on the target area for reconstruction and the degree of instability.

Posterior occipito-cervical instrumentation and decompression has been advocated as a good option in metastatic lesions of the high cervical spine [4], especially in cases of total destruction of the odontoid process [2]. Adding cervical pedicle screws can increase spinal stability and avoid an anterior column reconstruction [5]. Several papers have advocated the high anterior retropharyngeal (submandibular) approach [6-9], a proximal extension of the classical Smith-Robinson approach, providing exposure from the atlas to the subaxial cervical spine. The transoral approach in secondary lesions is not a safe or satisfying option given its morbidity, infection rate [10], and the difficulty in extending fixation down to C3 or C4. The transmandibular approach should also be avoided given its morbidity and the limited life expectancy of this patient population [11].

Anterior reconstruction can be achieved with a titanium mesh cage filled with bone cement, a specific C2 prosthesis [12], or by two screws placed in the dens with the C2 body defect filled with cement as described by Sjostrom [13]; the construct is typically completed by an anterior plate.

In major C1-C2 instability, combined anterior reconstruction with posterior instrumentation to the occiput [14, 15] or to C1 [16] is advised, in a single session or two separate sessions.

In our patient, a two-staged reconstruction and instrumentation was performed, with an anterior technique similar to the Sjostrom technique but using a long screw extending from the C3 inferior endplate to the odontoid process to create a more rigid anterior support. Posterior instrumentation spared the occipito-C1 joint to avoid unnecessary fusion and preserve postoperative quality of life [17].

## Operative Procedure
Under general anesthesia, closed manual traction maneuvers were performed using skull tongs, enabling reduction of the atlantoaxial luxation confirmed by image intensifier. A high anterior cervical (submandibular) approach provided access from C1 to C4. Intralesional resection of the C2 tumor was performed, including the anterior part of the inferior endplate; the posterior cortex and odontoid process were spared as they were uninvolved. Samples were sent for histopathology. The inferior endplate of C3 was then prepared (similar to preparing a C2 endplate for odontoid screw fixation), with a groove drilled into the anterior superior corner of C4. Under image guidance and direct vision, a K-wire was drilled across C3 and the C2 void and docked into the cortical bone of the proximal odontoid fragment. A C3-to-odontoid 4 mm/60 mm fully threaded cannulated screw was inserted to reconstruct the anterior column of C2. Polymethylmethacrylate cement was then filled around the screw between the odontoid process and the upper endplate of C3. An anterior cervical plate was modified (narrowed proximally) and slid between the anterior arch of C1 and the odontoid proximally, with two screws in C3 and two screws in C4 distally, acting as an anterior buttress for the reconstructed C2. A suction drain was inserted and the wound was closed.

For the second stage, the patient was placed in a Mayfield head holder in prone position. A midline exposure from C1 to C5 was performed. Instrumentation included lateral mass screws in C1, pedicle screws in C2, and lateral mass screws at C3, C4, and C5. No decompression was performed; two rods were placed and tightened.

## Clinical Outcome
The patient was discharged on day 14 with progressive recovery of tetraparesis. He wore a hard neck collar for 2 months. A postoperative CT scan verified implant positioning and quality of reduction (Fig. 5a-d). He subsequently underwent adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy. At 1-year follow-up (Fig. 6a-b), he had no neck pain or neurological deficit and no local recurrence.
