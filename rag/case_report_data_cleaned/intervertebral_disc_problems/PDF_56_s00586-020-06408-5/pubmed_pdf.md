# Delayed Presentation of Urinoma Mimicking Spondylodiscitis Secondary to Ureteric Injury Following Carrot Stick Fracture in Ankylosing Spondylitis

## Abstract

Introduction: Ureteric injuries are rarely associated with spinal trauma, with an incidence of less than 1%. Missed injuries can lead to urinoma collection, urosepsis, and even death.

Materials and Methods: A 75-year-old man presented 1 month following a fall with high-grade fever and severe back pain mimicking spondylodiscitis clinically. Plain radiograph showed features of ankylosing spondylitis with a suspicious transdiscal injury at L3–L4. Hyper-intense fluid within L3/L4 disk space communicating to a large psoas collection measuring $13\times6$ cm mimicking spondylodiscitis with abscess formation was observed in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). MRI with contrast enhancement demonstrated a leak through the left ureter into the psoas muscle, raising suspicion of a ureteric injury. Plain computerized tomography revealed a three-column fracture at L4, and a ureteric leak into the psoas collection with proximal hydronephrosis was seen after contrast administration, establishing the presence of a ureteric fistula resulting in urinoma.

Results: Following initial symptomatic improvement after ureteric stenting, the patient succumbed to urosepsis at 3 months.

Conclusion: Ureteric injuries in ankylosing spondylitis can present as spondylodiscitis. Clinicians need to be aware of the possibility of such injuries in hyperextension lumbar fractures in ankylosing spondylitis and treat them early to avoid urological complications.

Keywords: Urinoma, Ankylosing Spondylitis, Carrot Stick Fracture, Urosepsis

## Introduction

Ureteric injuries account for less than 1% of urogenital trauma and are usually associated with pelvic fractures. Their deep location in the retroperitoneal space and the protection offered by thick psoas muscles make them unlikely to get injured in spinal trauma. Missed ureteric injuries can lead to urinoma collection, and to our knowledge, this is the first report of post-traumatic urinoma due to hyperextension lumbar injury in a case of ankylosing spondylitis leading to chronic urosepsis and eventually death.

## Case Report

A 75-year-old man presented to the emergency department with high-grade fever (102°F) and severe low back pain for 2 days. Previous history revealed an acute episode of low back pain following a trivial fall 1 month earlier. A local orthopedic surgeon had managed it conservatively with lumbosacral orthosis and pain medications, presuming the absence of abnormalities in plain radiography. The patient had improved symptomatically and was ambulant without support. On clinical examination, tenderness over the lower lumbar spine with severe paraspinal spasm and high-grade fever was suggestive of spondylodiscitis. Neurological examination was normal. A review of the plain radiographic image taken at the time of the initial fall revealed a completely ankylosed spine with the classical "bamboo spine appearance," fused sacroiliac joints, osteoporosis, and a suspicious trans-discal break at L3–L4 disk. However, the patient was not aware of his clinical condition of ankylosing spondylitis and was not on any chronic immunosuppressive therapies.

With clinical suspicion of infective spondylodiscitis, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed, which showed a carrot stick fracture at L3/L4 disk, extending into the posterior L4 vertebral body and left L4 pedicle. Fluid noted within the L3/L4 disk space mimicking spondylodiscitis was seen communicating to a large psoas collection measuring 13×6 cm. The middle third of the left ureter was medially displaced and showed adhesion to the psoas collection anteriorly. Computerized tomography (CT) showed a classical three-column fracture disrupting the disk space anteriorly, vertebra in the middle column, and facet joint complex posteriorly. On contrast administration, a leak of the contrast into the psoas collection suggested the presence of a ureteric fistula. There was moderate proximal hydronephrosis due to narrowing at the level of the fistula/adhesion, and sagittal images demonstrated the impingement of the ureter between fracture fragments. The radiological picture was diagnostic of a psoas urinoma communicating to the disk space secondary to ureteric injury following hyperextension injury of the L3 vertebra.

He was transferred to the urology department, where ureteroureterostomy with stenting along with an omental flap around the ureteric injury was performed. Spine surgery was advised for the unstable fracture; however, it was refused by the patient and their relatives when the surgical risks were explained. In the postoperative period, after initial symptomatic improvement, he was discharged with antibiotics after 1 week of parenteral antibiotic therapy. Diagnosed with basal atelectasis at 1 month of follow-up, the patient was advised spine stabilization to avoid additional complications associated with recumbency. He was managed conservatively with chest physiotherapy and rehabilitation. This was followed by two visits to a local clinic for high-grade fever and respiratory distress within a span of 40 days, where he had refused a referral to a tertiary center and was under supportive management.

At 3 months following the index surgery, he was taken back to the emergency department for high-grade fever, delirium, respiratory distress, and reduced urinary output, and could not be resuscitated. The probable cause of death was sepsis and repeated episodes of pneumonia following the first episode of infected urinoma. An autopsy could have provided confirmation, which was unfortunately refused by the patient's relatives.

## Discussion

Ureteric injuries are rare as they are located deep in the retroperitoneum and are well protected by the pelvic structures, psoas muscles, and vertebrae. They can be missed easily and can have a high mortality rate of up to 17%. Urinary leaks in ureteric injuries can lead to a collection of extravasated urine, forming a retroperitoneal mass surrounded by a thick fibrous capsule known as urinoma. Urinoma has been reported as a rare complication following iatrogenic injury to ureters in retroperitoneal procedures and also following posterior spinal procedures such as lumbar spinal fusion and discectomy.

Post-traumatic urinoma following hyperextension injury in ankylosing spondylitis has not been reported.

Diagnosis of a urinoma is usually made through CT scans where the fluid collection opacifies following administration of contrast. The ureter lies adjacent to the aorta on the left and to the inferior vena cava on the right side, and its proximity to the L4–L5 disk space is crucial to understand the possibility of ureteric entrapment and injury in a case of ankylosing spondylitis due to a hyperextension mechanism. This can be easily missed, as in our case, where both the fracture and ureteric injury were not diagnosed initially, leading to the accumulation of urinoma, chronic urosepsis, and death.

Patients with rigid spines, such as those with ankylosing spondylitis (AS) and diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH), are susceptible to unstable three-column fractures following trivial injuries. However, up to 60% of these fractures can be missed in plain radiography. Further, elastic tissue dysfunction of the nearby visceral structures in these conditions predisposes them to adhesions and injuries following fracture displacements. Soft tissue injuries such as tracheal rupture, aortic injury, and diaphragmatic hernias have been reported following thoracolumbar fractures in AS, necessitating the use of advanced imaging such as CT and/or MRI to detect fractures and efficiently diagnose associated injuries.

Ureteral injuries have a subtle initial clinical presentation and can present later with complications such as urinoma formation, hydronephrosis, renal failure, urosepsis, and even death. Urinoma classification includes etiologies such as congenital, iatrogenic, spontaneous rupture of the ureteral wall, obstructive uropathy, and traumatic urinomas, the latter being very uncommon.

Appropriate and early diagnosis will provide the clinician with a chance for early drainage and ureteral repair. Due to vague symptoms and lack of literature on this clinical entity, urinomas are generally missed and can become infected, especially in elderly patients, as in our case of ankylosing spondylitis. This led to chronic urosepsis and pneumonia, and the patient eventually succumbed to the illness.

## Conclusion

Spinal fractures and associated visceral injuries in ankylosing spondylitis can be missed in plain radiographs. Ureteric injury associated with a spine fracture is rare, and if undiagnosed, it can lead to urosepsis and even death. A high index of suspicion is essential to avoid missing associated visceral injuries in ankylosing spondylitis. Advanced imaging such as CT and/or MRI is essential to diagnose urinoma and rule out other associated visceral injuries.
