# Reconstruction of the Upper Cervical Spine Using a Personalized 3D-Printed Vertebral Body in an Adolescent With Ewing Sarcoma

## Abstract
Study Design: Case report.
Objective: To describe a three-dimensional (3D) printed axial vertebral body used in upper cervical spine reconstruction after a C2 Ewing sarcoma resection in an adolescent boy.
Summary of Background Data: Ewing sarcoma is a malignant musculoskeletal neoplasm with a peak incidence in adolescents. Cervical spine as the primary site of the tumor has been related to a worse prognosis. Tumor resection is particularly challenging in the atlantoaxial region due to the complexity of the anatomy, the necessity for extensive resection according to oncological principles, and a lack of specialized implants for reconstruction. 3D printing refers to a process where 3D objects are created through successive layering of material under computer control. Although this technology potentially enables accurate fabrication of patient-specific orthopedic implants, literature on its utilization in this regard is rare.
Methods: A 12-year-old boy with a C2 Ewing sarcoma underwent a staged spondylectomy. Wide resection of the posterior elements was first performed. Two weeks later, a high anterior retropharyngeal approach was taken to remove the remains of the C2 vertebra. A customized artificial vertebral body fabricated according to a computer model using titanium alloy powder was inserted to replace the defect between C1 and C3. The microstructure of the implant was optimized for better biomechanical stability and enhanced bone healing.
Results: The patient had an uneventful recovery and began to ambulate on postoperative day 7. Adjuvant treatment commenced 3 weeks after the surgery. He was tumor-free at the 1-year follow-up. Computed tomography studies revealed evidence of implant osseointegration and no subsidence or displacement of the construct.
Conclusion: This case exemplifies personalized precision medicine in a surgical setting and demonstrates how 3D-printed, patient-specific implants may bring individualized solutions to rare problems wherein restoration of the specific anatomy of each patient is a key prognostic factor.

## Introduction
Ewing sarcoma is a malignant neoplasm that affects the long bones and the pelvis in most cases [1]. Cervical spine as the primary site of the tumor is uncommon and has been related to a worse prognosis [2]. Three-dimensional (3D) printing, also known as additive manufacturing, refers to a process wherein a 3D object is created from a digital model through successive layering of material under computer control. We describe a case of primary Ewing sarcoma in the C2 vertebra of a young patient who underwent spondylectomy and reconstruction using a personalized 3D-printed implant.

## Case Report
A 12-year-old boy was referred with severe neck pain that began after a minor injury while heading a ball in a soccer game 2 months earlier. The pain worsened despite oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. On admission, he reported 9/10 pain on the visual analog scale and wore a cervical collar for head support against gravity. He also complained of paresthesia and clumsiness of the intermediate and distal phalanges on both hands. History included asthma but was otherwise unremarkable.

On examination, lower-limb muscle strength was decreased (Oxford grade III–IV) with hyperactive patellar and Achilles tendon reflexes. No pathological reflexes were elicited. Plain X-rays demonstrated a lytic lesion with peripheral osteosclerosis involving the entire axis. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated paravertebral soft tissue involvement. Whole-body positron emission tomography revealed no metastasis. Immunochemical analyses of computed-tomography–guided percutaneous biopsy samples confirmed Ewing sarcoma. The Spine Instability Neoplastic Score was 13 [6]. Neurological function deteriorated during evaluation with worsening paresthesia and upper-limb weakness; the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score was 8 of 17 [7]. Given significant pain, progressive neurological impairment, and local instability, surgical intervention was pursued.

## Methods

### Surgical Planning
The tumor was staged as IIB according to the Enneking system [8] and Weinstein–Boriani–Biagini 1-12, A-D according to the Weinstein–Boriani–Biagini classification [9]. Because an extralesional en bloc spondylectomy at this level carried high risk [10], a staged intralesional spondylectomy was planned. First, radical excision of the posterior elements of C2 together with involved soft tissue would be performed extralesionally, without violating the tumor capsule. In surgery, a 7 x 4 x 3 cm mass was resected en bloc and pathology confirmed Ewing sarcoma. Two weeks later, a high anterior retropharyngeal approach would be used to remove the remaining C2 vertebra, decorticate the inferior articular surface of C1 and the superior endplate of C3, and insert a customized, self-stabilizing artificial vertebral body (SSAVB) to replace the cancerous vertebral body. No bone grafting was planned given the osteoinductive capability of the SSAVB. Informed consent was obtained.

### Design of the SSAVB
We previously reported the first generation of SSAVB in sheep [11]. The design was based on a porous metal scaffold providing anterior mechanical support while promoting bone in-growth. Electron beam melting, a 3D-printing technology, was used to fabricate the implant by successive layering of melted titanium alloy powder guided by a computer-aided design model. In the second generation described here, pore size and shape were fine-tuned based on prior results [13] to achieve an ultrastructural balance between strut dimensions and porosity that facilitates bone in-growth while maintaining cancellous-bone-like biomechanics.

The SSAVB was customized with proximal wings conforming to the inferior articular surface of C1, allowing greater proximity and maximal contact area and facilitating lateral mass screw access. The anterior surface had a zero profile to minimize dysphagia risk [14]. The inferior surface was tailored with a tilt matching the C3 upper endplate to reduce implant subsidence [15].

## Results
Staged surgeries proceeded as planned; however, during the second stage, caudal screws could not be placed into the C3 body because the screwdriver was blocked by the mandible in hyperextension. An ad hoc decision was made to use titanium wire for distal fixation. Before the second stage, a tracheostomy tube was placed due to the high incidence of pharyngeal complications and respiratory compromise associated with the anterior approach [16], and considering the history of asthma.

Postoperatively, the patient was managed in the intensive care unit and remained ventilator-dependent until postoperative day (POD) 4. Neurologic function improved, and he ambulated on POD 7 with a sternal-occipital-mandibular orthosis, later replaced by a cervical collar at 1 month. A nasogastric tube was placed on POD 8 to prevent aspiration in the setting of worsening cough; it was removed on POD 17 after cough control. Despite the high complication rates reported after upper cervical tumor surgery [16], the clinical course was relatively smooth. The JOA score was 10 of 17 at this point. Multiagent chemotherapy and local radiotherapy began after wound healing and recovery.

Follow-up at 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year showed no metastasis on positron emission tomography. Sagittal reconstruction images immediately postoperatively and at 1 year demonstrated implant osseointegration (bone in-growth into the built-in central vertical slit), no subsidence or displacement, and no local recurrence. The JOA score improved to 16 of 17. The patient reported marked symptomatic improvement without residual neurologic deficits and planned to return to school after the next cycle of radiation therapy.

## Discussion
Although multimodal, integrated therapies have improved the prognosis of Ewing sarcoma [7], the primary site remains a key predictor of clinical behavior [18]. Extensive tumor resection in the upper cervical spine according to oncologic principles improves survival [1] but creates some of the most challenging spinal reconstructions. Biomechanical studies of optimal reconstruction after C2 spondylectomy using conventional implants suggest both ventral cages and bone grafting are required to supplement dorsal instrumentation [2]. Local instability remains a concern with conventional titanium mesh cages [21], as the anterior arch anchor offers limited cancellous purchase and contact area with the atlas.

Bone graft is typically harvested from the iliac crest, with morbidities such as persistent pain and functional limitation [22], or obtained from substitutes (allograft/xenograft/synthetic) at increased cost. Advancing reconstruction in these patients requires a paradigm shift in cervical implant design. We investigated a precise, personalized implant manufactured via 3D printing.

Porous metal is a promising biomaterial for anterior cervical implants given its biomechanical stability, biocompatibility, and osteoinductive potential [23]. The SSAVB described here—fabricated by 3D printing—differs from conventionally manufactured implants in four major ways:
- Highly organized microstructure with uniform pore size/shape and continuous struts [11].
- Biomechanical properties (compressive strength and Young’s modulus) associated with reduced stress shielding and lower risk of subsidence [15].
- Greater pore density increases rough bone–metal interface area covered by melted titanium, potentially obviating bone grafting [13].
- Computer-aided, patient-specific geometry increases contact area and proximity between implant and graft site, adding stability and potentially reducing endplate fracture and collapse [26].

Specialized implants are sometimes required for uncommon reconstructive needs, yet development is often commercially disincentivized. With 3D printing, real-time creation of specialized implants has become a practical option. This case illustrates how 3D-printed, patient-specific implants can provide personalized solutions to rare problems in which restoration of unique anatomy is critical. Further study is warranted to validate 3D-printed implants in extensive spinal reconstruction and beyond. Utilizing the large internal surface area of the SSAVB for chemotherapeutic elution or placement of radioactive nanoparticles represents a potential avenue for future research.

## Conclusion
- This proof-of-concept demonstrates that 3D printing can produce precise, personalized implants for complex spinal reconstructions.
- Extensive tumor resection in the upper cervical spine per oncologic principles improves survival but creates some of the most challenging reconstructions.
- Porous metal is a promising biomaterial for anterior cervical implants due to biochemical stability, biocompatibility, and osteoinduction capability.
- The SSAVB, a porous metal implant made by 3D printing, differs from conventional implants in several aspects as detailed above.
