# Salvage of failed odontoid fixation through anterior C1/C2 transarticular screws

## Abstract

Odontoid fracture fixation allows for early mobilisation out of orthosis. Both anterior and posterior fixation techniques have been described, but anterior surgery has less postoperative morbidity through the use of natural cleavage planes. We describe the use of anterior transarticular stabilisation as a salvage procedure following a failed odontoid screw fixation.

## Case Presentation

A healthy 66-year-old male presented to the emergency department shortly after falling off a bicycle, complaining of neck pain and transient right upper and lower limb numbness lasting 20 minutes. He was wearing a helmet and hit his head against concrete. Following a basic examination, he was found to have a laceration to the forehead and mild neck tenderness on palpation. His neurological examination was reported as normal, and because of pre-existing stiffness in the neck following a fall 2 years previously, the limited neck range of motion was deemed insignificant. He was discharged home with no imaging.

He reattended the emergency department a few days later with persistent neck pain, limited range of motion, and altered sensation in the right upper and lower limbs with subjective weakness. Repeat examination revealed diffuse posterior cervical spine tenderness with reduced range of motion. Neurological examination showed right-sided upper and lower limb weakness (grade 4/5 in all myotomes) with altered sensation and a normal rectal examination. He was placed in a hard collar. X-ray and CT imaging identified a C2 fracture with C1/2 displacement, prompting referral to a regional spine unit.

After transfer, MRI of the cervical spine was performed. Following counselling, surgical stabilisation was deemed the preferred management for this unstable injury.

Under general anaesthesia, after reduction of the fracture, anterior odontoid osteosynthesis with a single central 38 mm × 4.5 mm cannulated odontoid screw was performed.

## Diagnostic Imaging

Cervical spine X-rays taken 4 days post-operatively demonstrated recurrent displacement of the odontoid, which was confirmed on CT scan. Neurological power had improved post-surgery and remained normal in all limbs; however, the fracture position was considered unacceptable. The patient was consented for revision fixation.

## Historical Review of the Condition, Epidemiology, Diagnosis, and Pathology

Odontoid fractures account for 9–18% of all cervical spine fractures and are the most common cervical spine fractures in the elderly [1]. They are typically caused by hyperextension or hyperflexion injuries. Anderson and D’Alonzo classified odontoid fractures into three types: type I, an avulsion fracture at the tip of the odontoid above the transverse ligament; type II, the most common, occurring between the transverse ligament and the base of the odontoid; and type III, extending into the vertebral body of the axis [2].

Grauer et al. later refined this classification to better differentiate type II from type III fractures. Type II fractures were defined as those located at or below the inferior aspect of the anterior C1 ring without extension into the superior articular facets of C2. Subtypes included type 2A (minimally displaced transverse fractures), type 2B (anterior-superior to posterior-inferior fracture lines or displaced transverse fractures), and type 2C (anterior-inferior to posterior-superior fracture lines or fractures with significant comminution) [3]. Based on this system, the patient sustained a type 2B odontoid fracture.

Although both conservative and operative treatments are described for type II fractures [4], surgical intervention has demonstrated superior clinical outcomes, higher rates of osseous union (78% vs 36%), and improved fracture stability on dynamic imaging compared with conservative management [1].

Several posterior surgical techniques have been described, including Gallie wiring [5], Magerl transarticular screws [6], Goel posterior C1 lateral mass and C2 pedicle fixation [7], and Harms and Melcher’s polyaxial screw-rod technique [8]. Despite fusion rates approaching 100%, these techniques are associated with risks such as vascular injury, cranial nerve injury, and approximately 50% loss of cervical rotation [4]. These concerns encouraged development of anterior odontoid screw fixation, initially described by Böhler [9].

Anterior odontoid screw fixation provides sufficient stability for early mobilisation while preserving C1/2 rotation [10]. Biomechanical studies suggest that a single 4.5 mm cannulated screw provides superior shear and torsional stiffness compared with two 3.5 mm screws [11], with similar fusion rates of approximately 83% [12]. In elderly patients, however, outcomes may be compromised by osteoporosis and fracture comminution [13,14].

## Rationale for Treatment and Evidence-Based Literature

Sen et al. described anterior C1/2 transarticular fixation via an anterior Smith–Robinson approach. Compared with posterior fusion, this technique offers easier positioning, less tissue trauma, and lower infection rates. Compared with Magerl’s posterior technique, the screw trajectory is considered safer relative to the vertebral artery, with comparable segmental stability [15].

In their series, screws were inserted from a starting point just below the anterior sulcus of C2, directed posteriosuperiorly at approximately 20° coronally and 30° sagittally to engage the subchondral bone of the C1 lateral mass. A 3-year follow-up of 22 patients treated with anterior C1/2 transarticular fusion demonstrated low complication rates, minimal blood loss, and immediate postoperative pain improvement [16].

In this case, failure of the initial odontoid screw fixation was attributed to underappreciated fracture comminution, which prevented maintenance of interfragmentary compression. Although posterior fusion was an option, revision anterior fixation supplemented with bilateral anterior transarticular screws was chosen to address the displaced anterior metalwork while avoiding the morbidity of posterior exposure.

## Procedure (Surgery, Intervention)

Revision surgery was performed under general anaesthesia with the patient supine. Bilateral anterior C1/C2 transarticular cannulated lateral mass screws (4.5 × 20 mm) were inserted under image guidance. The postoperative course was uncomplicated. A single drain was removed on the first postoperative day, and the patient was discharged fully weight-bearing in a moulded occipito-cervico-thoracic orthosis.

A size 8 endotracheal tube was used intraoperatively as a working channel for K-wire and screw insertion. Its bevelled end allowed atraumatic positioning on the anterior C2 lateral mass, and its radiopaque marker aided intraoperative imaging.

## Outcome and Follow-Up

Serial cervical spine X-rays were obtained postoperatively, and CT imaging at 3 months confirmed solid bony union without hardware loosening. The orthosis was removed at that time. At 1-year follow-up, the patient reported no neck pain, was satisfied with the outcome, and was cycling 60 miles per week. His Neck Disability Index was 2%, and his visual analogue pain score was 0/10.
