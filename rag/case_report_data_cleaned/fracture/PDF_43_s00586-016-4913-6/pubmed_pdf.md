# Migratory Low Velocity Intradural Lumbosacral Spinal Bullet Causing Cauda Equina Syndrome: Report of a Case and Review of Literature

## Abstract

**Background**: Migration of the bullet within the spinal subarachnoid space has long been recognized as an unusual complication of spinal gunshot injury.

**Objective**: We report a case of migratory low velocity intradural lumbosacral spinal bullet causing cauda equina syndrome. The relevant literature is reviewed, and all cases of migratory spinal bullets are summarized, and management strategies are discussed.

**Study design**: Literature review.

**Methods**: A 32-year-old male suffered an abdominal gunshot injury, for which emergency laparotomy and repair of colonic perforation were performed. The bullet was seen lodged within the sacral spinal canal behind the S1 vertebral body. The probable entry point was at the L2–L3 level. Caudal migration of the bullet within the spinal subarachnoid space led to the appearance of cauda equina syndrome.

**Results**: The bullet was retrieved following upper sacral and lower lumbar laminectomy. Prone positioning of the patient had led to cranial migration of the bullet at the L4 level, which was confirmed on fluoroscopy. The laminectomy had to be extended upwards with the patient in reverse Trendelenburg position for bullet removal.

**Conclusions**: Caudal migration of the bullet within the lumbosacral subarachnoid space results in cauda equina syndrome. Surgical retrieval of the bullet ensures the early recovery of neurological symptoms. Prone patient positioning can influence bullet location. Intraoperative fluoroscopy prior to skin incision is essential, in addition to preoperative imaging, to locate the bullet and thus avoid incorrect lower level laminectomy. Trapping the bullet after durotomy using suction and dissector in reverse Trendelenburg position is a useful aid in bullet removal.

**Keywords**: Spinal bullet, Intrathecal bullet, Migratory bullet, Spine trauma, Cauda equina syndrome.

## Introduction

Gunshot injuries to the spine, though not commonly encountered, can result in a range of neurological deficits depending on the location and extent of bullet impact [1, 2]. Migration of the bullet within the spinal subarachnoid space, resulting in the appearance of new onset or progressive neurological symptoms, is quite rare. We present the case of a 32-year-old male who suffered an abdominal gunshot injury with migratory intrathecal lumbosacral bullet leading to the appearance of cauda equina syndrome.

## Case History

A 32-year-old gentleman suffered a homicidal gunshot injury to the abdomen. Upon arrival in the emergency department, 24 hours after sustaining the gunshot injury, he was conscious but appeared pale. His pulse rate was 120/min, and BP was 90/70 mmHg. His abdomen was distended and tender. The entry point of the bullet was noted below the right costal margin. No exit wound was seen. He complained of paresthesias in both feet and radiating pain along both thighs. Neurological examination revealed a loss of sensation in bilateral L5, S1 dermatomes, and the perianal region. The anal tone was lax with absent bulbocavernosus reflex.

After the initial resuscitation, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen was performed, which revealed pneumoperitoneum, probably due to colonic perforation and associated retroperitoneal hematoma. The bullet was seen lodged behind the body of the S1 vertebra. Loculi of air were seen within the spinal canal at the level of L2–L3 vertebral body, which suggested the probable entry point into the canal. On exploratory laparotomy, ascending colonic perforation with massive fecal peritonitis was noted. A right hemicolectomy with ileotransverse anastomosis and drainage of the retroperitoneal hematoma was performed. The bullet tract could not be traced intraoperatively due to significant fecal contamination.

X-ray of the lumbosacral spine done on the third postoperative day revealed the bullet lying behind the S1 vertebral body. Upper sacral laminectomy was performed the next day, but the bullet could not be located at the S1 level. The position of the bullet was located using fluoroscopy. It was evident that the bullet had migrated upwards behind the body of L4. The laminectomy was extended upwards, and the reverse Trendelenburg position was given to prevent further upward migration of the bullet. On durotomy, the bullet was seen lying within the lumbosacral nerve roots. With suction placed at the upper end of the bullet to prevent further upward migration, the bullet was removed with a dissector without any injury to the nerve roots. Water-tight dural closure was performed.

Broad-spectrum antibiotic coverage was given for a period of 7 days. On follow-up, the patient had relief of paresthesia in both feet, improvement in perianal numbness, and had regained bowel continence.

## Discussion

Gunshot injuries to the spine account for 13-17% of all spinal cord injuries each year [2]. Gunshot injuries to the spine, though uncommon, can result in serious neurological deficits [3, 4]. Hyperkinetic projectiles, after entering the body cavity, travel in a straight line, exiting on the other side. Sometimes, these projectiles, following dissipation of energy, get lodged within the body cavity or spinal canal. These projectiles cause significant damage to surrounding tissue due to dissipation of both thermal and kinetic energy.

Most gunshot injuries cause acute neurological symptoms once the bullet gets lodged intrathecally. The extent of neurological symptoms depends on the site of bullet impact. Very rarely, spinal gunshot injury does not cause any neurological deficit. However, neurological symptoms may occur months or years later due to migration of the bullet intrathecally within the spinal canal. Migration of the bullet within the lumbosacral spinal subarachnoid space is caudal, assisted by gravity, respiration, and physiological cerebrospinal fluid circulation, trapping the bullet at the narrowest point of the sacral canal.

Cephalad migration is prevented by the natural narrowing of the spinal canal at the D10 level and above. However, few cases of caudal migration of the bullet from the cranium, cervical, or upper thoracic region to the lumbosacral region have been reported. The appearance of cauda equina syndrome correlates with this downward migration. During surgery, the prone positioning of the patient can lead to cranial migration of the bullet, which can be confirmed using intraoperative fluoroscopy.

Management of gunshot wounds to the spine is debated. Surgical treatment needs to be individualized, considering patient hemodynamic factors, associated injuries, and the location of the bullet. Proponents of conservative treatment cite neurological recovery without removing the bullet. Surgical retrieval of the bullet is generally advocated to prevent worsening or new neurological symptoms due to bullet migration, meningitis, or formation of reactive epidural fibrosis. Early bullet retrieval facilitates recovery, as axons in this region have the ability to regenerate.
