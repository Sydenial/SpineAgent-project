# Repeated posterior dislocation of total hip arthroplasty after spinal corrective long fusion with pelvic fixation

## Abstract

Purpose: Several reports have indicated that anterior dislocation of total hip arthroplasty (THA) can be caused by spinal degenerative changes with excessive pelvic retroversion. However, no reports have indicated that posterior dislocation can be caused by fixed pelvic anteversion after corrective spine surgery. We describe a rare case of repeated posterior THA dislocation that occurred 5 months after corrective spinal long fusion with pelvic fixation.

Methods: A 64-year-old woman had undergone bilateral THA 13 years before presenting to our institution. She had been diagnosed with kyphoscoliosis and underwent three subsequent spinal surgeries after the THA. We performed spinal corrective long fusion from T5 to ilium with pelvic fixation (with iliac screws). Five months later, she experienced severe hip pain when she tried to stand up from the toilet and was unable to move, due to posterior THA dislocation. Closed reduction was performed, and her left hip was easily reduced. However, she experienced 5 subsequent dislocations.

Results: We hypothesized that posterior dislocation could occur after spinal corrective long fusion with pelvic fixation due to three mechanisms: (1) a change in the THA cup alignment before and after spinal corrective long fusion surgery, (2) decreased and fixed pelvic posterior tilt in the sitting position, or (3) the trunk's forward tilting during standing-up motion after spinopelvic fixation.

Conclusions: Spinal long fusion with pelvic fixation could be a risk factor for posterior THA dislocation.

## Introduction

Dislocation is one of the most important complications after total hip arthroplasty (THA). The orientation of the acetabular cup during THA is important for preventing postoperative dislocation. Pelvic tilt directly affects cup orientation, and poor cup orientation can cause subsequent dislocation. Pelvic tilt varies according to posture, age, and other factors, and posterior pelvic tilt typically increases with age. This progression is usually gradual, due to the loss of lumbar lordosis, weakness of the back muscles, flexion contracture of the knee, and other factors. Progressive posterior pelvic tilt may cause anterior instability and dislocation of the THA. However, there are no reports indicating that posterior THA dislocation can be caused by fixed pelvic anteversion after spinal corrective long fusion with pelvic fixation. We report a rare case of repeated posterior THA dislocations that occurred 5 months after spinal corrective long fusion with pelvic fixation.

## Case Report

A 64-year-old woman presented to our outpatient clinic with acute left hip pain. She had no history of hip disease during her childhood but had undergone bilateral THA via the posterior-lateral approach for secondary hip osteoarthritis due to acetabular dysplasia 13 years before her presentation. Eleven years after the THA, she had severe low back pain and was diagnosed with degenerative kyphoscoliosis of the spine. She underwent spinal fusion from L3 to L5 at another institution, but her lower back pain did not improve. At that point, we diagnosed her back pain as due to lumbar kyphosis and spinopelvic malalignment and performed spinal corrective fusion from T12 to the ilium. However, proximal junctional kyphosis subsequently occurred, and her back pain worsened. Therefore, we extended the proximal spinal fusion from T12 to T5 as the final spinal fusion, which ranged from T5 to pelvis. Five months after the third spinal surgery, the patient experienced severe hip pain when attempting to stand up from the toilet and was unable to move by herself, requiring transport to our hospital.

## Physical Examination

Our physical examination revealed that the left hip joint was flexed, adducted, and internally rotated. She had severe left groin pain and was unable to move her left leg. However, her left lower limb exhibited no neurological impairment.

## Radiographic Evaluation

Hip radiographs revealed posterior dislocation of the left THA. A standing whole spine X-ray before the THA dislocation showed significant improvement in the sagittal vertical axis (SVA) after the spinal corrective long fusion with pelvic fixation. The sacral slope (SS) had changed anteriorly from 1° to 23° before and after the spinal corrective long fusion with iliac screws. 

Computed tomography was performed to examine the THA implant's orientation. The stem's anatomical anteversion was 13.7°. The cup's radiographic anteversion had been reduced from 30.2° to 14.2° after spinal corrective long fusion with iliac screws. Based on these findings, we speculated that the combined anteversion had been reduced, contributing to the THA dislocation.

Closed reduction was performed under sedation. The left hip was successfully reduced under fluoroscopic guidance. During the procedure, we found that the soft tissue tension was not loose, despite the posterior dislocation.

## Discussion

The interrelationship between the spine and hip has been defined as hip-spine syndrome. Studies have shown that sagittal spinal alignment in patients with severe hip osteoarthritis is linked to gluteus medius weakness in those with low back pain. Pelvic tilt affects acetabular cup orientation, and poor pelvic alignment can lead to dislocation. After spinal fusion surgery, the pelvis often becomes fixed in an anterior tilt, which can reduce acetabular anteversion, thereby increasing the risk of posterior dislocation. We have identified three potential explanations for the posterior THA dislocation in this case: (1) a change in the THA cup alignment due to spinal corrective fusion, (2) decreased pelvic posterior tilt and fixed anteverted pelvis in the sitting position, and (3) a forward bending trunk motion during standing up, leading to anterior impingement and posterior dislocation.

## Conclusion

We report a rare case of repeated posterior THA dislocation after spinal corrective long fusion with pelvic fixation. This spinal procedure may be a risk factor for posterior THA dislocation. Both hip and spine surgeons should be aware of this risk and discuss it with patients preoperatively.
