# A unique procedure of joined transoral and retropharyngeal high cervical approach (JTRC) without mandibulectomy for treating upper cervical neoplasm involving both C2 and C3

## Abstract

**Purpose** To investigate a unique procedure of joined transoral and retropharyngeal high cervical approach (JTRC) without mandibulectomy for treating upper cervical neoplasm involving both C2 and C3.

**Methods** A 23-year-old male patient had neoplasm involving C2 and C3 that caused pathologic fracture of C2 and bony destruction of C3. The neoplasm excision and cervical spine reconstruction were performed through the JTRC approach without splitting the mandible. In this approach, there were two surgery windows that could be applied in turns by closing or opening the mouth to gain an ideal exposure. By rotating the mandible, maximized exposure could be obtained for either surgery window. In addition, there was an overlap zone between the transoral approach and high cervical retropharyngeal approach that could eliminate the blind area behind the mandible.

**Results** All procedures were successfully performed, and the surgical incisions healed without infection. By operating alternatively between the two surgery windows, neoplasm excision and cervical spine reconstruction involving both C2 and C3 were successfully performed. Pathologic results showed metastatic renal cell cancer to the resected cervical tumor and confirmed the patient’s diagnosis of tuberous sclerosis (Bourneville disease). A 3-month postoperative cervical spine radiography and CT scan demonstrated favorable placement of the bone implant as well as favorable instrumentation.

**Conclusion** The JTRC approach could provide good surgical exposure for treating disorders involving both upper and lower cervical spine without splitting the mandible.

**Keywords** Standard transoral approach (STOA) · Transmandible approach by mandible splitting (TMA) · Retropharyngeal high cervical approach (RHC) · Joined transoral and retropharyngeal high cervical approach (JTRC)

## Background

In patients with disorders involving the craniocervical junction or upper cervical spine, the standard transoral approach (STOA) could expose lesions from the lower part of clivus to the C2/3 disk [1, 2], providing direct access for tumor resection or screw insertion. However, in cases involving C3 or distal cervical vertebrae, the common transoral approach fails to offer enough exposure for surgery due to anatomic limitations from the tongue and mandible.

An expanded transoral approach, transmandible combined with anterior cervical approach by mandible splitting (TMA), could provide more extensive exposure from clivus to lower cervical spine compared with the standard transoral approach, and be used to surgically treat patients with osseous metastases involving both upper and lower cervical spine [3–6]. However, this technique-demanding approach is usually associated with postoperative complications including mandibular osteotomy nonunion, occlusal disturbance, sensitivity disturbance, and carotid artery thrombosis. Additionally, the high morbidity due to the huge surgical wound has limited its wide clinical use.

In this report, we introduce a new method to deal with pathologies involving both upper and lower cervical spine without the need to split the mandible. We name it the “joined transoral and retropharyngeal high cervical (JTRC) approach”, where the mandible is kept intact and does not require surgical splitting during osseous metastases resection and reconstruction.

## Case report

A 23-year-old male patient presented with severe neck pain. Neurological examination revealed no paresis, normal deep tendon reflexes, and full power in both upper and lower extremities. Neck CT (computed tomography) scan revealed destructive osseous lytic lesions in C2 and C3: a pathologic fracture crossed the base of odontoid and extended into the C2 vertebra; another small round lytic lesion was located in the posterior C3 vertebra body. The posterior cortex of C2 vertebra had been destroyed. Additionally, there were sclerotic metastases in C4 vertebra body and spinal processes of C2, C3 and C4.

T2-weighted magnetic resonance images (MRI) of the cervical spine in the sagittal plane demonstrated neoplasm involving both C2 and C3 vertebrae bodies, while C2 collapsed from pathologic fracture and expanded around, with a long T2 signal in the front of C2–4 indicating hemorrhage. Axial T2 image showed a mass lesion destroying the body of axis and invading into the spinal canal, touching the thecal sac without compression. Bilateral vertebral arteries were not invaded by the metastatic lesion and passed through the groove normally.

Additional MRI of abdomen revealed a retroperitoneal tumor, which appeared to originate from the left kidney. Fine-needle biopsy of the cervical spine lesion and retroperitoneal mass both demonstrated vascular leiomyoma with low-grade malignant potential. Thus, the patient was diagnosed with tuberous sclerosis (Bourneville disease) associated with pathologic fracture of C2.

In view of the possibility of low-grade malignancy of the cervical lesions and the anatomical complexity in the upper cervical region, en bloc resection was not chosen. We decided to use piecemeal excision and anterior autograft bone implant with plate fixation combined with posterior instrumentation and fusion.

## Surgical procedures

Our surgical procedures included: (1) removal of the ventral lesion and reconstruction of cervical spine stability via an anterior approach; (2) occipitocervical fusion via a posterior approach.

The patient was placed in supine position. Nasal intubation was performed after general anesthesia induction. The surgical field was prepared with 10% povidone iodine and sterile drapes. A Codman retractor was used to expose the oral cavity and a fiberoptic light to ensure visualization of the posterior pharyngeal wall. A straight incision was made along the midline of the pharyngeal wall. A monopolar electrocautery was used to strip the bilateral longus colli for sufficient exposure of the anterior arch of C1 and anterior part of C2.

The axis was invaded by tumor and bulged into the surrounding soft tissue. A pathologic fracture could be seen at the base of odontoid. A rongeur was used to remove the tumor piece by piece, and special attention was paid to avoid damaging the vertebral artery. The anterior arch of atlas was kept intact, while the axis body, most part of dens, and the C2/3 disk were resected thoroughly by piecemeal under the transoral surgery window. However, C3 was hindered by the tongue and mandible and was out of reach via this approach. Subsequently, the Codman retractor was taken off and the patient’s mouth was closed. Another high cervical retropharyngeal incision was made at the right side of the neck to create another operation window for exposure of C2–5 vertebrae.

A long straight incision along the sternocleidomastoid muscle was made, and the platysma muscle was dissected. A gap between the carotid artery sheath and esophagus-trachea sheath was detected and retracted outward, allowing access to C3 and C4 vertebrae. After ligation of the superior thyroid artery and lingual artery, the circum-tongue soft tissue was retracted upward as far as possible with a retractor or periosteal detacher to gain access to the C2/3 gap. The hypoglossal nerve, lingual nerve, esophagus, and carotid artery were carefully protected. Then, the remaining tumor in C3 was removed thoroughly.

After that, a perfectly shaped bone bulk from the ilium was harvested and implanted into the tumor excision cavity carefully, spanning from the anterior arch of C1 to the C4 body. A properly sized titanium plate was chosen for fixation. After the anterior plate was applied and secured into place through the high cervical window, two locking screws were inserted into the anterior arch of C1 and body of C4 by different surgery windows, respectively. All procedures were performed smoothly as we successfully detoured the mandible for surgical procedures from C1 to C4 and avoided an otherwise huge wound brought by mandible splitting (TMA).

The incision at the pharyngeal wall was closed in two layers, and the high cervical incision was also sutured after a drainage tube was placed. After vertebrectomy of both C2 and C3, we worried about mechanical insufficiency with anterior instrumentation. Then the patient was turned to prone position, and posterior instrumentation and fusion were added.

## Results

All procedures were successfully performed, and the patient was sent to intensive care for 24 h. After extubation, the patient was transferred to the ordinary unit. The patient received 7 days of nasogastric tube feeding without complications. The surgical incisions healed with no infection. At 10 days after surgery, the patient started a rehabilitation program.

Pathologic results of the resected cervical tumor showed metastatic renal cancer and confirmed the patient’s diagnosis of tuberous sclerosis (Bourneville disease). The patient then received rapamycin for further treatment. He was discharged on postoperative day 14 with a rigid cervical collar. Postoperative cervical spine radiography and CT scan demonstrated favorable placement of the bone implant as well as favorable instrumentation. At 3-month follow-up, there was no displacement or loosening of the instrumentation on CT and radiography, and the patient recovered to work.

## Discussion

When dealing with lesions in the upper cervical region, there are three kinds of anterior surgical approaches:

1. **Standard transoral approach (STOA)**, commonly chosen for ventral lesions located between the clivus and the upper cervical vertebra. This approach can provide direct access to lesions, allowing excellent decompression of the ventral medulla and upper cervical spinal cord. Via this approach, resection of neoplasms, release and/or reduction for atlantoaxial facet joint, and even atlantoaxial fixation with plate can be performed [7–9]. However, for cases with lesions extending into the lower cervical spine, the standard transoral approach fails to provide adequate exposure due to constraint by the mandible.

2. **Retropharyngeal high cervical approach (RHC)** [10, 11], recognized as an extension of the common anterior cervical approach. This approach can be used for removal of lesions involving the atlas and axis. Compared to the transoral approach, surgeries via this approach are less likely to be complicated by infection. However, it has limitations of awkward trajectory, restricted depth of exposure, and indirect midline access to the upper cervical spine. Especially when the mandible is small or in a low position, the mandible may hinder complex procedures involving the upper cervical spine.

3. **Transmandible combined with anterior cervical approach by mandible splitting (TMA)**, an enlarged transoral approach by splitting the mandible to further expose the lower cervical spine. This approach is commonly applied to treat ventral lesions involving both upper and lower cervical spine [12–15]. Although good exposure can be achieved, it is not widely accepted due to high incidence of complications, including dysphagia, dysphonia, dysmasesia, nonunion of the split mandible, and cosmetic problems. After surgery, patients’ quality of life is poor; they may require nasogastric tube nutrition for a long time until fusion of the split mandible.

Thus, we explored a novel method to achieve extensive exposure for both upper and lower cervical spine without splitting the mandible, thereby avoiding these complications.

In this report, we introduced the JTRC approach, meaning that by this approach the mandible is kept intact and does not require surgical splitting while performing neoplasm excision and reconstruction similarly to TMA.

In our surgical procedure, two surgery windows were established: a transoral operation window and a high cervical retropharyngeal operation window. Surgical procedures could be shifted between the two windows as needed to obtain ideal exposure.

There seems to be a relative blind area under the mandible, but it can be avoided by switching the two windows in turn. When operating within the mouth, the mandible opens downward; when switching to the high cervical window, the mouth is closed. The mandible can be pushed upward as far as possible, working like a door that sways around the mandibular joints. Through this mechanism, maximized exposure for either surgery window can be obtained. An overlap zone could be accessed by both upper and lower surgery windows, leaving no blind area between them. Although the overlap zone may appear like a tunnel, it is adequate for procedures.

When working in the high cervical window, the superior thyroid artery and lingual artery require ligation. The hypoglossal nerve, lingual nerve, esophagus, and carotid artery are important structures that require careful protection. There is no need to expose the nerves deliberately; the carotid artery and esophagus can be retracted softly to provide enough room for exposure.

In the overlap zone, the work trajectory is still somewhat awkward compared with the mandible splitting approach. When the neoplasm invades surrounding soft tissue extensively, this JTRC approach may not be optimal for performing en bloc resection, or when the pharyngeal wall needs to be closed with a free flap, which is a limitation.

In summary, the advantages of JTRC include (1) smaller incision and less invasion than TMA; (2) better cosmetic results, as there is no incision or scar on the face; (3) better postoperative quality of life than TMA, with more rapid recovery to normal eating. The outcomes in this patient indicate this new method is favorable for improving quality of life, decreasing incidence of complications, and achieving better cosmetic result than MSTO. Contraindications may include mouth-opening disability, restricted neck extension, poor condition of the mandible with small size or hypoplasia, and oral infection.

Our preliminary experience suggests that the JTRC approach may provide good surgical exposure for treating disorders involving both upper and lower cervical vertebrae without splitting the mandible.

With the development of endoscopic techniques and the design of the “fibroscope”, endoscopy has been routinely performed in neurosurgery. Recently, endoscopic endonasal, transoral and transcervical approaches have been developed as promising alternatives to traditional transoral microsurgery to the craniocervical junction (CVJ) [16, 17]. If combined with endoscopic assistance, JTRC could be a more minimally invasive and convenient method for treating a variety of pathological conditions that affect the craniovertebral junction.
