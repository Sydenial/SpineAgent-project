# Scissors stab wound to the cervical spinal cord at the craniocervical junction

## Abstract

### Background Context
Stab wounds resulting in spinal cord injury of the craniocervical junction are rare. A scissors stab wound to the cervical spinal cord has only been reported once in the literature.

### Purpose
To report a case of Brown-Séquard-plus syndrome in an 8-year-old boy secondary to a scissors stab wound at the craniocervical junction.

### Study Design
Case report and review of the literature.

### Methods
The case study of an 8-year-old boy who was hospitalized because of a scissors stab wound at the craniocervical junction is presented. The patient developed Brown-Séquard-plus syndrome on the left side of the body. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a laceration of the spinal cord at the craniocervical junction with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage. Careful cleansing and interrupted sutures of the wounds were performed to prevent CSF leakage. Rehabilitation therapy was initiated 2 days later.

### Conclusion
Treatment of scissors stab wounds to the cervical spinal cord, whether conservative management or thorough surgical exploration, should be individualized based on history, examination, and imaging. Despite conservative management, complete recovery, which was unexpected, was attributed to initial mild laceration of the spinal cord and ipsilateral spinal cord functional compensation.

## Introduction

Scissors stab wounds resulting in spinal cord injury at the craniocervical junction are rare [2,13]. There is only one large review of such injuries published from Cape Town by Peacock et al. [13]; most of the remaining data have come from case reports. According to the review conducted by Peacock et al., 5% of spinal cord injuries have been reported to result from sharp injuries, and 84.2% of these sharp injuries resulted from stabbing. Scissors stab wounds to the cervical spinal cord have only been reported once before by Karadag et al. [7], which was a case of penetrating cervical spinal trauma with an ipsilateral vertebral artery injury.

We report a unique case of an 8-year-old boy with a scissors stab wound at the craniocervical junction that resulted in Brown-Séquard-plus syndrome.

## Case Report

### History and Examination

An 8-year-old boy was accidentally stabbed in the neck by scissors, which were thrown as a dart by his little brother while playing. The boy immediately fell to the ground and could not stand up. He presented to the emergency department of a local hospital, where the wounds were superficially debrided and closed. The patient was then transferred to our hospital for further treatment.

At admission, blood pressure was 110/65 mmHg, heart rate was 96 beats/min, respiratory rate was 20/min, and arterial oxygen saturation was 100%. Physical examination revealed two sutured skin wounds (approximately 0.9 cm on the right and 1.3 cm on the left) with minor seeping of blood and exudate in the occipitocervical region. Neurologic examination showed motor weakness of the left upper and lower extremities (grade 0/5). Sensory disturbances of vibration and positional sensation were present on the left side below the C1 level. Impaired perception of pain and temperature stimuli were noted on the right side below the C1 level. The patient had absent abdominal reflexes, brisk limb reflexes, bilateral ankle clonus, extensor plantar responses, and urinary retention. These findings were consistent with Brown-Séquard-plus syndrome caused by a disturbance to the left side of the high cervical cord with additional contralateral involvement [14].

### Imaging

T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a linear high-signal lesion within the spinal cord at the craniocervical junction. A fluid collection in the paravertebral region at the level of C2 was also identified, consistent with CSF leakage along the trajectory of the penetrating object.

### Management

The patient was managed conservatively. Careful cleansing and interrupted sutures of the wounds were performed to prevent CSF leakage. After wound closure, the patient received a glucocorticosteroid protocol (NASCIS II), prophylactic broad-spectrum antibiotics, anti-dehydration therapy, and neurotrophic drugs. Rehabilitation therapy was initiated 2 days after injury.

### Post-injury Course

On the fourth day of admission, muscle strength of the left upper limb and left lower limb improved to grade 1/5 and grade 2/5, respectively. Muscle strength continued to improve gradually during hospitalization. Sensory and motor function returned to normal levels by the 27th post-injury day. At discharge, the patient had a stable gait, muscle strength of grade 5/5 in the left limbs, and restoration of bladder function. At 8 months post-injury, follow-up examination revealed complete neurologic recovery.

## Discussion

Stab wounds to the spinal cord are rarely reported. Injuries caused by sharp objects such as knives, screwdrivers, penetrating drill bits, fishing harpoons, spear guns, bicycle spokes, garden forks, pencils, and glass fragments have been described [1,6,10,11,13,16–18,21,22,24]. Scissors stab wounds to the spinal cord have only been reported once by Karadag et al. [7], who described a case involving dural penetration and compression of the spinal cord within the subarachnoid space rather than direct laceration.

With respect to stab wounds at the craniocervical junction, the instrument rarely penetrates the occipital squama due to its oblique orientation and thickness. Instead, it typically slides along the undersurface of the occipital bone and is deflected into the atlanto-occipital or atlanto-axial interspace [2]. In the present case, the blade entered obliquely through the atlanto-occipital interspace and partially severed the spinal cord without significant bony or ligamentous injury.

Scissors differ from other penetrating instruments in that spreading the blades may reduce penetration depth. Additionally, one blade has a sharp edge while the other is relatively blunt. In this case, the sharp side lacerated the spinal cord, whereas the blunt side displaced it. MRI findings suggested that more than half of the cervical cord tracts were preserved.

Brown-Séquard-plus syndrome refers to Brown-Séquard syndrome accompanied by additional neurologic findings such as bladder dysfunction [14,20]. The neurologic findings in this patient were consistent with this diagnosis. There was no evidence of vertebral artery injury or bony fragments on imaging. Given stable neurologic deficits and absence of ongoing CSF leakage or bleeding, surgical exploration was not indicated. Conservative management with wound closure and rehabilitation resulted in complete recovery, consistent with prior reports that incomplete neural injury often shows significant improvement [9,13].

## Conclusion

Treatment of scissors stab wounds to the cervical spinal cord, whether conservative management or thorough surgical exploration, should be individualized based on history, examination, and imaging. Despite conservative management, complete recovery was achieved in this case, likely due to mild initial laceration of the spinal cord and ipsilateral spinal cord functional compensation.
