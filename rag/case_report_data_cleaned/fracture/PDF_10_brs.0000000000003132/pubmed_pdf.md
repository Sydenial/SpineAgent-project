# A Rare Spinal Injury: Gunshot Wound to the Odontoid Process

## Abstract

**Study Design.** Case report.  
**Objective.** We present an 18-year-old male patient with a gunshot injury through the right scapular region, with the bullet ending up and causing a fracture in the odontoid process.  
**Summary of Background Data.** Odontoid fracture owing to gunshot wound is an extremely rare injury.  
**Methods.** An 18-year-old male patient was brought to the emergency department with a gunshot injury. X-ray imaging showed a retained bullet in the odontoid process. Computed tomography scan showed fracture of the odontoid and body of the scapula consistent with the trajectory of the bullet. Owing to a normal neurological examination, conservative follow-up was decided and a Philadelphia collar was applied.  
**Results.** At 2 years’ follow-up, the patient demonstrated limited axial rotation and flexion/extension. However, no pain or neurological problems were observed.  
**Conclusion.** Odontoid fracture owing to gunshot wound can be treated conservatively; emergency or elective surgical intervention was not necessary. During follow-up, the patient did not demonstrate any signs of neurological impairment or infection.  
**Key Words.** Bullet, cervical spine, collar, gunshot wound, odontoid fracture.

## Introduction

Vertebral and spinal cord injuries owing to firearms have a high rate of morbidity and mortality and are most commonly seen in young males, accounting for approximately 20% of traumatic spinal injuries. Cervical spine injuries, in turn, comprise up to 20% to 30% of all vertebral gunshot wounds [2].

Owing to close proximity to the spine and vertebral arteries, gunshot injuries of the cervical vertebrae can be life-threatening [3]. We present an 18-year-old male patient with a gunshot injury through the right scapular region, with the bullet ending up and causing a fracture in the odontoid process without neurological impairment. We aim to share our experience with this very rare and potentially devastating injury.

## Case Report

### History

An 18-year-old male patient was brought to the emergency department with a gunshot injury. The patient was shot from outside his car while running from law enforcement.

### Physical Examination

The patient’s vital signs were stable and within normal ranges upon presentation. Physical examination revealed one entry hole in the lateral right thigh with a corresponding exit hole in the medial thigh, and one entry hole in the right scapular region with no corresponding exit hole. No further injuries were found on examination. The patient had no neurological or circulatory deficits.

### Radiological Examination

X-ray imaging showed a retained bullet in the odontoid process. Computed tomography angiography demonstrated a fracture of the scapular body and a fracture of the odontoid process consistent with the trajectory of the bullet. The vertebral arteries were intact.

### Treatment

The patient was given a Philadelphia collar. Bullet entry and exit wounds were debrided and left to heal secondarily. The patient was admitted and started on first-generation cephalosporin and aminoglycoside prophylaxis. Neurological examination was repeated at regular intervals and remained stable. Owing to a normal neurological examination and the possible catastrophic complications of invasive procedures, conservative follow-up was chosen after discussion with the patient and his relatives. A simple arm sling was prescribed for the scapular fracture.

### Follow-up

Range-of-motion exercises were initiated. The bullet entry wounds healed without complication. The patient was followed for 12 weeks with a Philadelphia collar for the odontoid fracture. He did not report neck pain, and neurological examinations remained normal at each follow-up visit. At the end of 12 weeks, a control cervical CT scan was performed, and the collar was discontinued. Neurological examination and infection markers, including C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate, were normal.

At the 6-month follow-up, the patient was pain-free. X-ray and CT scans demonstrated no signs of instability. Axial rotation and flexion/extension of the neck were limited. Physical therapy was initiated to increase range of motion.

### Final Visit

At the 2-year follow-up, CT scans demonstrated no signs of instability. However, the patient continued to demonstrate limited axial rotation and flexion/extension. Axial rotation was 60° to both sides, and lateral flexion was 30° to both sides. Clinical examination revealed cervical flexion and extension were limited to 50° and 15°, respectively.

## Discussion

There is currently no consensus on the surgical treatment of cervical firearm injuries. Complications of cervical gunshot wounds include vascular and neurological injuries, problems in fracture healing such as malunion and nonunion, and infection [4]. Injuries to the vertebral arteries can be potentially devastating [5]. Although emergency surgical intervention is not usually indicated, delayed spinal cord decompression, stabilization, or debridement may be required in cases of hemodynamic instability or excessive hematoma.

Neurological injuries are common in cervical gunshot wounds. Gunshot injuries to the vertebrae may cause neurological damage through direct or indirect mechanisms. Even in the absence of direct spinal injury, the concussive effect of the bullet may cause neurological impairment [3]. Infection is also a potential risk due to soft tissue injury [2].

In neurologically and vascularly intact patients, bullet removal remains controversial. Some authors advocate removal to prevent rare late infections or delayed neurological deficits, whereas others argue that removal may cause neurological deficits or other complications due to surgical manipulation or the void left by the bullet [6]. The clinical relevance of bullet toxicity or neurotoxicity remains debated.

In the absence of vascular and neurological injury, the decision to operate on cervical spine gunshot fractures is usually based on fracture type and spinal instability. However, instability is rare, and uncomplicated cervical gunshot injuries requiring surgical intervention are uncommon. Most cervical vertebrae injuries reported in the literature are stable [5]. Kupcha et al. [2], in a case series of 28 patients, reported that routine cervical dissection was not beneficial because none of the patients had unstable injuries. Medzon et al. [4] reported 81 patients with nonmilitary firearm injuries to the head and neck; only 19 (23%) had vertebral fractures, and only three required surgical stabilization. Mechanical instability is uncommon because civilian gunshot injuries are generally low-energy injuries.

In our case, neither emergency nor elective surgical intervention was necessary. During follow-up, the patient did not demonstrate neurological impairment or infection; however, loss of cervical range of motion was a notable complaint.

## Conclusion

Odontoid fracture owing to a gunshot wound is an extremely rare injury. In the absence of neurological or vascular deficits, conservative treatment with external immobilization can be effective. Although some loss of cervical range of motion may persist, satisfactory clinical outcomes without surgical intervention can be achieved.
