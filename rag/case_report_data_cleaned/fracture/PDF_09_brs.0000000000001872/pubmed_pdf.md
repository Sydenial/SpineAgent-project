# Management of Unusual Atlantoaxial Dislocation

## Abstract

Study Design: A case report and review of the literature.  
Objective: The aim of this study was to describe the successful treatment of one posterior atlantoaxial dislocation without fracture and to review the relevant literature.  
Summary of Background Data: Posterior atlantoaxial dislocation without fracture of the odontoid process is extremely rare. Management of these patients is still unknown.  
Methods: A posterior atlantoaxial dislocation without fracture in a 58-year-old man with incomplete quadriplegia was treated surgically with posterior atlantoaxial pedicle screws internal fixation and fusion after closed reduction. The images, treatment, and related literature are reviewed.  
Results: The patient had complete recovery of neurologic deficit and bony fusion of the atlantoaxial joint was identified on the follow-up computed tomography taken 3 months after posterior fixation. To our knowledge, no case of posterior atlantoaxial dislocation with neurologic deficit has been previously reported in English medical literature.  
Conclusion: We described a rare case of posterior atlantoaxial dislocation with neurologic deficit. Treatment procedure of posterior atlantoaxial dislocation was presented.  
Key words: management, neurologic deficit, pedicle screws, posterior atlantoaxial dislocation.  
Level of Evidence: 5

## Introduction

Posterior atlantoaxial dislocation (PAAD) without fracture of the odontoid process is extremely rare; only 12 cases have been reported in the literature. These 12 cases had no or mild neurological deficit. In the current report, the authors present a rare instance of PAAD with neurologic deficit caused by a traffic accident. This case was treated by closed reduction without anesthesia, and pedicle screws internal fixation via the posterior approach was used.

## Case Report

A 58-year-old man was admitted with a 3-day history of incomplete quadriplegia, which manifested immediately after a traffic accident. Neurologic examination showed paresthesia below the shoulders and decreased muscle strength in arms and legs. The patient complained of considerable pain and stiffness in the neck. His neck was skewed to the right. The patient, with normal vital signs, was immobilized in a soft collar brace and transferred to our university hospital from a local county clinic.  
Plain radiographs of the cervical spine showed a posterior dislocation of the atlas with respect to the axis and soft tissue swelling around the neck. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated apparent compression of the spinal cord, with no intramedullary cord signal abnormality found at the level of PAAD. The images showed abnormal signal intensity from cervical (C) 1 to C5 vertebrae on T2-weighted and T1-weighted images.  
Axial computed tomography (CT) scans revealed the odontoid peg lying ventral to the anterior arch of the atlas, with no fracture of the anterior arch.  
The patient was put on occipital-mandibular leash traction first. The initial 3 kg weight was gradually increased to 6 kg under fluoroscopy. At this stage, the atlas had been positioned over the axis. The procedure was performed without anesthesia, and the patient could report his sensory changes during the process. He did not experience neurological deterioration. Pain in the neck was relieved once the reduction was done.  
Halo-vest fixation was applied to prepare for the posterior surgery under local anesthesia. Reduction of the atlantoaxial dislocation was preserved.  
The patient showed significant improvement over 11 days. Neurologic examination showed complete recovery of muscle strength and sensation. Two weeks after the halo-vest fixation, the patient complained about neck pain. The instability of the atlantoaxial joint was found by fluoroscopy. Surgery was then performed using a standard posterior midline approach with pedicle screw internal fixation for atlantoaxial arthrodesis. Postoperative imaging revealed that the constructs were satisfactory, and MRI confirmed that there was no compression of the spinal cord. A solid bony union of the atlantoaxial fusion mass was achieved. The patient remained asymptomatic and had no neck pain at the most recent follow-up 10 months after the operation.

## Discussion

Movement of the atlantoaxial joint is limited by the C1-C2 articulation, ipsilateral transverse ligament, and contralateral alar ligament. PAAD without fracture is a rare injury, and several authors have suggested that such an injury would tend to cause severe distraction of the spinal cord and immediate death. Only 12 patients with PAAD who survived have been reported.  
The mechanism of PAAD is likely high-grade hyperextension with variable amounts of distraction, often caused by high-energy injuries such as traffic accidents or earthquakes. Older patients, particularly those with cervical disc degenerative disease, may have less tolerance to such injuries. Most patients surviving PAAD have been younger, and interestingly, nearly all cases were male, potentially due to irritability and driving habits in the male population.  
Except for our case, all other patients with PAAD did not have definite signs of neurologic deficit, which can be explained by Steel's Rule of Thirds. This rule proposes that the space available for the cord (SAC) in the level of the atlas is about one-third of the spinal canal. If the posterior dislocation reduces the canal area by less than 36%, it may be sufficient to avoid cord compression. In our case, despite the dislocation occupying over the tolerance of SAC, the patient developed incomplete quadriplegia.  
PAAD is a rare injury, and treatment procedures are unclear. We suggest that the dislocation be reduced by closed reduction initially. It is better to perform this technique while the patient is awake and without anesthesia, especially for patients with neurologic deficit. While closed reduction can reduce surgical harm, it increases the risk of spinal cord compression and endorhachis avulsion. A careful, slow approach should be followed, and the process should be stopped immediately if the patient reports discomfort.

## Conclusion

We described a rare case of posterior atlantoaxial dislocation with neurologic deficit. Closed reduction is suggested as the first-line treatment. In cases where there is a concern about residual instability or incomplete reduction, pedicle screws internal fixation via a posterior approach may provide sufficient rigid stability.

## Key Points

- This is the first case illustrating a rare case of posterior atlantoaxial dislocation with neurologic deficit.
- Complete recovery of quadriparesis was followed by reduction. Bony fusion of the atlantoaxial joint was identified after posterior fixation.
- The level of C1-C2 is a vital center of the body. Treatment of posterior atlantoaxial dislocation is crucial for managing such injuries.
