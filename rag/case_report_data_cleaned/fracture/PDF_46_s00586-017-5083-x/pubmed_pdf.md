# Traumatic Multiple Cervical Spine Injuries in a Patient With Osteopetrosis and Its Management

## Abstract
Study design: Single case report.  
Objective/purpose: To report multiple-level fractures of the cervical spine in a patient with osteopetrosis and its management.  
Summary of background data: Osteopetrosis is a rare inherited condition characterized by defective bone remodeling resulting in hard, brittle bones with diffuse osteosclerosis. Fractures of the spine are rare compared to the common long bone fractures. We report a case of traumatic multiple-level fractures of the cervical spine in osteopetrosis and its management, which has rarely been reported previously.  
Methods: A 17-year-old boy presented with severe neck tenderness and restricted range of motion following a trivial injury in a swimming pool. Neurological examination was normal, and he was diagnosed with autosomal dominant osteopetrosis on evaluation. Imaging findings, clinical course, and treatment are described.  
Results: Radiological evaluation revealed multiple-level fractures of the cervical vertebrae with endplate sclerosis. The patient was managed with cervical skeletal traction in appropriate extension for 6 weeks followed by a hard cervical collar for another 6 weeks. Follow-up radiographs at 18 months and 2.5 years showed healed fractures with no residual instability or symptoms.  
Conclusion: This case illustrates the rare occurrence of multiple-level cervical spine fractures following trivial neck trauma in a patient with osteopetrosis and successful conservative management.  
Keywords: Osteopetrosis; Multiple fractures; Cervical spine; Conservative management.

## Introduction
Osteopetrosis is a rare condition characterized by marked radiodensity of bones throughout the skeleton [1] and poor remodeling due to dysfunction of osteoclast-mediated bone resorption. As with many hereditary skeletal disorders, it is not a single disease but a syndrome with several variants [1]. Broadly, it is categorized into an infantile malignant autosomal recessive form and an adult benign autosomal dominant form. The infantile form worsens rapidly and, in the absence of effective medical intervention, leads to death in the first few years of life [1]. The autosomal dominant form is usually asymptomatic, and many patients are not diagnosed until the appearance of a bone fracture [2].

We discuss the rare occurrence of multiple traumatic cervical vertebral fractures in a patient diagnosed with osteopetrosis following injury and its management. After an extended search, we found no similar case of traumatic multi-level fractures of cervical vertebrae in osteopetrosis reported in the English language literature. It was therefore considered worth reporting such a complex cervical injury in an osteopetrotic patient, its treatment, and outcome. The aim of describing this case scenario and technical tips is to help readers faced with similar problems.

## Case Report
A 17-year-old boy presented to the emergency department following a trivial injury. He was swimming in a pool when another person jumped into the water and accidentally landed both knees on his neck. Sudden severe neck pain led to discontinuation of swimming, and he was taken to the hospital.

On examination, the patient had no associated injuries elsewhere and was hemodynamically stable. There was tenderness in the neck with restricted range of motion and no neurological deficit. A hard cervical collar was applied and the patient underwent radiological evaluation. A computed tomography (CT) scan showed multiple fractures of the cervical vertebrae with vertical split of the C4-C7 bodies, fractures of the posterior elements of C3-C7, and C5-C6 unifacetal dislocation with anterolisthesis (Fig. 1).

As the X-rays showed marked sclerosis of the vertebrae, the patient was subjected to further radiological and hematological investigations to rule out other causes of osteosclerosis. He did not have any hematological abnormalities, and blood biochemical values were within normal limits except elevated acid phosphatase levels (15 U/L). X-rays showed diffuse osteosclerosis primarily involving the axial skeleton with symmetrical involvement of long bones (Figs. 2, 3). Vertebral endplate sclerosis was seen with well-demarcated margins between the peripheral sclerotic and central relatively lucent bone, consistent with a "sandwich vertebrae" appearance. The visualized lumbar vertebrae also demonstrated a "bone within a bone" appearance secondary to sclerosis along the inner margins of the lateral aspect of the vertebral bodies, along with endplate sclerosis. These radiographic findings, along with the elevated acid phosphatase levels, were consistent with autosomal dominant osteopetrosis [1, 3, 4].

The injury extended from C2 to C7. Surgical stabilization involving one normal level above and below the injured segments would have amounted to fixation of the entire cervical spine (probably leading to an occipito-D1 construct), thus compromising mobility to a large extent. This long-segment fixation would also be accompanied by added complications of surgical intervention in an osteopetrotic spine. We therefore aimed to achieve stability by allowing the cervical spine to fall into reasonably acceptable alignment with closed reduction of the facetal dislocation and maintaining the alignment with traction as the soft tissues healed. Reassessment of stability would then be considered, and any residual subluxation managed with fixation of that particular segment, thus avoiding fixation of the entire cervical spine.

Traction was achieved with weights applied to Gardner-Wells tongs and suspended freely over a pulley attached to the head end of the bed (Fig. 4). An initial weight of 10 lbs was applied in a flexed position. Increments of 5 lbs were made at 20-minute intervals, followed by a lateral shoot-through X-ray and neurological assessment. After reduction was achieved, the neck was placed in extension with lighter maintenance weights. The mattress was pulled toward the feet, ending at shoulder level, and the head was supported with a soft cushion or a water-filled glove under the occiput to prevent pressure sores. The head rested at a lower level than the shoulders, thus maintaining the neck in an extended position. Counter-traction was achieved by elevating the head end of the bed. Adequate skin care at the pin insertion sites was ensured.

Continuous traction, in addition to causing reduction of facetal dislocation, produced a relative fixation of injured segments by the rigidity conferred by surrounding soft tissue structures under tension. Reduction of facetal dislocation allowed the cervical spine to fall into fairly acceptable alignment, which was supervised with weekly lateral X-rays.

With healing of the soft tissues after 6 weeks, tongs were removed and stability was reassessed. A flexion-extension radiograph showed no residual instability that would otherwise require surgical intervention as anticipated. Encouraged by these early good results, traction was discontinued and the patient was permitted to ambulate, with immobilization continued for another 6 weeks in a hard cervical collar. A repeat radiograph showed well-consolidated fracture sites and the cervical collar was discontinued. MRI was then performed to further evaluate the injury (Fig. 5). The vertebral bodies and intervertebral discs were fairly normal in height and intensity. No foraminal narrowing or spinal cord compression was observed.

Follow-up at 18 months and 2.5 years with flexion-extension radiographs suggested no residual instability, and the patient was completely asymptomatic with preserved range of motion (Figs. 6, 7, 8). Repeat MRI at 2.5 years showed no residual facetal subluxation or any obvious reduction in the injured C5-C6 disc space, contrary to the typical natural history of an injured disc (Fig. 9). Capsular and soft tissue healing over time led to an apparent restabilization of the injured C5-C6 segment, and we presume that in the long run this disc will probably degenerate.

## Discussion
We discuss the rare occurrence of traumatic multiple-level fractures of the cervical spine following trivial neck injury in a patient with osteopetrosis. Fracture is a common complication in osteopetrosis, but spiral, compression, or comminuted fracture is rare [2]. It seems that the osteopetrotic bone resists force in parallel with the cortical layer but is fragile to forces at right angles to the direction of the cortical layer. Most fractures are thus transverse, and the fracture line is perpendicular to the direction of the cortical layer [2]. Our patient had a vertical split fracture of the C4-C7 vertebral bodies at right angles to the sclerosed endplates. The posterior elements fracture line was also at right angles to the cortical layer.

Historically, most fractures in osteopetrosis have been treated conservatively [5]. Because the healing response is variable, management must be adapted and individualized to address the technical challenges unique to this patient population. Operative intervention has a high rate of intraoperative and postoperative complications [6]. Cervical fractures and instability in patients with osteopetrosis represent a unique challenge to orthopedic surgeons. Thus, careful planning and anticipation of possible technical difficulties and associated complications are mandatory, and decisions should be taken after weighing the risk-benefit ratio. De Palma et al. [7], in a report on histology of fracture callus in a patient with autosomal dominant osteopetrosis, found that at one year after a healed fracture, biopsy showed unorganized woven bone with absence of osteoclasts. Thus, osteopetrotic fractures do heal but remodeling does not occur. The fact that osteopetrotic bone heals despite defective remodeling and Haversian system makes the contribution of periosteum in fracture healing a potential area of research.

We report a case in which intervertebral cervical discs survived a severe discoligamentous injury—as documented by MRI. In times where surgical aggressiveness is high and such injuries are typically recommended for interbody fusion at primary surgery, we believe this case can stimulate a necessary discussion. Granted, the case is in a young adult with altered bone biology, but it nevertheless demonstrates what conservative care together with surgical patience and restraint can accomplish. It is no small difference for this patient to remain with a mobile cervical spine as opposed to entering adult life with a multilevel fusion.

## Conclusion
In our patient with multiple fractures of the cervical vertebrae following a trivial neck injury, complete recovery was seen after conservative treatment with cervical traction followed by a hard cervical collar, with no residual instability at 2.5 years of follow-up. However, the possibility of deformity or intervertebral disc degeneration should be monitored during long-term follow-up, and patient education is necessary to prevent further spinal injuries that may occur due to fragile bones.
