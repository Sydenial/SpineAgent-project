# Percutaneous Radiofrequency-Targeted Vertebral Augmentation of Unstable Metastatic C2 and C3 Lesions Using a CT-Guided Posterolateral Approach

## Abstract

Percutaneous radiofrequency–targeted C2 and C3 vertebral augmentation using a posterolateral approach and high-viscosity cement.

**Summary of Background Data:** Percutaneous vertebral augmentation is a minimally invasive procedure used for stabilization and pain control in vertebral compression fractures. Its use in the cervical spine, especially the upper cervical spine, is very limited mainly due to technical challenges.

**Methods:** We report the first use of an ultra–high-viscosity cement and posterolateral approach with computed tomography (CT) guidance and CT fluoroscopy in a patient with lytic lesions in C2 and C3 and a pathologic fracture of C2 for the purpose of stabilization and pain palliation.

**Results:** Technically successful vertebral augmentation of the C2 and C3 vertebral bodies was achieved. There were no complications. The patient reported pain relief and improved range of motion after treatment, and the hard cervical collar was removed.

**Conclusion:** CT-guided radiofrequency-targeted vertebral augmentation of the cervical spine using a posterolateral approach and ultra–high-viscosity cement is a technically feasible procedure that may be used in patients with advanced osteolytic cervical spine metastases who are not surgical candidates for the purpose of pain palliation and fracture stabilization.

## Introduction

The literature on cervical vertebral augmentation is limited and includes a few reports of vertebroplasty using anterior, anterolateral, lateral, and transoral approaches [1–3]. The posterolateral approach is a recently described method with only a small number of reports in metastatic disease [4–6]. In addition, energy-responsive, ultra–high-viscosity cement has recently been introduced for the treatment of vertebral compression fractures and has a potential role in reducing cement leakage compared with lower-viscosity cements [7,8] and in increasing the uniformity of cement filling [9]. There are very few reports of its application in the thoracic and lumbar spine and no reports of its use in the cervical spine.

This is the first report of radiofrequency-targeted vertebral augmentation using a posterolateral approach for the management of a pathologic fracture and pain in osteolytic lesions of the upper cervical spine.

## Case Report

Cervical spine imaging of a 55-year-old female with osseous metastases from squamous cell carcinoma of the lung demonstrated unstable lesions involving C2 and C3 with a pathologic fracture of C2. The patient’s pain was disabling and poorly controlled, and she was placed in a hard cervical collar for stabilization. She was not a surgical candidate due to the potentially significant morbidity and had received maximum radiation therapy to the spine. Vertebral augmentation was requested for stabilization of the cervical spine and for pain palliation.

After obtaining informed consent, general anesthesia was initiated and the patient was placed in the prone position with neutral craniocervical junction positioning on the CT table. For CT guidance, a 16-row CT scanner equipped with CT fluoroscopy was used. Fluoroscopic images were acquired with a low-dose technique and displayed in real time on an in-room monitor, facilitating efficient handling of the procedure.

Using a posterolateral approach, the C2 vertebral body was entered via the right pedicle with a 10-gauge introducer needle using a technique previously described in the thoracic and lumbar spine [10], following a pathway that avoided critical structures including the vertebral arteries. Under intermittent CT guidance, the needles were advanced into the midline of the C2 and C3 vertebral bodies. An osteotome was used to create channels across the midline to allow cement flow to the contralateral side, avoiding the need for a bipedicular approach.

Ultra–high-viscosity cement was prepared, and under CT fluoroscopy guidance, 5 mL of cement was injected into the C2 vertebral body, filling the large lytic lesion and the left lateral mass pathologic fracture. In the C3 vertebral body, 1 mL of cement was injected to fill a lytic lesion within the anterior aspect of the vertebral body. After completion of cement delivery, the cement-driving cannula was removed and the stylet was reinserted to completely empty the locking delivery cannula. No cement leakage was identified.

The patient was observed for 24 hours and showed no clinical complications while reporting substantial pain relief, with numeric pain rating scale scores improving from 9/10 to 5/10. The uncomfortable hard cervical collar was subsequently removed.

## Discussion

The posterolateral approach has the advantage of avoiding the carotid space and anterolateral vascular structures. With CT guidance and careful respect for the cortical boundaries of the pedicle and foramen transversarium, both the spinal cord and vertebral artery can be avoided. Preprocedural contrast-enhanced CT or magnetic resonance imaging, as well as CT or magnetic resonance angiography, can be used to delineate the course of the vertebral artery. In this case, contrast-enhanced cervical spine magnetic resonance imaging was used for preprocedural planning.

Combining CT and CT fluoroscopy provides precise guidance for needle placement. CT fluoroscopy allows near real-time monitoring of cement injection and prompt detection of cement leakage. In this case, CT fluoroscopy enabled the safe placement of a larger volume of cement in C2 than has been previously described. This is of critical importance in the cervical spine given the potentially severe complications associated with cement leakage into the spinal canal or foramen transversarium.

The radiofrequency-targeted vertebral augmentation system used in this case allows remote-controlled hydraulic cement delivery, enabling the operator to remain at a distance from the patient and thereby reducing radiation exposure [10,11]. A unipedicular approach, made possible by site-specific channels created using a navigational osteotome, may further reduce radiation dose and procedural invasiveness compared with traditional bipedicular approaches [11].

The ultra–high-viscosity cement used in this procedure contains particles of varying sizes that decelerate polymerization. Application of high-frequency alternating current immediately prior to injection accelerates polymerization, resulting in consistently high cement viscosity during delivery. Controlled delivery rates, targeted flow into osteotome-created channels, and higher cement viscosity all contribute to a reduced likelihood of cement leakage [11].

## Conclusion

Minimally invasive, posterolateral radiofrequency-targeted vertebral augmentation of the upper cervical spine using CT guidance is technically feasible and may offer advantages over other techniques for stabilization and pain palliation in patients with advanced cervical spine metastases who are not surgical candidates.

## Key Points

- Radiofrequency-targeted vertebral augmentation of the cervical spine may be an option for pain control and stabilization in patients with end-stage metastatic disease who are not surgical candidates.  
- CT guidance and CT fluoroscopy may decrease the risk of cement leakage and vascular or organ injury.  
- Rate-controlled delivery of radiofrequency-activated high-viscosity cement into channels created using a navigational osteotome may further reduce cement leakage.  
- The posterolateral approach may offer advantages over other methods for cervical vertebral augmentation.
