# Asymmetric C7 Pedicle Subtraction Osteotomy for Correction of Rigid Cervical Coronal Imbalance Secondary to Post-Traumatic Heterotopic Ossification: A Case Report, Description of a Novel Surgical Technique, and Literature Review

## Abstract

### Purpose
Deformities of the cervical spine are uncommon in the coronal plane. In this report, a unique case of a 31-year-old male with a fixed 30° left coronal deformity due to heterotopic ossification 3 years status post polytrauma was treated with an asymmetric C7 pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO).

### Methods
Case report.

### Results
Pre-operatively, the patient had a fixed 45° left tilt of his neck and radiographs demonstrated a rigid 30° scoliosis, 7 cm coronal imbalance, and 4 cm negative sagittal balance, with diffuse bridging bone between the spinous processes and the facet joints of C5 to T1 bilaterally. An asymmetric C7 PSO with C2–T3 posterior spinal fusion was completed without complication. There was residual 9° coronal deformity, 2.9 cm left coronal imbalance, and 2.3 cm sagittal imbalance. He had a marked improvement in function, as assessed by the SF-36 physical component score (pre-op 31.1; post-op 44.7) and mental component score (pre-op 46.0; post-op 66.8). Post-operatively, Neck Disability Index scores also improved (pre-op 38; post-op 16). Although the patient passed away from a drug overdose 14 months post-operatively, he did not report neck pain, had not sought evaluation from another physician for his neck, and had not undergone a subsequent neck operation before his passing.

### Conclusion
In this single patient, an asymmetric C7 PSO was performed safely. While it was effective in addressing a fixed cervical coronal imbalance, its efficacy and safety profile should be confirmed in larger cohorts.

## Introduction

Post-traumatic heterotopic ossification (HO) of the cervical spine is uncommon. Nevertheless, it may cause restricted range of motion, nerve impingement, pain, soft tissue ulceration, and joint ankylosis [1–3]. Resultant deformity in the sagittal or coronal planes may adversely affect quality of life [4]. While deformity of the cervical spine often occurs in the sagittal plane, coronal plane deformity is also recognized [5–7]. Cervical kyphotic deformity has been successfully and safely treated with a pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO) at the cervicothoracic junction [8–10]. To the best of our knowledge, this report details the first successful use of an asymmetric PSO at C7 to correct a rigid coronal cervical deformity due to post-traumatic heterotopic ossification.

## Case Report

### History, Physical Examination, and Imaging

A 31-year-old male presented to the outpatient spine clinic with a chief complaint of neck tilting and pain. Three years prior, he had suffered a motorcycle crash that resulted in a traumatic brain injury, a right upper extremity amputation, a left lower extremity amputation, and prolonged intubation. After discharge, he developed torticollis that progressed despite physical therapy and botulinum toxin injections, causing functional impairment of vision, voice, and ambulation. Additional details regarding the extent of these impairments were not available.

Clinically, he had a fixed 45° tilt of his neck to the left. Imaging demonstrated 7 cm of coronal imbalance to the left and a 4 cm negative sagittal imbalance. Standing full-spine radiographs could not be obtained due to his prior left lower extremity amputation. Diffuse bridging bone between the spinous processes and facet joints of C5 to T1 bilaterally was noted and presumed to be heterotopic ossification, given its extent and the patient’s traumatic brain injury history [4]. There was no facet dislocation or anterolisthesis; sagittal images demonstrated well-aligned facets at all levels.

To address the fixed multiplanar cervical deformity, the patient was scheduled for a C2–T3 posterior spinal fusion with a C7 PSO. Correction was planned based on the pre-operative coronal malalignment and negative sagittal alignment, with the goal of centering the patient’s head between his shoulders without significantly altering sagittal alignment. Given the rigidity of the deformity, a three-column osteotomy was deemed necessary. The distal extent of fixation was set at T3 to provide adequate stabilization, while the construct was extended proximally to C2 with pedicle screws to improve fixation strength.

## Surgical Technique

Under general anesthesia, the patient was intubated supine, and a halo was applied. He was then positioned prone on a flat operating table with a Jackson four-post frame, and the halo was secured to the table. Given the rigid coronal deformity, initial halo positioning reflected the pre-operative head-to-body alignment.

A midline posterior incision from C2 to T3 was made, and the spine was exposed subperiosteally. Pedicle screws were placed bilaterally at C2, T1, T2, and T3, and lateral mass screws were placed bilaterally at C3, C4, and C5. The inferior and superior articular processes at C6, C7, and T1 were found to be autofused due to extensive heterotopic ossification. The C7 level was identified by locating the T1 ribs bilaterally and dissecting one level proximally. Laminectomies were performed at C6, C7, and T1, and the C7 and C8 nerve roots were identified and followed to their foramina.

The C7 pedicles were skeletonized, and the transverse processes were removed bilaterally. The pedicles were sequentially tapped with increasing sizes to decancellate the vertebral body. The lateral wall of C7 was dissected and removed through the pedicle holes using rongeurs and osteotomes. The middle column was removed with down-pushing curettes to create an asymmetric closing wedge resection of approximately 30° on the right side.

No provisional stabilization was used during the osteotomy, as the head was rigidly fixed to the table via the halo. After completion of the osteotomy, gradual correction of the coronal deformity was achieved by manually translating the halo to the right under direct visualization and neuromonitoring. Correction was performed solely in the coronal plane, resulting in minimal change to sagittal alignment. The degree of correction was guided by trunk alignment and neuromonitoring signals, prioritizing neurologic safety. Once satisfactory head positioning was achieved, the halo was re-secured to the table.

Rods were placed from C2 to T3, and decortication was performed along the posterior elements. Allograft bone was placed posterolaterally. No bone graft was placed within the osteotomy site, acknowledging an increased risk of nonunion at this level, particularly with a two-rod construct.

## Post-Operative Course

The patient tolerated the procedure well and was discharged on post-operative day 7 with an intact neurologic examination. At the 6-week follow-up, he demonstrated improved head balance, level shoulders, and improved vocal and ambulatory function. Radiographs showed a residual coronal deformity of 9°, a left coronal imbalance of 2.9 cm, and a sagittal imbalance of −2.3 cm.

Functional outcomes improved substantially. The SF-36 physical component score improved from 31.1 pre-operatively to 44.7 post-operatively, and the mental component score improved from 46.0 to 66.8. The Neck Disability Index score improved from 38 to 16.

The patient did not return for further follow-up after relocating out of state due to military service. He passed away 14 months post-operatively from a drug overdose. Prior to his death, he reported no neck pain or functional complaints related to his cervical spine and had not undergone further evaluation or surgery.

## Discussion

Fixed coronal cervical deformity is a rare clinical entity. While mild deformities may not cause significant functional impairment, severe coronal malalignment presents substantial challenges for both patients and surgeons. Existing literature primarily focuses on sagittal plane cervical deformity and pediatric cases involving hemivertebra resections [11, 12]. To date, there have been no reports describing surgical correction strategies for fixed cervical coronal imbalance in adults, making this case the first to detail the use of an asymmetric C7 PSO for this indication.

The described technique represents a modification of previously reported cervicothoracic PSOs used for sagittal imbalance correction [8–10]. Consistent with prior studies, this case demonstrates that a cervical PSO can be performed safely and effectively in selected adult patients. In this patient, the asymmetric osteotomy resulted in meaningful improvements in posture, mobility, and quality of life.

Alternative techniques for cervical coronal deformity correction have been limited to pediatric case reports involving congenital hemivertebrae [11, 12]. While these approaches achieved satisfactory deformity correction, they differ significantly in pathology, patient population, and surgical strategy compared to the adult post-traumatic deformity described here.

This case report has several limitations. It represents a retrospective review of a single patient treated at one institution, introducing inherent bias and limiting generalizability. Detailed assessments of pre- and post-operative vocal and ambulatory function were unavailable, and imaging from the time of initial injury was not accessible. Full-spine alignment could not be accurately assessed due to the patient’s lower extremity amputation. Additionally, follow-up with health-related quality-of-life measures was limited to 6 weeks, precluding long-term evaluation of outcomes. Although long-term radiographic and clinical follow-up was not possible due to the patient’s death, he reported no cervical spine–related symptoms more than one year after surgery.

## Conclusion

Proper patient selection, thorough pre-operative planning, and careful intraoperative execution are essential for successful correction of rigid cervical coronal deformity using a cervicothoracic PSO. Although this isolated case demonstrates that an asymmetric C7 PSO can be performed safely with meaningful deformity correction, further studies in larger cohorts are necessary to validate its efficacy and safety.
