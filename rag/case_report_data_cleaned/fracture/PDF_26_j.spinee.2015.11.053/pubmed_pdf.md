# Vertebral Compression Fracture Within a Solid Fusion Mass After Removal of Pedicle Screws

## Abstract
BACKGROUND CONTEXT: Many complications of lumbar fusion have been reported; however, there are almost no reports of compression fractures occurring within a fusion mass.  
PURPOSE: To report a case of vertebral body compression fracture within a solid lumbar fusion mass after removal of pedicle screw instrumentation.  
STUDY DESIGN: Case report.  
METHODS: A 67-year-old woman presented with gait disturbance and pain radiating to both lower extremities. She had a history of lumbar fusion at L2–L5 for degenerative stenosis. We identified spinal stenosis at L5–S1 and ossification of the ligamentum flavum at T12–L2. Posterior decompression and fusion were performed to address these lesions, and previously inserted fusion instrumentation at L2–L5 was removed at the patient’s request. After surgery, her symptoms improved and she was discharged. Five weeks later, she returned with sudden, severe back pain without trauma. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a compression fracture at the L3 lower end plate. She was treated with analgesics and a thoracolumbosacral orthosis; her pain decreased over 2 weeks, and she was discharged.  
RESULTS: Before implant removal, complete union of the fusion mass was verified by computed tomography. Despite this, an unexpected compression fracture occurred within the fusion mass without trauma. Likely contributing factors included straight sagittal imbalance of the spine, cantilever motion in the anterior disc after posterior fusion, and decreased bone strength.  
CONCLUSIONS: Vertebral compression fracture in a solid fusion mass may occur as a complication of implant removal. Surgeons should maintain normal sagittal alignment during spinal fusion and consider the risks of implant removal, particularly in patients with osteoporosis, sagittal imbalance, long-segment fusions, and specific fusion types.

## Introduction
Lumbar fusion using pedicle screw fixation is widely used to treat various spine diseases [1-4]. Reported complications include adjacent-segment pathology, pseudarthrosis, implant failure, and adjacent-segment fracture in patients with osteoporosis. However, complications related to implant removal after solid fusion are rarely reported [5,6]. Reports of compression fractures occurring within a fusion mass are particularly scarce. We describe a case of vertebral body compression fracture in a solid lumbar fusion mass after removal of fusion instrumentation.

## Case Report
A 67-year-old woman presented with gait disturbance and bilateral lower extremity radiating pain. She had a history of posterior lumbar surgery with a solid dorsal fusion mass from L2 to L5 on plain radiographs and three-dimensional computed tomography. We also identified spinal stenosis at L5–S1 and ossification of the ligamentum flavum at T12–L2, which were considered the causes of her symptoms.

Posterior decompression and fusion were performed for these lesions, and the previously inserted fusion instrumentation from L2 to L5 was removed at the patient’s request. Intraoperatively, a solid posterior and posterolateral fusion mass at L2–L5 was confirmed. Postoperatively, her symptoms gradually improved, and she was discharged at 2 weeks with a recommendation for rehabilitation to address gait disturbance.

Five weeks after surgery, she returned with sudden, severe back pain without trauma. Physical examination revealed marked tenderness in the lower back. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a compression fracture at the L3 lower end plate. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry showed a femoral neck T-score of −1.8 (osteopenia). She was managed with analgesics and a thoracolumbosacral orthosis; however, she poorly tolerated the orthosis. After 2 weeks, her back pain was nearly alleviated, and she was discharged.

At 3 months, plain radiographs showed height loss and collapse of the L3 vertebral body, although she had no back pain. She did not return until 3 years postoperatively, when follow-up radiographs demonstrated collapse involving L2–L4. She reported back pain, a stooping gait, and used a cane because of sagittal imbalance from lumbar kyphosis. There was no focal lumbar tenderness and no history of trauma. We believe that if complete posterior fusion at L2–L5 had not been present, further fracture progression or kyphotic deformity would have been more severe.

## Discussion
Spinal fusion is a common treatment for spinal stenosis, spondylolisthesis, and deformity, but it is associated with multiple complications [1-4]. Complications specifically following implant removal after solid fusion are rarely described; to our knowledge, this is the fourth reported case of a vertebral compression fracture occurring within a fusion mass. Patients sometimes request implant removal due to perceived hardware-related discomfort, but indications for removal after fusion are not well established and often depend on surgeon preference. When removal is considered—for example, for mechanical irritation, adjacent-segment pathology requiring surgery, or persistent patient request—complete union of the fusion mass should be confirmed. In this case, union was verified by computed tomography before implant removal, yet a compression fracture still occurred.

Ha and Kim reported bilateral pedicle stress fractures at L4 after removal of instrumentation from an L3–L5 posterolateral fusion, and a separate case of compression fracture within a fused segment in a patient with osteopenia [5,6]. Waelchli et al. described two cases of compression fracture at the uppermost vertebral body of the fusion mass after implant removal, implicating subcortical defects at the screw tracks as a common factor [7]. From a biomechanical perspective, cantilever motion in the anterior column after posterolateral fusion may increase anterior compressive loads and contribute to fracture risk [5].

In our patient, the fracture occurred at the inferior end plate within the fusion mass, and additional vertebral collapse developed over time, rather than at the pedicle screw tracks. Lumbar lordosis and thoracic kyphosis were decreased after prior lumbar fusion, creating a straight sagittal imbalance. Subsequent thoracolumbar fusion and loss of instrumentation support likely increased axial load transfer to the lower vertebrae. Posterior fusion (relative to interbody fusion) may further increase anterior column loading, amplifying the cantilever effect. Decreased bone strength also contributed: spine bone mineral density could not be measured due to prior surgery, but femoral neck osteopenia was present, and radiographs suggested generalized vertebral demineralization (increased radiolucency, loss of trabecular bone, cortical thinning, and a well-demarcated cortical rim).

Vertebral compression fracture within a solid fusion mass can occur after implant removal. Preservation of normal sagittal alignment is crucial during spinal fusion. Surgeons should be cautious when considering implant removal in patients with risk factors such as osteoporosis, sagittal imbalance, long-segment fusion, and posterior-only fusion constructs.
