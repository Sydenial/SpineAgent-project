# Posterior Atlantoaxial Dislocation Without Fracture or Neurological Symptoms Treated by Transoral–Posterior Approach Surgery: A Case Report and Literature Review

## Abstract

**Background** Atlantoaxial dislocation usually results from hyperextension trauma and is almost always accompanied by odontoid fracture and neurological symptoms. In most cases, patients with atlantoaxial dislocation die instantly. This is a rare report of posterior atlantoaxial dislocation without fracture and neurological symptoms treated by a transoral–posterior approach surgery, and only eleven similar cases have been previously reported.  

**Objective** To describe the very rare case of an adult posterior atlantoaxial dislocation patient without fracture who was neurologically intact and treated using transoral–posterior approach surgery, and to review the relevant literature.  

**Methods** A 52-year-old man riding a motorcycle was rear-ended by a car. Using X-ray, computed tomography (CT) scan, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), he was diagnosed with posterior atlantoaxial dislocation without a related fracture or a significant change in spinal cord signal. Transoral–posterior approach surgery with sustained skull traction was used after failed closed reduction.  

**Results** During a 6-month follow-up observation, lateral cervical spine radiography and sagittal reconstructions of CT scans demonstrated no instability of the atlantoaxial complex. Few patients experience posterior atlantoaxial dislocation without an associated fracture or spinal cord deficit. For a patient who experiences trauma with hyperextension, such as a rear-end collision, X-ray, CT scan, and MRI should be performed to ensure that this injury is diagnosed. It is necessary to perform surgery to recover atlantoaxial stability, even in the absence of fracture or neurological symptoms.  

**Conclusion** Transoral–posterior approach surgery is a safe and effective way to manage irreducible posterior atlantoaxial dislocation.

**Keywords** Atlantoaxial dislocation; Transoral–posterior approach; Skull traction

## Introduction

Posterior atlantoaxial dislocation resulting from traffic accidents usually causes death immediately regardless of odontoid fracture, and very few patients have survived, both with and without neurological symptoms. Related reports describing treatments for posterior atlantoaxial dislocation in the absence of fracture or neurological deficit are very rare. By using conservative traction, most dislocations have been reduced. Posterior or anterior internal fixation and fusion should be supplemented to increase atlantoaxial complex stability. Although a few similar cases have been reported subsequently, the choice of ideal treatment strategy remains controversial and the prognosis is not fully clear. Here, we present a new case that was treated effectively through a combined anterior–posterior approach. The patient was informed that data concerning the case would be submitted for publication and consented to publication.

## Case Report

A 52-year-old male patient riding a motorcycle was struck by a car and brought to a local county hospital immediately. He was diagnosed with atlantoaxial dislocation, multiple soft tissue lacerations, and dislocation of the left ankle joint. According to the county hospital medical record, the patient was conscious, vital parameters were normal, and he complained of pain in his neck and throat. After 2 days of treatment, including reduction of the ankle joint, debridement of soft tissue lacerations, and immobilization with a neck collar, the patient was transferred to our hospital for further diagnosis and treatment.

Physical examination upon arrival showed that motion of the neck was obviously limited, but movements of the limbs were normal with grade IV–V muscle strength. Neurological function was defined as grade E according to the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) standardized neurological classification of spinal cord injury.

Neither severe brain nor thoracoabdominal injury was found on initial CT scan. Anteroposterior, lateral, and open-mouth X-rays of the cervical spine demonstrated a retropositioned atlas and total atlantoaxial dislocation. CT scanning and MRI confirmed the X-ray findings. There was no spinal cord compression or significant change in spinal cord signal.

Four kilograms of skull traction was applied. Intraoperative real-time fluoroscopy showed that the distance between the atlas and axis was enlarged but still suitable for reduction. The traction weight was not increased to avoid severe spinal cord injury. The preoperative plan was partial odontoidectomy to facilitate reduction.

The axis and atlas were exposed via a transoral retropharyngeal approach with oral tracheal intubation. Intraoperative real-time X-ray after anterior atlantoaxial release indicated that the dislocation had been reduced. The anterior incision was closed in anatomical layers without grafting. Maintaining the tracheal cannula and skull traction, the patient was repositioned for posterior approach surgery. C1–C2 fixation was performed using a pedicle screw system under lateral fluoroscopy, with autologous morselized iliac crest bone graft. No spinal cord monitoring was used except for a wake-up test.

After surgery, the oral tracheal cannula was removed immediately, and the patient was returned to the ward with cardiac monitoring. No complex complications, iatrogenic neurological deficits, or postoperative infection occurred. Postoperative lateral radiography and CT scans showed no instability of the atlantoaxial complex. Neck and throat symptoms gradually resolved. Three months after surgery, the patient was instructed to remove the neck collar. He returned to normal life, resumed work, and was fully self-sufficient.

## Discussion

According to the anatomical structure of C1–C2, the anterior arch of the atlas and the transverse ligament form an osteoligamentous ring that stabilizes the odontoid process together with the interlocking articular processes. Odontoid fracture and ligament rupture characterize most atlantoaxial dislocations. Severe extension trauma combined with ligament laxity may result in the odontoid slipping out of the osteoligamentous ring, leading to posterior atlantoaxial dislocation. Only eleven cases similar to the present case have been reported in the English-language literature [1–11]. Based on previous experience, hyperextension injuries producing posterior atlantoaxial dislocation generally cause immediate and fatal spinal cord damage.

However, in emergency settings, this dislocation may be missed because of the absence of neurological deficit or odontoid fracture. Thus, its incidence may be higher than reported. The neural canal at the atlantoaxial level includes the odontoid, cerebrospinal fluid, and spinal cord. In some cases, sufficient canal space may prevent spinal cord compromise.

Diagnosis of atlantoaxial dislocation without neurological symptoms or odontoid fracture is challenging. Plain radiography may not clearly show the atlas and axis because of cranial bone overlap and complex anatomical positioning. Therefore, misdiagnosis is not uncommon. For patients with hyperextension trauma, such as rear-end collisions, additional imaging including open-mouth radiographs, CT, and MRI should be performed. CT provides clear visualization of bony structures, while MRI offers superior soft tissue assessment. In this case, CT showed ventral displacement of the odontoid peg without fracture, and MRI demonstrated altered cervical cord curvature without signal abnormality or compression.

Treatment options include closed reduction with traction and open surgical reduction. Closed reduction can be effective but carries significant risk of neurological injury or death due to overdistraction. High technical skill and careful control of traction weight are required. Some authors have reported successful closed reduction using phased traction techniques, followed by external immobilization or posterior fusion when instability persists.

Posterior atlantoaxial dislocation without odontoid fracture or neurological symptoms is extremely rare, and no standard surgical technique exists. Although partial odontoidectomy has been reported, our case demonstrates that anterior atlantoaxial release alone may be sufficient for reduction, followed by posterior fixation. Given the high risk associated with closed reduction, spinal cord monitoring is recommended when attempting traction-based reduction strategies.
