# Resolution of Traumatic Bilateral Vertebral Artery Injury

## Abstract

Blunt cerebrovascular injury (BCVI) is a rare occurrence, often resulting from blunt trauma to the neck. Screening for blunt cerebrovascular injury should be performed in patients with certain signs, symptoms, and risk factors. We describe a case of traumatic bilateral vertebral artery injury (VAI), including unilateral vertebral artery occlusion, which resolved 3 months post-injury with antiplatelet and direct oral anticoagulant therapy. This resolution of traumatic bilateral VAI is very rare. Vertebral artery injury should be suspected in patients with displaced fracture dislocation of the cervical spine, particularly in the elderly and those with ankylosing spondylitis. Imaging of these patients should include a modality to assess the patency of the vertebral arteries.

**Keywords**: Spinal cord injury, Blunt cerebrovascular injury, Vertebral artery injury, Vertebral artery occlusion

## Introduction

Blunt cerebrovascular injury (BCVI) is rare, with the incidence of blunt traumatic vertebral artery injury (VAI) reported to be less than 1% among patients with blunt trauma. However, VAI has been identified in around 15% of high-risk patients who were screened for blunt VAI. Furthermore, posterior circulation stroke and a mortality rate of up to 8% have been reported in 24% of patients with blunt VAIs. Since VAI can cause potentially serious sequelae, screening for BCVIs should be conducted in patients with certain signs, symptoms, and risk factors. The Biffl scale is commonly used to classify VAI when diagnosed. Although the optimal treatment for VAI remains unclear, the early administration of systemic heparin or antiplatelet therapy is recommended to reduce the risk of stroke in patients with VAI grades I–IV. Endovascular therapy may be an option for certain injuries, but there is no clear indication for its use. This case describes traumatic bilateral VAI, including unilateral vertebral arterial occlusion, that resolved 3 months post-injury with antiplatelet and direct oral anticoagulant therapy.

## Case Description

A 70-year-old man diagnosed with ankylosing spondylitis and restricted neck extension and flexion collapsed, hit his forehead, and presented with tetraplegia. He was conscious, with a blood pressure of 120/60 mmHg, a pulse rate of 66/min, and a respiratory rate of 16/min. His oxygen saturation was 91% on room air. Neurological examination revealed tetraplegia. He presented with dyspnea and was intubated and artificially ventilated under appropriate precautions against displacing his neck.

Computed tomography (CT) showed C3 fracture dislocation, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed high signal intensity and intense compression of the spinal cord from C2 to C3. He underwent emergency reduction of the C3 fracture manually and was fitted with a halo ring and vest to support his neck under radiographic guidance. After these procedures, CT angiogram and cerebral angiogram showed left vertebral artery occlusion (Biffl grade IV) and right vertebral artery stenosis (Biffl grade I). Brain MRI did not show abnormality, and cerebral angiography confirmed the occlusion and stenosis. Heparin was administered to prevent posterior circulation stroke and was later replaced with clopidogrel. Despite this, no further intervention was needed immediately, though it was delayed due to the need for anticoagulation. He underwent posterior fixation on day 26 post-admission. CT angiogram showed no improvement on post-operation day 1. The patient was diagnosed with deep vein thrombosis (DVT) post-operatively, and apixaban was administered to reduce the risk of recurrent DVT and pulmonary embolism. Three months later, a cerebral angiogram showed the resolution of the bilateral VAI. Brain MRI revealed no abnormality. Though he did not make a full recovery, he was transferred to another care facility for rehabilitation.

## Discussion

BCVI is rare, but cervical spine fractures are a significant risk factor for BCVI. The Denver screening criteria indicate that patients with cervical fractures need evaluation for BCVI. Magnetic resonance angiography is considered the best screening modality for diagnosing BCVI; however, CT angiography or angiography is necessary if arterial flow voids cannot be determined on MRI. BCVI is classified using the Biffl scale, where grade III and IV injuries are less likely to resolve spontaneously. Total occlusion of both vertebral arteries can result in brainstem infarction, making the prevention of posterior circulation stroke extremely important. 

In this case, CT showed C3 fracture dislocation and cerebral angiogram revealed left vertebral artery occlusion (Biffl grade IV) and right vertebral artery stenosis (Biffl grade II). Three months post-injury, a cerebral angiogram showed the resolution of bilateral VAI. To our knowledge, this is the first description of the resolution of traumatic bilateral VAI.

It is known that vertebral artery occlusion often improves spontaneously, though this is less likely in grade IV injuries. Scott et al. reported that 65% of grade IV VAI patients showed persistent occlusion, 30% demonstrated improvement, and 5% had injury resolution despite therapeutic systemic anticoagulation.

Antiplatelet therapy is recommended for grade IV VAI to prevent posterior circulation stroke. Endovascular stenting is reserved for the rare patient with symptoms or a markedly enlarging pseudoaneurysm, as routine stenting carries increased risks and costs. In this case, anticoagulation therapy was administered along with antiplatelet therapy due to DVT. 

Single antiplatelet therapy did not lead to re-canalization of the vertebral artery, but direct oral anticoagulants (DOAC) did. However, the collateral circulation via the circle of Willis was likely responsible for preventing brainstem stroke, rather than late re-canalization of the vertebral arteries.

## Conclusions

This case presents a rare instance of traumatic bilateral VAI, including unilateral vertebral arterial occlusion, which resolved 3 months post-injury. Vertebral artery injury should be suspected in patients with displaced fracture dislocations of the cervical spine, particularly in the elderly and those with ankylosing spondylitis. Imaging in these patients should include a modality to assess the patency of the vertebral arteries.
