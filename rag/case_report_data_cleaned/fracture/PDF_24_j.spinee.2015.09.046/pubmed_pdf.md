# Pars/Pedicle Fracture And Screw Loosening Associated With Cortical Bone Trajectory: A Case Series And Proposed Mechanism Through A Cadaveric Study

## Abstract
Cortical bone trajectory (CBT) technique for pedicle screw placement in the lumbar spine has become more popular since its introduction in 2009. The distinct advantages of utilizing CBT technique involve increased screw purchase within cortical bone and reduced surgical dissection. However, contrary to several favorable biomechanical results, there were anecdotal reports of clinical complications associated with CBT. This study reports two unique pars/pedicle fracture cases involving the use of CBT technique and performs a cadaveric pilot study to determine the possible mechanism for this fracture pattern.

## Background
Cortical bone trajectory (CBT) technique for pedicle screw placement in the lumbar spine has become popular since its introduction in 2009 by Santoni. The distinct advantages of utilizing CBT technique involve increased screw purchase within cortical bone and reduced surgical dissection. These factors make CBT technique an attractive alternative to standard pedicle screw fixation. This technique is particularly attractive in patients with metabolic bone disease. Several biomechanical studies have demonstrated comparable pullout and cyclic fatigue strength between CBT and traditional pedicle screw technique. However, Glennie et al. reported screw loosening in 5 of 8 patients, which is consistent with our initial series involving 2 out of 22 patients who developed pars/pedicle fractures and 2 additional patients who developed early screw loosening. 

The objective of our study was twofold: first, to report on two unique pars/pedicle fracture cases, and second, to perform a cadaveric pilot study to determine the possible mechanism for this fracture pattern.

## Methods
### Case 1
A 77-year-old female with a BMI of 27 presented to the clinic with symptoms of neurogenic claudication and back pain. The patient’s Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) and Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores were 24 and 6, respectively. Neurological examination was unremarkable for any significant sensory or motor deficits. The patient had failed to respond to conservative therapy, including medication, physical therapy, and steroid injections. Imaging studies showed central canal stenosis involving L3-L4 and L4-L5 along with grade 1 spondylolisthesis at both levels.

Surgery: Decompression was performed at both L3-4 and L4-5 levels. Pedicle screws (5.0 x 30 mm) using the CBT technique (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) were placed on the left side first without complication. However, when placing screws using CBT technique on the right, a fracture through the pars of L3 was encountered. Given the significant pars fracture that had occurred intraoperatively, use of CBT technique was aborted, and traditional pedicle screw placement was employed on the right side at L3, L4, and L5. Postoperatively, the patient's pain improved with ODI and VAS scores of 6 and 0, respectively. The patient's most recent follow-up was 15 months after her initial surgery.

### Case 2
A 56-year-old male with a BMI of 27.8 and a history of hypertension and a 42-year smoking history presented to the clinic with severe progressive low back and right leg pain. The patient's ODI and VAS scores were 36 and 10, respectively. Additionally, he complained of associated numbness, weakness, and paresthesias within the right L5/S1 nerve distribution. The patient failed conservative treatment, including medication, therapy, and epidural steroid injection.

Surgery: The patient underwent decompression at L5-S1 and posterior instrumentation at L4 and L5 using CBT technique (6.5 x 30 mm screws, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN). Sacral alar screws (7.5 x 30 mm) were placed at S1. Bone graft was placed posterior laterally from L4 to S1. Postoperatively, the patient reported initial modest improvements; however, after 4 weeks, he began to have increasing low back pain. A CT scan of the lumbar spine revealed a right pars/pedicle fracture at L5, as well as loosening of both sacral alar and right L5 pedicle screws. The patient subsequently required 2 additional revision surgeries. The first revision surgery included anterior instrumentation and fusion from L3 to S1, which resulted in pseudarthrosis and broken hardware 9 months after the first revision surgery. The second revision surgery involved posterior L3 to S1 fusion with L3 to ilium instrumentation. Two screws were placed at each L3 pedicle using both CBT technique and standard pedicle screw trajectories.

## Cadaveric Study
Given these two similar clinical complications, we performed a cadaveric study to determine the possible mechanism of these fractures.

### Material & Method
19 fresh frozen lumbar vertebrae were obtained from eight cadavers. To quantify bone mineral density (BMD), each vertebral level was scanned with dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA). All screws were inserted using the cortical bone trajectory technique as described by Matsukawa et al. The starting point for CBT technique was at the level of the caudal aspect of the transverse process, approximately 2 mm medial to the lateral margin of pars inter-articularis. A surgical burr with a diameter of 1.7 mm (Midas Rex Legend, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) was used to create a starting point, then directed approximately 25 degrees cephalad and 8 lateral. Tap of 4.5 mm was used to tap line to line. 4.5 x 25 mm poly-axial pedicle screw (Nuvasive, San Diego, CA) was inserted. The insertion continued until either the tulip head contacted the pars/lamina or a fracture occurred. A total of 37 pedicle screws were placed under video recording. X-ray of AP/Lateral/Axial views of each vertebra were obtained after instrumentation. Anatomical dissections were performed at the end of the experiment to directly visualize each vertebra to confirm screw placement and identify potential fractures.

## Results

To be able to reach the necessary angle for medial to lateral trajectory, 13 out of 19 (68%) spinous processes had to be removed. There were a total of 7 complications. One pars/pedicle fracture (2.7%) and six trajectory deviations (16.2%) resulted in gross loosening observed in 7 out of 37 screws (18.9%).

The fracture observed during the cadaveric experiment was identical to the fractures previously described in the clinical cases. The fracture started at the screw insertion point on the lateral border of the pars, extended in a semi-sagittal oblique plane through the medial aspect of the superior facet, continued through the pedicle, and exited out the lateral aspect of the pedicle.

Through video analysis, it was clear that both screw loosening and the development of fractures were caused by the same mechanism. There was no observable abnormality during the initial drill and tap process. However, during final screw placement, the head of the screw impinged medially against the base of the spinous process and lamina, causing a subtle but sudden lateral deviation to the initial tapped trajectory. This deviation caused either the loss of purchase or, in the extreme case, the fracture of the pars/pedicle.

## Discussion
To our knowledge, there has only been one study in the literature mentioning complications of CBT technique. Glennie et al. retrospectively reviewed records for a 2-year period at their institution and found a total of 8 patients who had undergone pedicle screw placement with CBT technique. Their mean follow-up time was 16.4 months. They placed CBT pedicle screws using O-Arm, and trajectories were confirmed with post-op CT scans. Of the 8 patients who had undergone instrumentation of the lumbar spine using CBT technique, 5 had screw loosening, and 2 of them required revision surgery after a period of one year. They suggested that a possible mechanism of early failure involved the trapezoidal shape of the final construct.

In a biomechanical study by Paik et al., the effect of the pedicle screw head hubbing against the lamina with traditional trajectory was examined. Hubbing was defined as an additional aperture purchase of the dorsal lamina to the ventral aspect of the screw head. Their study showed that hubbing pedicle screws resulted in significantly lower pullout strength compared to non-hubbing screws. These differences persisted irrespective of bone mineral density. They also encountered fractures in 11 out of 22 screws that were hubbed. The fracture pattern was visualized through the lamina, superior articular facet, and the lateral pedicle wall. Their observations demonstrated that deeper screw insertion essentially resulted in catastrophic failure with fracture propagation through the dorsal lamina, pedicle, and the superior articular facet. The hubbing mechanism, with the head of the screw impinging against posterior bony elements inducing fracture, could certainly be similar to the mechanism of pars/pedicle fracture caused by cortical bone trajectory.

## Conclusions
The study proposes that complications associated with cortical bone trajectory technique could be prevented by performing decompressive laminectomy prior to final screw insertion to avoid head impingement, leaving the screw proud to avoid hubbing, and using screws with modular head assembly, so the head can bypass the posterior elements.
