# Floating Lumbar Spine: Proposed Mechanism with Review of Literature

## Abstract

Hyperextension injuries of the lumbar spine resulting in lumbosacral dislocation are a rare entity. We report a case of a 60-year-old male who presented to the outpatient department with a history of trivial fall from a bicycle, sustaining fractures through the pedicles extending from L2 to L5 with lumbosacral dislocation and free-floating posterior elements, while maintaining intact neurology. This is the first reported case of a four-level extension–compression injury with lumbosacral dislocation leading to a floating lumbar spine, to the best of the authors’ knowledge. Treatment consists of reduction of the lumbosacral dislocation first and fusion of the disc space, followed by reduction of the proximal fractures. These injuries may present after trivial trauma in elderly patients with spondylotic spines. MRI and CT scans should be performed early to identify, reduce, and fix these injuries, as many cases associated with trivial trauma may not present with neural deficits.

Keywords: Hyperextension, Floating lumbar spine, Lumbosacral dislocation

## Case Presentation

A 60-year-old man with no history of comorbidities except hypertension presented with a history of falling from a bicycle after colliding with another bicycle. He experienced back pain but was able to stand and ride home after the injury. He presented to the outpatient department two days later with severe back pain. On examination, the patient had paraspinal muscle spasm. A step deformity was palpable in the lower back, but neurological examination was normal.

## Diagnostic Imaging

Plain radiographs showed lumbosacral dislocation with fractures of the pedicles from L2 to L5. CT scan demonstrated multilevel fractures through the pedicles from L2 to L5 with associated lumbosacral dislocation. There was no evidence of preexisting spondylolysis. A DEXA scan was performed to rule out osteoporosis. The patient had no significant preexisting comorbidities.

## Historical Review

Hyperextension injuries of the lumbar spine resulting in lumbosacral dislocation have been reported in the literature. These injuries were originally classified as B3.2 in the Magerl classification [1]. Most reported cases describe anterior dislocations only, while posterior spinopelvic dissociation is very rare. Approximately ten posterior dislocations have been described to date. In anterior spinopelvic dissociation, the fracture line extends from anterior to posterior, with disc failure preceding posterior column failure. Fractures of lumbar pedicles from L2 to L5 associated with lumbosacral dislocation have not been previously reported. Existing case reports and series describe only isolated lumbosacral dislocation. Management of these complex injuries without neurological deficit is challenging due to the potential instability created by lumbosacral dislocation and freely floating posterior elements.

## Rationale for Treatment and Evidence-Based Literature

Lumbosacral dislocation was first described by Watson-Jones in 1940 [2], where the fifth lumbar vertebra translates over the sacrum. Aihara et al. classified lumbosacral dislocation into five types [3], though the present injury pattern has not been previously classified. More than 100 cases of lumbosacral dislocation have been reported, but none following trivial trauma with this injury configuration. Early identification of three-column injuries using CT and MRI is essential. The pathomechanics remain controversial [4]. The authors hypothesize that hyperextension of the lumbar spine can result in pedicle fractures with lumbosacral dislocation. While some authors suggest hyperflexion with compression as a mechanism for anterior spinopelvic dissociation, injuries involving fractures of multiple proximal pedicles in conjunction with lumbosacral dislocation have not been previously described.

This injury is rare, as fracture at L5 typically dissipates the force, preventing further injury. In this patient, the fracture progressed cranially, involving pedicles of L5, L4, L3, and L2. The pattern of failure suggests progressive disruption of pedicles and neural arches from caudal to cranial levels. Continuous compressive force applied in an extended position may explain this mechanism. Unlike previously reported cases, this patient sustained low-energy trauma without osteoporosis or inflammatory disease and had no neurological deficit, likely due to auto-decompression from posterior element failure. Transverse process fractures may serve as sentinel signs for lumbosacral dislocation [5]. Conservative management has no role in such injuries; operative intervention is required. The authors propose the term “floating lumbar spine” to describe fractures involving lumbar pedicles with lumbosacral dislocation and free-floating posterior elements.

## Operative Procedure

The patient was positioned prone with hips flexed to avoid hyperextension. Bolsters were used for positioning. Initial reduction achieved was minimal. Given good bone density, fixation to the pelvis was not performed, and distal fixation was limited to S1. Reduction and fixation of L5/S1 were performed first. S1 pedicle screws were placed toward the sacral promontory to achieve bicortical purchase under image intensification. L5 reduction pedicle screws were inserted partially to facilitate reduction of L5 over S1. Gradual reduction was achieved using distraction, with careful attention to avoid screw pullout and L5 nerve root stretch. Neuromonitoring was used throughout reduction. Temporary rod stabilization was followed by disc preparation and TLIF at L5/S1. Once distal stability was achieved, partial spontaneous reduction occurred proximally from L2 to L4 due to intact discoligamentous structures. Fixation was then extended sequentially to L2 using reduction screws, and a contoured rod from L2 to S1 replaced the temporary rod. Biopsy from vertebral bodies and pedicles showed normal histology.

## Procedure Imaging

Intraoperative radiographs obtained at the completion of surgery demonstrated satisfactory reduction.

## Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was mobilized on the first postoperative day and discharged on postoperative day four. Follow-up evaluations were conducted at two weeks, three months, one year, and two years. Radiographs at two years demonstrated maintained reduction. The patient resumed agricultural work without limitations or complaints. The authors suggest that floating lumbar spine should be considered in elderly patients presenting after mild trauma. Extension–compression is the most likely injury mechanism. Management involves distal-to-proximal reduction with fixation and bone grafting. Initial reduction and fusion at L5/S1 should be followed by proximal fixation. In cases of osteopenia, pelvic fixation may be considered.

## Conclusion

Floating lumbar spine represents a rare but distinct clinical entity characterized by multilevel lumbar pedicle fractures with lumbosacral dislocation and intact neurology. Early recognition and surgical management are essential to achieve stable fixation and favorable outcomes.
