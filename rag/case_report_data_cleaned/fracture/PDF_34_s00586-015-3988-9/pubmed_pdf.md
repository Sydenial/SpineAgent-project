# Intradural Migration of Cervical Posterior Fixation Rods

## Abstract

Instrumented spinal fixations are an important tool in the management of traumatic conditions, and delayed complications are rare. We present a case of open reduction and fixation of a traumatic C5/6 facet fracture dislocation with a late complication in the form of intradural hardware migration. To our knowledge, this is the first report of an intradural rod migration distant from the initial surgery in a patient without posterior decompression. This case highlights the need for long-term follow-up of patients with spinal instrumentation.

## Background

Instrumented spinal fixations are an important tool in the management of traumatic conditions, and delayed complications are rare. There are no firm guidelines regarding the length of follow-up necessary after these fixations, and most clinicians have devised their own schedules. The instruments used often differ considerably between manufacturers in the mechanisms of locking the caps and rods. It is vital that surgeons and theater staff are comfortable with the use of the equipment.

## Case Report

A 29-year-old man presented to the Accident and Emergency Department after being involved in a rugby tackle during which he sustained a bilateral C5/6 facet fracture dislocation. On presentation, there was no neurological deficit. He underwent anterior cervical discectomy, open reduction, and fixation with an interbody cage and plate. In addition, he also had a posterior fixation of C5 and C6 with lateral mass screws, but no decompression, and the posterior elements were not disturbed. Postoperatively, there were no complications, and the patient made an excellent recovery. Although he did not return to playing rugby, he pursued several other sporting activities as before his injury and returned to full-time work as an account manager.

The patient was seen in the follow-up clinic three months after his operation. At this stage, X-rays of the cervical spine were obtained, and implant position and spinal alignment were deemed satisfactory. The patient then moved out of the area and was therefore not followed up further at our institution.

Four years later, the patient re-presented to the outpatient clinic with lower back pain. Investigations failed to reveal a surgical cause for this pain, but during the consultation, the patient mentioned that two months prior to this clinic appointment, he had experienced a sudden onset of severe occipital headache and neck pain without any trauma or precipitating factors. He did not seek medical advice at the time, and over the following month the symptoms resolved. There was no neurological deficit.

MRI and CT scans of the cervical spine, as well as flexion and extension cervical spine X-rays, were arranged. These investigations showed that the rods of the posterior instrumentation had become loose and migrated cranially. One rod was situated in the vicinity of the foramen magnum, and the second rod was located in the musculature of the left lateral neck at the levels of C2 to C4, with the tip embedded in the lamina of C2. The MRI scan confirmed that one rod was in an intradural position. However, fixation at C5/6 appeared solid, with bony consolidation around the disc and cage.

The patient was taken to theater the following day for removal of the dislodged rods. Through a midline incision cranial to the previous approach, the bony spine was exposed from C1 to C4. The right-sided rod was found in the paraspinal muscles around C2 and C3 and partially embedded into the C2 lamina; this rod was removed. The left-sided rod was visible through a partially healed dural defect between the C1 and C2 laminae, although no active cerebrospinal fluid leak was observed. A C1 laminectomy and widening of the foramen magnum were performed. The dura was opened over C1, and the rod was visible through the arachnoid layer. The arachnoid membrane was opened, and the rod was removed without complications. The dura was repaired and closed.

The patient made an uneventful recovery and remained neurologically intact. He was reviewed in the outpatient clinic ten days postoperatively. Flexion and extension X-rays of the cervical spine obtained three months postoperatively were entirely satisfactory, as were the X-rays at his two-year follow-up visit.

## Discussion

Instrumented fusion is an important tool in the treatment of unstable traumatic spinal injuries. Although there are guidelines detailing the indications for these instrumented fusions [1], there is no consensus on how to follow these patients up. Most clinicians have devised their own schedules according to the individual case and the specific fixation system used. In our institution, we routinely follow patients with traumatic cervical spinal injuries who have been fused with 360-degree fixation for one year using a combination of X-rays and CT scans.

During the primary procedure in the patient discussed in this report, there were no complications or untoward incidents. The surgeon was an experienced spine surgeon familiar with spinal instrumentation and torque devices for tightening of the caps and felt that satisfactory positioning and fixation of the metalwork had been achieved. Clinically, the patient made a very good recovery and returned to normal levels of activity. He denied experiencing any neck problems during the following four years. Due to the patient moving house, he was last seen in clinic three months postoperatively with flexion and extension cervical spine X-rays.

There have been sporadic reports of intradural migration of metal fixation devices in the literature, especially involving wires [2,3]. Migration of a spinal fixation rod and screw from an occipitocervical fusion has also been reported [4]. However, intradural migration of spinal fixation rods from C5/6 into the foramen magnum has not previously been reported, particularly in the absence of posterior decompression. In this case, the rods migrated from the C5/6 level along the laminae, and one rod pierced the dura between C1 and C2. Despite migration of the entire rod, the spinal cord was not injured.

In previously reported cases, it was not proven that associated intracranial complications were caused by the migration of the spinal rods, and migration was therefore assumed to represent chronic hardware failure. In our case, although there was no neurological dysfunction, the rod lay adjacent to the upper spinal cord and cerebellum and therefore required removal.

We believe that the reason for failure of this fusion may lie in failure of the torque screwdriver or the tightening technique. In older fixation systems, the surgeon tightened the caps onto the rods and screws by hand, judging tightness by the applied force. In current fixation systems, a torque screwdriver is employed, which shows external markers to guide the surgeon as to the tightness achieved. However, there is no way for the surgeon to check whether the torque applied corresponds to the torque indicated by the markers, and it is possible that the mechanism fails gradually over time. We therefore recommend that manufacturers regularly check the accuracy of torque mechanisms.

It is our suspicion that the rods migrated slowly over the years and that if the patient had been followed up for longer than three months, X-rays would have shown early movement of the implants. As the patient moved out of the area, follow-up may have been insufficient to detect early signs of rod migration.

This case illustrates the importance of ensuring that surgeons and theater teams are completely familiar with the spinal fixation systems they use. It also highlights the need for adequate long-term follow-up and investigation of new symptoms to detect delayed instrumentation-related complications. There may be a need for guidelines regarding the type and duration of follow-up, particularly in the context of pressures to reduce follow-up periods in modern healthcare systems and increasing patient awareness of the advantages and disadvantages of specific investigations.
