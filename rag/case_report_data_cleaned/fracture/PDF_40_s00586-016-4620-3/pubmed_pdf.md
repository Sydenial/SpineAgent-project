# Successfully treated transoral crossbow injury to the axial spine causing mild neurologic deficit: case presentation

## Abstract

Purpose: To detail the management, complications, and results of a crossbow arrow injury, where the broadhead went through the mouth, tongue, soft palate, C2 vertebra, spinal canal, dural sack, exiting the neck posteriorly, and the arrow shaft lodged in the spine causing mild spinal cord injury.  
Methods: Case presentation.  
Results: A penetrating axial cervical spine crossbow injury was treated successfully despite the following interdisciplinary complications: meningitis, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, re-bleeding, and cardiac arrest. The shaft was removed from the neck, and C1-3 dorsal stabilization was performed. Controlled Computed Tomography (CT) showed adequate implant position. After 4 months, the patient's fine motor skills improved, and he became able to button his shirt on his own, eat, and drink without any help. Additionally, he was able to walk without support. At the time of control at the outpatient clinic, his behavior was adequate: he cooperated with the examining doctor and answered with short sentences, although his psychomotor skills were slightly slower.  
Conclusions: Although bow and crossbow spine injuries are rare nowadays, they still occur. The removal of a penetrating missile resulting in such a spinal injury required a unique solution. General considerations, such as securing the airway, leaving the penetrating arrow in the neck and immobilizing both the arrow and neck for transport, thorough diagnostic imaging, preventing cerebrospinal fluid leakage, administering prophylactic antibiotics with broad coverage, and stabilizing the spine if required, are advised.  
Keywords: Arrow · Spinal injury · Crossbow · Axial spine · Transoral  

## Introduction

This case report presents a suicide attempt (a severe cervical spine injury caused by a crossbow arrow fired at close range) with its complications, treatment, and results. A crossbow spine injury is an extremely rare event: there have been only four known arrow-caused cervical spine injury reports in the medical literature: two are crossbow arrow injuries (one modern and one medieval) and two are from other types of bows. In none of these previous cases did the crossbow arrow completely penetrate the neck and the spinal canal, splitting the C2 vertebra while the patient remained alive. In the modern-age case of crossbow injury, the arrow penetrated the oral cavity and stopped in the spinal canal, passing between the dens and lateral mass of the C1 vertebra, bypassing the spinal cord. In this case, the arrow's broadhead, which penetrated the mouth and pierced the tongue, caused a dens fracture and sagittal splitting of the body of the C2 vertebra. The arrow continued through the neck and exited posteriorly, with the shaft remaining lodged in the spine.

## Case Presentation

A 57-year-old male, attempting suicide, shot himself in the mouth at close range with a crossbow. The broadhead went through the patient's mouth and exited posteriorly through his neck, with the shaft lodging in the patient. The ambulance crew found the patient conscious, laying him on his side as the arrow prevented a supine position. The patient's head was supported and stabilized by bubble wrap on-site, and he was transported to the hospital's emergency department. At the time of admission, the patient was somnolent and could communicate only by moving his limbs, eyes, and eyelids. He was able to move all his extremities against gravity, and major palsy was not detected. The patient was sedated, and a tracheotomy was performed, through which artificial ventilation was started. Both the broadhead and the end of the arrow were removed by cutting them from the shaft, but the middle section of the arrow remained in the patient. Complex head and cervical spine CT scanning was performed, and scans showed the lodged arrow shaft running through the mouth cavity and pharynx, penetrating the tongue, soft palate, C2 vertebra, and spinal canal, and finally partially protruding from the neck posteriorly. The artifact caused by the shaft made the CT scan evaluation difficult, although it showed the arrow penetrating the C2 vertebra at the junction of the dens and body, causing the sagittal split of the C2 body. A Type II dens fracture could be suspected. From the posterior cortex of the C2 vertebra, the broadhead penetrated the spinal canal on the right side, between the C1 posterior arch and C2 lamina.

## Procedure

The patient was transported to the operating room (OR) for surgery after blood tests were conducted, where a halo brace was placed on him in the supine position while his head with the protruding shaft was held by an assistant. The halo frame prevented the posterior end of the shaft from touching the table in the supine position. After repositioning the patient to the prone position, both lateral sides of the halo frame were elevated from the table with padding, keeping the ventral end of the shaft from coming in contact with the table. Prophylactic antibiotics were administered intravenously. A posterior cervical surgery approach was then performed. A slightly curved incision was applied instead of the routine midline skin incision, involving the outlet of the shaft. After midline dissection of ligamentum nuchae, the injured trapezius, splenius capitis, and semispinalis capitis muscles were cut transversally until the shaft on the right side lay exposed. This transection allowed for the subperiosteal detachment of the muscles from the spine. The spinal exposure was accomplished from the occiput to the C6 vertebra, exposing the shaft in the spinal canal between the C1 posterior arch and the C2 lamina, piercing the atlanto-axial posterior membrane. A C2 laminectomy was performed, and the posterior dural tear caused by the broadhead was observed. The spinal cord appeared intact. The shaft was pulled out with extreme care in the direction of the back of the neck. The dural tear was lateral and could not be surgically sutured, with massive intradural venous bleeding from the cranial direction of the dural tear. A C1 posterior arch was removed, and an additional midline dural incision was made to identify the source of the bleeding. The majority of the bleeding originated from the ventral venous plexus, which was stopped using absorbable gelatin (Spongostan). The Spongostan was used to close the ventral dural opening and secure the mucous membrane in the oral cavity. A C1-3 dorsal vertebral stabilization was considered due to the Type II dens fracture, sagittal split of the C2 body, and loosened left C2 pedicle. C1 and C3 lateral mass screws and C2 pedicular screws were inserted, creating stable C1-C3 dorsal fixation. The skin wound caused by the arrow was excised to receive fresh skin edges, and drains were left in the wound. The posterior cervical wound was closed routinely, and the patient was repositioned in the supine position. There was no significant bleeding from the oral cavity wound during the procedure. An ENT specialist coagulated the tongue and soft palate wounds using bipolar forceps and closed the pharyngeal mucous membrane with absorbable sutures. The halo brace was removed immediately after the procedure, and the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit, where a lumbar drain was inserted to divert cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).

## Postoperative Management and Complications

Due to the pharyngeal injury, the patient was fed through a nasogastric tube and received fluids intravenously. Intravenous prophylactic antibiotics were continued. Sedation (Propofol) was stopped on the first postoperative day, and the patient regained consciousness. He could communicate with gestures and articulation, drinking beside the tracheostomy. No palsy was observed, and the patient was transferred to the neurosurgery department. The patient was found disoriented and agitated, with mild ataxia and impaired fine motor movements. A control CT scan confirmed appropriate screw placement and the suspected Type II dens fracture. Despite prolonged antibiotic prophylaxis, meningitis and fever evolved on the second postoperative day. The antibiotic spectrum was broadened and changed to specific treatment according to the CSF and blood culture results (E. coli). With the improvement of meningitis, the patient became oriented.

## First Reoperation

Nine days after the original surgery, the patient's neck swelled massively posteriorly, suggesting hemorrhage in the dorsal cervical wound. CT angiography was performed, but no vertebral artery injury was found. The re-bleeding was epidural, providing mass effect. Although the patient's neurological status did not worsen, the epidural bleeding with significant size threatened the spinal cord by compression, leading to emergency surgery. The dorsal cervical wound was reopened, and the epidural blood clot was removed. Severe bleeding was noted between the occiput and the C1 posterior arch, from the direction of the vertebral artery. Hemodynamic instability occurred, and Ligaclips were placed on the left vertebral artery course at the posterior arch of C1 to stop the bleeding. After reanimation, the patient stabilized. The wound was rinsed with antiseptic agents and saline, then closed layer by layer over two drains. The patient regained consciousness and moved all extremities, maintaining stable cardiopulmonary status. He was admitted to the neurosurgery department with no significant changes in his senso-motor condition.

## Second Reoperation

The original dural tear was identified as the source of CSF leakage. The tear was sutured as best as possible, and the hole was covered with TachoSil, followed by a muscle patch and another TachoSil sheet. DuraSeal was applied to provide additional waterproofing. The muscle, fascia, and cutaneous skin layers were sutured. A new lumbar drain was inserted. Control CT or MRI was not performed, as the patient did not develop symptoms of vertebral artery occlusion, and his neurological condition improved. After the second reoperation, the wound healed without CSF fistula, and the patient's fever receded. The tracheostomy tube was removed on the 30th postoperative day, and the patient was fed through a nasogastric tube for 2 days. He was allowed to take water orally on the third postoperative day and solids on the 35th day.

## Outpatient Clinical Control

Six weeks after the injury, the patient was mostly oriented, though still undergoing psychiatric treatment. He cooperated with the examining doctor, and his psychomotor skills had slightly decreased. He was able to walk without support, and his behavior remained stable after 4 months. At the final follow-up, the patient's amnesia about the treatment remained, but he was able to recognize his suicide attempt. His fine motor skills had improved, and he could button his shirt and eat independently. There were no major palsies, and his deep tendon reflexes were normal. Mild spinal ataxia was observed in his walking pattern, but he was able to walk without support. He had no sensory deficit or issues with urination or defecation. The posterior neck wound healed, and follow-up X-rays showed stable fixation without screw loosening.

## Discussion

There is no other crossbow injury case in the medical literature where an arrow entered through the mouth, causing a C2 vertebra split and Type II dens fracture, then went through the spine and lodged in the neck, with the broadhead coming out under the occiput, resulting in minimal neurologic deficit.

Although modern warfare does not use bows (including crossbows), the growing popularity of hunting and sport archery highlights the importance of special considerations in treating this rare injury. In this case, the broadhead traveled through the spinal canal without damaging the spinal cord, which was purely lucky. The mild ataxia and fine motor skill disturbances suggest the arrow grazed the spinal cord.

## Conclusions

Removing a spine-penetrating foreign body that does not cause major neurological deficits can be a challenge requiring immense preparation and evaluation of surgical options. Treatment continues after the removal of the foreign object, as complications may arise and require special solutions.
