# Delayed chance fracture pattern injury in a case of skeletal fluorosis

## Abstract

**Purpose**  
To document a rare complication of a delayed “chance fracture pattern”-type injury through the proximal end of a pedicle screw construct in the clinical scenario of skeletal fluorosis.

**Methods**  
A 72-year-old man with fluorosis presented following a fall which resulted in a T12–L1 fracture. Investigations revealed an unstable three-column injury, so the patient was treated with surgical stabilisation using pedicle screw fixation from T11 to L2. He presented 1 month following surgery with worsening back pain. Investigations revealed a fracture through T11 in a “chance fracture pattern” along the pedicle screw tracts at the proximal end of the construct. An extension of fixation was performed proximally to T8 and he made an uneventful recovery showing fusion at 20-month follow-up.

**Results**  
Complication of delayed pedicle fractures, in a “chance fracture pattern” at the ends of pedicle screw fixation constructs, are rarely reported in the literature. The occurrence of such a complication in a hyperostotic spine associated with fluorosis makes this a unique clinical scenario which is previously unreported to the best of our knowledge.

**Conclusions**  
This report highlights a very rare complication of chance fracture pattern injury in the clinical scenario of fluorosis. A hyperostotic stiff spine, poor quality of bone, and extension of pedicle screw tracts to the anterior cortex during primary surgery may have resulted in the occurrence of this rare complication.

## Introduction

Pedicle fractures are seen often as an intra-operative complication or as a delayed stress fracture following pedicle screw instrumentation and posterolateral fusion procedures [1]. Chance fracture occurring through the upper or lower instrumented spine is very rare and has been reported in patients with cerebral palsy and osteoporotic spine. We present a unique case of a chance fracture pattern injury occurring through the upper instrumented vertebra following pedicle screw fixation in a case of skeletal fluorosis.

## Case Report

A 72-year-old gentleman presented to our emergency department following an accidental fall at home with severe back pain and difficulty in ambulation. The patient was a known case of fluorosis which was diagnosed following an intertrochanteric fracture 2 years prior to the current presentation. He had severe tenderness over the thoracolumbar junction with intact neurology. Radiographs revealed a hyperostotic stiff spine, diffuse ligament calcification, and a break in continuity in the anterior syndesmophytes.

Computed tomography showed an undisplaced carrot stick-type fracture at T12–L1 extending along the disc end plate junction. Surgical stabilisation was planned in view of a three-column injury and fused spinal segments above and below the fracture level. Posterior instrumented stabilisation and fusion from T11 to L2 was done with pedicle screw fixation under intra-operative CT navigation.

As the bone was hard and sclerotic, pedicle screw tracts were prepared carefully. The pedicle screw entry point was prepared with a high-speed cutting burr. The screw tract was then prepared with a power drill using a 3.2-mm drill bit. The tract was frequently probed and trajectory was confirmed with a reference tool to ensure accurate screw placement. Once prepared, the tract was tapped with a 5.5-mm hand-held tap. Titanium screws of 6.5 mm diameter and 45 mm length were inserted at T12–L2, and 40 mm screws were inserted at T11 bilaterally.

The intra-operative course was uneventful, and the patient made an excellent post-operative recovery. Immediate postoperative radiographs showed satisfactory implant position with no evidence of a fracture line at the proximal instrumented vertebra. The patient was mobilised on postoperative day 2 and discharged after 6 days.

One month following surgery, the patient presented with progressive pain over the operative site, limiting activities. The surgical wound had healed well, with no local signs of inflammation and normal neurology. Radiographs showed a faint fracture line extending along the pedicle screw tract up to the anterior cortex at T11. A CT scan confirmed a fracture extending along the upper instrumented level along the pedicle screw tract in the configuration of a chance fracture, involving all three columns. Although no translation was noted, the unstable fracture pattern prompted extension of stabilisation up to T8.

The patient experienced good pain relief and made an excellent recovery. At 20-month follow-up, the fracture had healed well with a good functional outcome.

## Discussion

Pedicle screw instrumentation has been associated with complications such as malpositioning of screws, neurological injury, screw loosening or back-out, loss of curve correction, intra-operative pedicle fracture, and pseudoarthrosis [1]. Stress fractures of the proximal pedicles following instrumented posterolateral fusion have been described, attributed to cantilever motion at the junction of the fusion mass and persistent anterior disc motion [2,3]. These fractures typically occur at the junction of the pedicle with the vertebral body rather than along the long axis of the pedicle.

Chance fracture through the proximal or distal end of an instrumented spinal segment is rare. Such fractures have been reported in pediatric patients, individuals with cerebral palsy, osteoporotic kyphosis, and rigid spinal deformities [4–6]. Contributing factors described include rigid spinal implants, poor bone quality, junctional stress, and technical factors such as cortical breach during instrumentation.

In the present case, a chance fracture pattern occurred through the proximal instrumented vertebra in a stiff spine due to skeletal fluorosis. Although by definition a chance fracture is not iatrogenic and typically involves posterior osteoligamentous disruption, the fracture pattern in this case closely resembled a chance fracture configuration involving the vertebral body and pedicle.

Hyperostotic conditions such as ankylosing spondylitis and fluorosis predispose to stiff spines that are vulnerable to fractures. Despite their sclerotic radiographic appearance, fluorotic bones are brittle due to fluoride incorporation into hydroxyapatite and are often associated with osteomalacia, further compromising bone quality [7–10].

In this case, multiple factors may have contributed to fracture development, including advanced age, poor bone quality from fluorosis, and surgical technical factors such as parallel screw tracts, breach of the anterior cortex, and hubbing of pedicle screws. The use of power drilling in dense, brittle bone may have created stress risers, with cyclic postoperative loading propagating the fracture along the screw tract. Extension of fusion was the most reliable means of restoring stability, as fractures extending into the vertebral body compromise construct integrity.

Preventive strategies may include using multiple fixation points, avoiding parallel pedicle screw tracts, preserving the anterior cortex, careful sequential tapping, and appropriate rod contouring to minimise stress at the bone–implant interface. A high index of suspicion is necessary when evaluating postoperative pain in patients with stiff spines, and CT imaging is essential for early detection of fractures. Early recognition and intervention can prevent displacement and neurological complications.

## Conclusion

This report highlights a very rare complication of a chance fracture pattern in the unique clinical scenario of skeletal fluorosis. A hyperostotic stiff spine, poor bone quality, and extension of pedicle screw tracts to the anterior cortex during primary surgery may have contributed to the occurrence of this complication.
