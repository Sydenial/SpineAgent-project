# Anterior approach for a stab wound with penetrating rebar injury causing incomplete cauda equina syndrome in lumbosacral spine lesion: a case report and literature review

## Abstract

**Purpose** To report a unique case of incomplete cauda equina syndrome (CES) following a rebar penetrating injury in the perineal region with a retropulsed fragment, which was treated with an anterior approach, and to discuss a suitable surgical approach.

**Methods** Incomplete cauda equina syndrome caused by non-missile penetrating injury is extremely rare. A 26-year-old male patient presented with incomplete cauda equina syndrome due to a penetrating rebar wound extending from the perineal region to the lumbosacral spine. Computed tomography demonstrated a bony fragment broken from the S1 body compressing into the spinal canal.

**Results** Using an anterior approach, we performed partial corpectomy of L5, decompression by retrieving the bony fragment, and L5–S1 interbody fusion. The patient had a significant recovery, and no clinical complication was found after over 2-year follow-up.

**Conclusion** It is challenging to determine the optimal strategy of surgical treatment for penetrating spinal injuries with retained foreign bodies. Here we suggest an anterior approach in situations where it has the advantage of effectively performing decompression and preventing iatrogenic damage to the thecal sac and nerve rootlets.

**Keywords** Penetrating injury; lumbosacral spine trauma; stab wound; incomplete cauda equina syndrome; anterior approach.

## Introduction

Cauda equina syndrome (CES) manifests as a triad of symptoms including saddle anesthesia, urine and/or bowel dysfunction, and motor paralysis combined with back discomfort. CES is distinguished as complete or incomplete according to the severity of urination symptoms. Complete CES presents with loss of bladder control, including difficulty with micturition and urinary leakage because of loss of detrusor function. Incomplete CES is characterized by reduced urinary sensation, lack of the urge to urinate, a weak urine stream, and a need to strain during urination [1–3].

Central lumbar disc herniation is the most common cause of CES; less common causes include postoperative hematoma, neoplasm, trauma, complications after chemonucleolysis, and penetrating injuries [1, 3].

Penetrating injuries are less commonly reported as the cause of CES. Gunshot or stab wounds are the main types of penetrating spinal injury. Spinal gunshot injuries are more frequently reported and have a higher prevalence of neurologic impairment. In contrast, neurologic damage following stab wounds has been reported as extremely rare in clinical circumstances, but surgical treatment has been associated with better neurologic recovery [4, 5].

CES requires urgent surgical decompression. Central lumbar disc herniation, the most common cause of CES, is often sufficiently decompressed with posterior laminectomy. However, if a solid and large structure located in front of the dura mater causes compression, isolated posterior decompression may not allow complete neurologic recovery, and the operator should consider the risk of iatrogenic dural tear and complications due to dural compression [2, 6]. Especially in traumatic spinal injuries with retropulsed fragments and incomplete neurologic deficits, posterior laminectomy alone may leave unrelieved tension on nerve roots and can progress neurologic deficit due to a bowstring effect of neural tethering [7, 9, 10].

There is no definitive study establishing superiority between anterior and posterior surgery, and combined surgery should sometimes be considered. The optimal treatment strategy should account for the patient’s circumstances, including injury mechanism and stability of the posterior osteoligamentous structures.

Here we report a unique case of incomplete CES following a rebar penetrating injury in the perineal region with a retropulsed fragment, treated using an anterior approach, and we discuss a suitable surgical approach for the bony fragment with a literature review.

## Case Report

A 26-year-old male patient presented to our hospital after falling from a height of one meter onto a rebar that vertically penetrated his perineal region (Fig. 1). The emergency rescue team found him impaled on the steel bar and could not remove the rebar because it was firmly fixed to the ground. As there was no fluid leakage or active bloody discharge, they rescued him by lifting him off the rebar and transported him to our emergency department as soon as possible.

On neurologic examination, voluntary anal contraction was absent and deep anal sensation was markedly decreased. Voluntary urination was also lost. Other motor and sensory functions were preserved. No significant accompanying injury such as abdominal or urethral lesion was identified based on specialist assessment.

Computed tomography (CT) showed that the rebar penetrated the posterior margin of the S1 body and broke a cylindrical bony fragment (20 × 15 × 13 mm). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated that this bony fragment compressed the thecal sac anteriorly at the level of the fifth lumbar vertebral body (Fig. 2).

The patient was diagnosed with incomplete CES, and decompression surgery was performed within 8 h after injury. The surgery lasted 3 h 40 min, including autogenous fibular strut bone harvest, with an estimated blood loss of 1.902 L.

We performed an anterior (trans-abdominal) approach with microscopic magnification and partial corpectomy of L5 to create a retrieval window of approximately $(21\times16\times14\ \mathrm{mm})$ to remove the S1 bony fragment (20 × 15 × 13 mm), followed by reconstruction with an autogenous fibular strut bone graft (Fig. 3).

The rebar penetrated the posterior longitudinal ligament and the outer layer of the ligamentum flavum through the interlaminar space between L5 and S1, extending to the right side of the spinous process and the tip of the L4 spinous process. A bony fragment from the tip of the L4 spinous process was displaced to the level of the L3 spinous process. There was almost no damage to the dura and no cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage. After decompression, we performed interbody fusion between L5 and S1 using an autogenous fibular strut bone graft with a fragmentary screw and bone graft at the site of partial corpectomy (Fig. 4).

Voluntary anal contraction markedly improved immediately postoperatively, and voluntary urination was possible 4 weeks after surgery. We recommended absolute bed rest for 2 weeks and prescribed a low back brace for 6 weeks. The patient began walking with a manual wheelchair after bed rest and then walked independently after 3 days of wheelchair use without subsidence of the autogenous fibular bone graft. Immediate postoperative CT and follow-up plain radiographs showed little subsidence of the autogenous fibular strut bone graft. MRI confirmed expansion and decompression of the cauda equina; however, follow-up CT was not performed to evaluate bony healing and fusion. The patient was followed for over 2 years without clinical complications. Serial radiographs showed slight subsidence of the autogenous fibular strut bone graft, but stability and lumbar lordosis remained well aligned (Fig. 5).

## Discussion

The most frequent causes of spinal injuries include falls, motor vehicle accidents, and sports-related mishaps. Compared to injuries caused by blunt trauma, penetrating spinal injuries are uncommon [5], and the most typical causes are bullets and knives. Penetrating rebar injuries to the spine are extremely rare, with only one similar case reported in the literature. Zhou, Z. S. reported a case with a similar mechanism treated using a posterior approach. In contrast, we report a unique case of CES following a rebar penetrating injury in the perineal region with a retropulsed fragment decompressed using an anterior approach [10].

CES is a complex neurologic disorder managed as a surgical emergency. Traumatic cauda equina injuries are a frequent cause of abrupt and acute neurologic deterioration. Assessment should identify lower extremity sensory and motor impairments during the initial emergency evaluation [5].

On radiographic evaluation, anteroposterior and lateral spine radiographs should be obtained and evaluated promptly. In emergency situations, CT is the ideal investigative modality. If the composition of the foreign body is uncertain—especially when it is close to important structures—or when a patient has a neurologic deficit without clear evidence of canal compression or a trajectory across the canal, MRI can be helpful [5, 11].

Immediate radiologic evaluation provides insight into the cause of CES, including fractured bony anatomy, foreign material, and tissue material such as disc herniation. Clear definition supports more objective judgments about stability and three-dimensional relationships between the canal and the cauda equina before surgical decision-making.

After evaluation and diagnosis of CES, emergency surgical decompression is recommended, particularly in incomplete CES. However, the timing of surgery remains controversial [2, 3, 6, 12–14]. Ahn et al. performed a meta-analysis of 332 CES cases (published in 2000) and suggested no evidence of neurologic improvement between patients treated before 24 h and those treated within 24–48 h, but reported significant improvement in CES symptoms when treatment occurred within 48 h [6]. Kohles et al. raised concerns regarding methodology and interpretation of that meta-analysis and emphasized prompt surgical intervention because injured nerve tissue may progressively deteriorate due to swelling and tightening [13]. In 2005, Todd N.V. suggested that patients treated within 24 h could be expected to have more sufficient recovery from bladder dysfunction [7], though limitations of the analysis were also noted, including inappropriate summary statistics and heterogeneity across studies [14]. Chau, A.M. et al. reported a systematic critical review (2014) and concluded there was no evidence supporting a strict 48 h threshold; both early and delayed surgery may improve neurologic deficits, but earlier intervention may yield greater clinical improvement given the continuous biologic progression of nerve injury [14].

In our case, anterior decompression was performed within 8 h after injury, and we consider that earlier decompression contributed to earlier recovery from bladder dysfunction.

This patient presented with incomplete CES due to an anteriorly compressive retropulsed fracture fragment of S1, and we also considered infection risk due to the penetrating injury through the perineal region. We therefore chose anterior decompression with an autogenous fibular strut bone graft. Nevertheless, determining the optimal surgical strategy for CES remains challenging.

In general, posterior approaches are widely used for degenerative diseases and commonly provide excellent access to neural structures through a single incision, and they are familiar to spinal surgeons. However, when a hard structure compresses the dura anteriorly, isolated posterior laminectomy may not achieve adequate thecal sac decompression and may be associated with bowstring effects of neural tethering. In such cases, incomplete reduction and limited neurologic recovery may necessitate additional anterior decompression [7, 9, 19].

Anterior approaches are commonly used for infection and tumor removal, neural decompression, deformity reconstruction, and spinal fusion. Advantages include excellent exposure of the disc space with rapid endplate preparation and the ability to insert larger implants, which may improve lordosis, foraminal height, and fusion rate [19].

Kirkpatrick et al. reported that patients with incomplete deficits—especially those with large retropulsed fragments and marked canal compromise—may benefit from anterior decompression to provide adequate visualization of the anterior dura in traumatic thoracolumbar fractures. Kingwell, S.P. reported that anterior decompression may offer potential benefits for bladder recovery in traumatic conus medullaris and cauda equina injuries [7, 20, 21]. However, surgeons should consider prior abdominal surgery and adhesions, and vascular injury remains a potentially devastating complication of anterior approaches. Mobbs, R. J. also reported that high-grade spondylolisthesis (more than grade 2) is a contraindication to anterior approaches without posterior fusion [19, 20].

In our patient, the trans-perineal penetrating trauma produced a large retropulsed S1 fragment that nearly completely compromised the canal at L5–S1. First, posterior laminectomy alone might not allow complete removal of a large retropulsed fragment without iatrogenic dural tear or additional facetectomy, and neurologic compromise could persist due to bowstring effects. Second, because the rebar tract did not injure the facet joints or posterior ligament complex, we considered that additional posterior fusion or instrumentation was not necessary. Third, placing a cage could increase infection risk after a penetrating injury.

We therefore performed partial corpectomy of the L5 vertebral body via an anterior approach to remove the S1 fracture fragment. After fragment removal, the compressed dura expanded fully. Stability was reinforced by reconstructing the partial corpectomy site using an autogenous fibular strut bone graft.

## Conclusion

Incomplete CES caused by non-missile penetrating injury is extremely rare. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of CES following a rebar penetrating injury in the perineal region that was decompressed using an anterior approach in a literature review. Although anterior approaches carry a risk of vascular injury, posterior decompression may not allow complete neurologic recovery in the presence of a retropulsed fragment due to bowstring effects of neural tethering. We suggest that an anterior approach for sacral lesions with retropulsed fragments can help overcome potential neurologic injury risks, enable effective decompression, and prevent iatrogenic injuries to the thecal sac and nerve rootlets.
