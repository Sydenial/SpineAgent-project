# Management of Fracture Dorso-Lumbar Spine in a Pregnant Female by Surgery in Prone Position

## Abstract

Surgical management of thoraco-lumbar spine fracture in pregnancy has specific concerns during surgery like positioning, approach, and radiation exposure to fetus. We report a case of burst fracture of 12th thoracic vertebrae with paraplegia in a young female with 26 weeks of gestation. Surgery was performed in prone position on a Toronto frame. This report also discusses the management of thoraco-lumbar spine fracture in pregnancy with review of literature.

Keywords: Dorso-lumbar fracture, Pregnancy, Toronto frame

## Introduction

Fracture spine in pregnancy is uncommon. They occur following traumatic events; however, relative osteoporosis of pregnancy is found to be associated. There are only a few reports which discuss the management of thoraco-lumbar spine fracture in pregnancy. We report a case of 26-week pregnancy with traumatic fracture of the twelfth thoracic vertebra (D12) with paraplegia, which was operated in prone position on a Toronto frame.

## Case History

A 28-year-old female with 26 weeks of gestation (G3P2L2) had a history of falling from a ladder, arriving at the casualty after being referred from another primary care center. She presented 36 hours after the fall with severe back pain and inability to move both lower limbs. She had no history of vaginal bleeding, pain in the abdomen, or loss of consciousness. Her pulse was 88/min, blood pressure 128/76 mmHg. Her fundal height was approximately 26 weeks, fetal heart rate was 156/min. Per vaginal examination was normal. Spine examination revealed tenderness at the dorso-lumbar junction. Neurologically, the patient had total spinal cord injury below the L1 segment level, which was classified as American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) grade A neurological impairment. Knee and ankle reflexes were exaggerated. Urgent ultrasonography (USG) of the abdomen showed a normal fetus with no other organ injury.

After obtaining consent from the patient, a plain radiograph of the thoraco-lumbar spine was taken. The X-ray showed anterior wedging of the D12 vertebra. MRI showed burst fracture of the D12 vertebra with cord compression. A decision for posterior surgical decompression was made, after explaining the risks to both the maternal and fetal health. The Toronto frame was assembled and fixed to the radiolucent operating table. The patient was placed in a prone position on the Toronto frame, and the height of the frame was adjusted to ensure the abdomen remained free. The major advantage of this frame was minimal pressure on the abdomen or pelvis, while the patient was supported in a prone position by her shoulder and iliac crest.

Before the procedure, fetal heart sounds (FHS) were assessed with a handheld Doppler, and intraoperative monitoring of FHS was done using a cardiotocogram machine with an FHS belt. Care was taken to avoid blood loss and hypovolemia, which could lead to fetal bradycardia. If fetal bradycardia occurred, it would be managed with rapid intravenous Ringer's lactate infusion, high-flow oxygen inhalation, and, if necessary, the left lateral position. The procedure was performed rapidly, with careful attention to hemostasis to prevent hemorrhage. A D12 laminectomy with posterior decompression and short-segment pedicular screw fixation was done. The postoperative period was uneventful, and Doppler done immediately after the surgery showed no evidence of fetal distress. The patient's neurological status remained the same during the indoor stay. Upon discharge, paraplegia care instructions were given, and the patient was mobilized in a wheelchair with a Taylor’s brace. At 39 weeks of pregnancy, the patient delivered a healthy 2.8-kg baby by cesarean section under general anesthesia. At a 2-year follow-up, the patient remained paraplegic with partial recovery of bowel and bladder function.

## Discussion

The management of spine fractures in pregnancy is poorly described in orthopaedics literature. Most of the reported cases discuss the obstetric aspect of this issue. Surgical indications involve a comparison of the relative risks and benefits of two different treatment methods. Surgical management of spinal fracture in pregnancy carries risks to both mother and fetus due to positioning, blood loss, and inherent risks of anesthesia. Conservative management, on the other hand, precludes early mobilization, exposing the patient to hazards such as deep venous thrombosis (a major pregnancy risk factor) and pulmonary complications. An individualized approach is thus recommended in managing spinal surgery in pregnancy. It is always prudent to postpone surgery until after delivery if the patient is near term; however, in earlier weeks of pregnancy, unstable spinal column injury and incomplete neurological deficit are definite indications to consider surgery.

The surgical approach and positioning for these thoraco-lumbar fractures in pregnancy is a debatable issue. Anterior approach has the advantage of avoiding pressure on the gravid uterus. However, in later weeks of gestation, when the gravid uterus reaches up to the xiphisternum, approaching the thoraco-lumbar spine via an anterior approach becomes difficult. The posterior approach has disadvantages, such as pressure on the gravid uterus due to positioning and the need for radiation exposure during pedicular screw fixation. 

Relton et al. described a specialized frame (Toronto frame) for reducing blood loss in scoliosis surgery in the prone position by relieving extrinsic pressure on the inferior vena cava (IVC). The frame consists of four supports with a 45° inward tilt, arranged in two V-shaped pairs supporting the lateral aspects of the upper thoracic cage and the antero-lateral aspects of the pelvic girdle between the iliac crests and the greater trochanters. With suitable adjustment, this frame provides adequate support and prevents extrinsic pressure on the abdomen. The utilization of the same frame in our patient was advantageous, as we were able to give a prone position without causing external pressure on the gravid uterus.

Radiation exposure is a significant concern in stabilizing spinal fractures using the posterior approach. Potential biological effects of in utero radiation exposure include prenatal death, intrauterine growth restriction, mental retardation, organ malformation, and childhood cancer. The gestational age and the level of absorbed dose are crucial factors in determining the occurrence of these effects. Most teratogenic effects of radiation occur with exposure during the first trimester, during the period of organogenesis. After the 25th week, major fetal malformations and functional anomalies are highly improbable. In our patient, due to the gestational age of 26 weeks, fluoroscopy for pedicular screw fixation was used, with careful attention to minimizing radiation exposure.

Pregnancy complicated by traumatic paraplegia carries risks to the fetus. Goller et al. performed a retrospective study on pregnancy in paraplegic patients and concluded that pregnancy complicated by paraplegia is more threatened than in normal circumstances due to:

1. The trauma itself.
2. The immediate post-traumatic situation of the patient.
3. Chronic infections and anemia in pregnancies following spinal cord injury.

They also found that malformations and disabilities occurred in patients who became paraplegic during pregnancy.
