# Post-traumatic Collet-Sicard Syndrome: Personal Observation and Review of the Pertinent Literature with Clinical, Radiologic, and Anatomic Considerations

## Abstract

The lesion of the lower four cranial nerves, commonly called Collet-Sicard syndrome, can be caused by blunt head and neck trauma. It may be associated with an isolated fracture of the occipital condyle or of the atlas. The aim of this report is to assess the modality of the trauma, the type of fracture, the anatomic characteristics, the treatment, and clinical results of this syndrome. We discuss 14 cases of fracture of the occipital condyle and atlas, as well as one personal case. Methods included an analysis of 14 cases collected from the literature between 1925 and 2013 and a personal case, along with an anatomical study of the paracondylar, atlas, and styloid process region. The anatomical dissection assessed the relationships in the site of transit where the nerves IX, X, XI, and XII are injured. The results showed that 9 cases were caused by condyle fractures and 5 by atlas fractures. The treatment varied between conservative immobilization and surgical procedures, with the majority of cases showing modest improvements. Conservative treatment is the option of choice, and surgical options are not considered to fix nerve damages.

**Keywords:** Collet-Sicard syndrome, Atlas, Occipital condyle, Vertebral fracture, Jefferson fractures

## Introduction

Collet-Sicard syndrome (CSS) is an unilateral paralysis of the lower cranial nerves (IX, X, XI, and XII). It is named after Justin Frederic Collet and Jean-Athanase Sicard, who independently described the syndrome. It is an uncommon syndrome caused by lesions of the skull base, such as primary or metastatic tumors, vascular lesions, inflammatory processes, iatrogenic complications, and trauma.

Closed head and neck injuries resulting in atlas or occipital condyle fractures may cause post-traumatic Collet-Sicard syndrome (p-CSS). This report presents a clinical case of such syndrome in a patient with a C1 fracture and reviews the literature on closed head trauma resulting in p-CSS. A cadaver dissection was performed to study the anatomical relationships between vascular, osteo-ligamentous structures, and the cranial nerves involved in this syndrome.

## Methods

We conducted a literature review of p-CSS cases, reported a personal case, and performed an anatomical study on cadavers.

## Literature Review

A computer-aided search of MEDLINE using PubMed was conducted from 1925 to 2013 to collect p-CSS cases in closed head and neck trauma. The search was performed using keywords such as "Collet-Sicard syndrome", "atlas fracture", and "condyle fracture", and was limited to human studies. A total of 13 traumatic cervical lesions associated with p-CSS were found, 9 of which were caused by condyle fractures and 4 by atlas fractures. The overall mean age was 44.2 years. The type of fracture was identified according to classifications reported in the literature. The classification system used for condyle fractures was the one proposed by Anderson and Montesano, while the anatomical classification for atlas fractures was that of Jefferson, updated by Landells and Van Peteghem.

## Results

The analysis of the 14 collected cases showed that 9 were caused by condyle fractures and 5 by atlas fractures. In the condyle fracture cases, the clinical results showed modest improvements in most cases. One case of atlas fracture had complete recovery, while others showed significant improvement. The majority of cases were treated conservatively with cervical immobilization, and only two cases required surgical procedures.

## Personal Observation

A 58-year-old man presented with a C1 fracture after falling from a horse. The patient was treated conservatively with a Philadelphia cervical collar. At the initial clinical exam, the patient showed signs of cranial nerve palsy, including hoarseness, difficulty swallowing, and tongue deviation. An MRI confirmed the right articular mass fracture of C1. The patient showed gradual improvement, with a slight dysphonia and residual tongue deviation remaining after 10 months.

## Anatomical Observation

An anatomical study using cadaveric specimens revealed that the lower four cranial nerves pass through a narrow space between the transverse process of the atlas and the styloid process. This space is crucial in understanding the mechanisms that lead to nerve injury in p-CSS. The dissection also highlighted the relationship between the occipital condyle, the atlas, and the cranial nerves.

## Discussion

Collet-Sicard syndrome is caused by lesions of the lower cranial nerves (IX, X, XI, and XII), leading to paralysis of the vocal cords, palate, trapezius muscle, and sternocleidomastoid muscle. The trauma mechanisms causing condyle or atlas fractures often involve axial forces, such as falls or accidents, resulting in compression and stretching of the cranial nerves. The anatomical observations from cadaver dissections provide insights into the narrow transit space through which these nerves pass, explaining the injuries sustained in p-CSS. 

The p-CSS associated with occipital condyle fractures generally results in more severe cranial nerve damage compared to atlas fractures, which tend to result in less severe nerve damage and better clinical outcomes.

## Atlas Fractures

Atlas fractures are generally caused by axial load or vertical compression of the atlas. The biomechanics of these fractures involve flexion, extension, lateral bending, and rotation forces. The anatomical dissection revealed that the lower cranial nerves pass through a narrow space between the atlas and the styloid process, which can become compressed during trauma. This explains the development of p-CSS in atlas fractures.

## Occipital Condyle Fractures

Occipital condyle fractures are often caused by condyle dislocation. The biomechanics of these fractures can lead to severe cranial nerve damage, with little potential for recovery. In contrast to atlas fractures, condyle fractures often result in more significant and permanent deficits.

## Conclusion

Post-traumatic Collet-Sicard syndrome is caused by occipital condyle fractures with dislocation of a fragment or by atlas fractures with articular mass dislocation, following trauma with force directed through the top of the head. The severity of nerve damage is greater in condyle fractures, leading to poor long-term outcomes. Atlas fractures, however, tend to result in less severe nerve damage and better recovery. These findings are supported by anatomical dissections that show how the lower cranial nerves transit through narrow spaces that are vulnerable to compression and stretching during trauma.
