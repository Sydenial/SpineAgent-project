# Delayed Presentation of Infected Common Iliac Artery Pseudoaneurysm Caused by Malpositioned Pedicle Screw After Minimally Invasive Scoliosis Surgery

## Abstract
Purpose: To report delayed onset common iliac artery perforation and infected pseudoaneurysm caused by a malpositioned pedicle screw after minimally invasive scoliosis surgery (MISS).

Methods: A 21-year-old female presented with a 1-week history of abrupt right-sided low back pain, lower abdominal pain, and fever, 18 months after MISS using cannulated pedicle screws. Paravertebral arterial erosion with pseudoaneurysm and retroperitoneal and paraspinal abscess were suspected.

Results: We performed resection of the pseudoaneurysm, vascular repair of the right common iliac artery by angioplasty with a bovine patch, and implant removal. At 6 months after surgery, she had no limitations or recurrence of symptoms and no loss of deformity.

Conclusions: Misplaced pedicle screws can cause potentially fatal complications, such as infected pseudoaneurysm, even in the late postoperative period.

## Introduction
Pedicle screw instrumentation has been used to treat various complex spinal disorders, including spinal deformity correction [1]. However, damage to major vessels, including the aorta and iliac arteries, due to pedicle screw fixation has been reported [2-4]. Most acute major vessel injuries cause sudden hemodynamic instability, which may inadvertently lead to a life-threatening condition [5, 6]. More rarely, major vessel injuries after pedicle screw misplacement may be found with delayed symptom presentation or as incidental findings in asymptomatic patients [7-10].

We experienced delayed onset common iliac artery perforation and infected pseudoaneurysm caused by a malpositioned pedicle screw after MISS. This case highlights that even posterior instrumentation surgery of the lower lumbar vertebra may cause major arterial injury in the late postoperative period, and a malpositioned cannulated screw could be a facilitating conduit for the spread of infection.

## Case Report
A 21-year-old female presented with a 1-week history of abrupt right-sided low back pain, lower abdominal pain, and fever. Eighteen months earlier, she had undergone MISS for adolescent idiopathic scoliosis using a cannulated pedicle screw system from T4 to L4. Abdomino-pelvic computed tomography (AP-CT) revealed an approximately 6.5 x 11 x 9 cm collection of pre- and paravertebral fluid with rim enhancement around the L3-5 level, containing more than two lobulated spaces filled with high-density contrast material (maximum 4.1 cm, Fig. 1a), predominantly at L4-5. In addition, a cystic lesion with a similar appearance was observed around the adjacent paraspinal muscle area, measuring about 3.3 x 5 x 10.5 cm from T12 to right S1 (Fig. 1b). Subsequent lumbar spine radiographs and CT revealed that the right L4 screw was positioned too far anteriorly with periscrew halo formation, adjacent vertebral body erosion, and detachment of the transverse process (Fig. 2).

Review of records and postoperative radiographs from the index hospital confirmed the surgical details. There were no specific findings to suggest infection in the perioperative period except for a long operation time (about 10 hours). Deformity was corrected with a MISS technique composed of free-hand cannulated polyaxial pedicle screw insertion through 2-4 skin incisions (each about 3 cm), precontoured rod insertion, thoracoplasty using the same skin incision, arthrodesis by facetal fusion, and correction using rod translation and rotation maneuvers [1]. Immediate postoperative radiography after the first operation showed right L4 pedicle screw malposition without a screw halo.

Clinically, paravertebral arterial erosion with pseudoaneurysm and retroperitoneal and paraspinal abscess were suspected, and surgical treatment was inevitable. Our operating strategy was an interdisciplinary single-session two-step approach with a vascular surgery team. First, retroperitoneal exploration through an anterior midline abdominal incision identified a 3 mm perforation at the posterior wall of the right common iliac artery (just below the aortic bifurcation) and a pseudoaneurysm behind the artery with surrounding abscess (Fig. 3). After drainage of the abscess, vascular repair of the right common iliac artery was performed with a bovine patch angioplasty. The perforation was treated with primary suture repair. Second, the right-sided instrumentation was removed and the paravertebral abscess was drained. Dark-black mucoid fluid with abundant granulation tissue was found around the right-sided implant at T12-L5. A fistula from the right L4 pedicle screw insertion site to the retroperitoneal abscess cavity was identified. Left-sided instrumentation was not removed because there was no evidence or sign of infection.

Postoperatively, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) was identified in tissue cultures from the abscess, and the patient was treated with intravenous ciprofloxacin according to susceptibility results. By postoperative day 7, the patient was ambulating independently and was discharged without pain or fever. At 6 months after surgery, she had no limitations in daily activities and no recurrence of low back pain, abdominal pain, or fever. Standing whole-spine radiographs at 6 months showed no progression of residual deformity despite removal of the right-sided instrumentation (Fig. 4).

## Discussion
The rate of pedicle screw misplacement in the lumbar spine varies from 5% to 41%, depending on underlying pathology, surgical technique, and site of screw fixation [12, 13]. The pedicles of the lumbar spine are larger than those of the thoracic spine; therefore, the risk of screw misplacement is lower in the lumbar region [14, 15]. Nevertheless, complications due to pedicle misplacement in the lumbar spine have been consistently reported.

Major vascular injury is one of the most fatal complications of malpositioned pedicle screws. The overall reported incidence of major vascular injury during spine surgery is less than 0.01% [16, 17]. Major vessel injury can remain undiscovered during follow-up and may become symptomatic in the late postoperative period. Patients may be asymptomatic for months or years after the initial operation. Delayed presentation is most commonly associated with pseudoaneurysm and arteriovenous fistula [18, 19]. Parker et al. reported the incidence of vascular encroachment (a pedicle screw touching or deforming the wall of a major vessel) as 0.22% among 6816 consecutive pedicle screws in the thoracic and lumbosacral spine [20]. Foxx et al. reported that 33 of 680 inserted screws were in contact with a major vessel on routine postoperative imaging after a mean follow-up of 44 months, including the aorta (4 cases), the iliac artery (7 cases), and the iliac veins (22 cases), but there were no symptoms or sequelae such as pseudoaneurysms, erosions, or deaths [2].

In this case, the patient developed sudden symptoms about 18 months after MISS. MISS for adolescent idiopathic scoliosis has potential benefits such as less blood loss, shorter hospital stay, earlier mobilization, and relatively less pain, but the minimally invasive approach usually requires a longer operative time and carries greater potential for radiologic complications, such as screw loosening or malposition [21-23]. The initial entry point of the misplaced screw was too lateral, and the screw tip was placed too far anteriorly; at that time, the screw tip may have contacted the common iliac artery. Screw halo formation, adjacent vertebral body erosion, and detachment of the transverse process indicate that the pedicle screw was not securely fixed to the vertebra. Poor bone-screw anchorage likely provided insufficient fixation strength and resulted in continuous, repetitive motion around the misplaced screw. Scoliosis is one of the most common spinal deformities to influence the location of the aorta relative to the vertebral body [24]. Vertebral rotation and angular alterations in scoliosis may increase the risk of major vessel injury [14]. In addition, because the misplaced screw was located at the lowest instrumented vertebra (LIV) of the construct, repetitive toggling motion associated with lumbar spine motion might have occurred. This toggling motion was presumed to cause chronic erosive damage to the adjacent iliac artery and contribute to delayed pseudoaneurysm formation.

In this case, the patient also had an infected pseudoaneurysm with abscess formation. Based on the fever episode, complicated infection was presumed to have begun 1 week before presentation. Paradoxically, the infectious condition facilitated early detection of the late vascular complication. The cause of sudden bacterial infection is presumed to be hematogenous seeding complicated by hematoma around the vessel. In addition, the cannulated pedicle screw used for MISS may have contributed to increased bleeding and/or spread of infection to the paravertebral muscle layer from the retroperitoneal space where the abscess first occurred.

Our repair strategy for the damaged common iliac artery was open resection of the pseudoaneurysm and angioplasty with a bovine patch. With effective antibiotics and the patient's relatively young age, early recovery was achieved without complications. However, direct repair of chronic perforation of that vessel seems dangerous, especially in the setting of complicated infection. Transient insertion of a vascular stent may also be considered as an alternative technique until infection is controlled.

In summary, we report a rare case of delayed vascular complication following MISS due to a malpositioned lumbar pedicle screw, and the cannulated screw could be a facilitating conduit for the spread of infection. Misplaced pedicle screws can cause potentially fatal complications in the late postoperative period, such as infected pseudoaneurysm. In addition, the pedicle screw inserted at the LIV must be placed in the correct position with sufficient holding strength to prevent delayed complications.
